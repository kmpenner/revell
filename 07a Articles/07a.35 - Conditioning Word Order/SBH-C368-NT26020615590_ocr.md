Introduction
1. This paper explores the factors which determine the order 
of the constituents in the standard two-part verbless clause and 
its variations in the books of Judges, Samuel, and Kings.1 
Little space is given to these clauses in grammars, and concen­
tration on verbal clauses has tended to obscure the fact that a 
number of features of structure are common to clauses of both 
types. The same structures act as constituents of both types of 
clause, apart, of course, from the verbal constituent which 
characterizes verbal clauses. For present purposes these con­
stituents can be classified simply as nominal or adverbial. Any 
noun or noun equivalent (including participles, adjectives, 
pronouns, or a phrase or clause functioning as the equivalent 
of a noun) can function as a nominal constituent. An adverb, 
or a preposition followed by a nominal, or by an infinitive and 
its modifiers, functions as an adverbial constituent.
2. Verbless clauses differ from verbal clauses (apart from the 
use of the verb) chiefly in the number of constituents used. 
Verbal clauses often have, beside the verb and its subject, 
several constituents which modify the verb, and are related to 
each other only through their relationship to the verb. Verbless

3. The constituents of a verbal clause are commonly said to

the primary focus of the reader’s attention. Thus in a narrative 
passage, there the story is treated as a sequence of events, the 
verb, which presents the event, typically stands first in each

The simple two-part verhless clause
5. The basic form of verbless clause in Hebrew is composed of 
two parts: subject (the topic of the clause) and predicate (the 
information supplied on that topic). These are semantic classi­
fications; neither subject nor predicate is consistently identified 
by any formal marker in any type of clause. Nevertheless, an 
adverbial, or a nominal headed by a participle or adjective, can 
confidently be identified as the predicate where the other 
constituent is an ordinary nominal. In such clauses, the stan­
dard (or most common) order is subject - predicate, which 
occurs in some sixty per cent of the cases where the predicate is 
adverbial, and in some eighty-five per cent of the cases where 
the predicate is headed by a participle or adjective. Conse­
quently the description below concentrates on the cases in 
which the predicate precedes the subject. Where both constit­
uents are nominal, but neither is headed by a participle or 
adjective (or if both are), the identification of the subject is 
more problematical, so a more general description is given. 
However it still appears that the subject precedes the predicate 
in a majority of cases. In verbal clauses, too, the subject 
typically precedes all other constituents but the verb in a verb­
initial clause, and is by far the most common initial constituent 
where the verb does not stand first. This, of course, reflects the 
fact that the subject is generally the most significant constituent 
in a verbal clause (save for the verb in consecutive narrative)
