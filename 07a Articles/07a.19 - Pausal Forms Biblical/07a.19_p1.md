I.INTRODUCTION

Pausal forms are generally (probably universally) held to be
conditioned by phonological features similar to the intonation
patterns which mark the ends of sentences in English. If this is
so, they ought to occur regularly, according to predictable
patterns. Their distribution is usually described in terms of the
accent system. It is true that as a general rule pausal forms occur
with silluq, atnab, and ‘oleh we-yored. In fact a considerable pro­
portion of pausal forms (over 25% in some books) occurs with
other accents (even conjunctive accents), and there is a small but
significant number of cases in which pausal forms are not used
with major pausal accents. The accent system cannot, then,
either be the conditioning factor, or reflect it.1

3.  THE ORIGIN OF PAUSAL FORMS:

EVIDENCE FROM PHONOLOGY

It is generally agreed that pausal forms reflect the vowel patterns
of a stage of the Hebrew Language somewhat earlier than that
represented by the received vocalisation. That is, they aroseat
some period prior to that of the masoretes.1 It is also true,
although this has not been noted, that the conditions which
created the difference between pausal and contextual forms had
ceased to operate by the time the received vocalisation was
established. In various forms showing patab or segol in the stress
syllable qames appears in pausal position where the orginal vowel
was “a” but, where it was “i”, the patah or segol is usually
retained in pause.2 If the two vowels had been the same when the
pausal forms were developed, they would have changed in the
same way. The fact that patab or segol deriving from original “i”
is not usually replaced by qames in pause, shows that the creation
of new pausal forms had ceased before the time when the reflex
of original “i” in these forms became identical to that of original
“a”, that is, before the period represented by any of the three
vocalisation systems.3 A similar conclusion can be drawn from
the fact that the few contextual forms which occur with atnah
and silluq, and the few pausal forms which occur with conjunctive
accents (no doubt also the larger number which occur with minor
disjunctives) also remain unchanged.4 It is clear, then, that the
pausal forms were already fixed in the reading tradition when its
received form was established by the masoretes. Consequently,
their position in the text, and so the system of text division which
they represent, must date from some earlier period.

4.  THE ORIGIN OF PAUSAL FORMS:

EVIDENCE FROM BIBLICAL LITERATURE

4.1.  The majority of pausal forms occur at the ends of clauses,
where a stop might be expected in any linguistic tradition. The
cases where pausal forms are used within clauses frequently do
not coincide with the conventions of other languages, so that if
another tradition does use stops in a similar way, the two are
likely to be related. Two cases are particularly striking. (1) A
pausal form is not usually used at the end of an introduction to
speech, which conflicts with the Greek and Latin convention.
1
(2) Lists are usually divided into groups of two or three items.
This contrasts with the accentuation of the Mishna, where a
pause is usually marked after every item in a list, agreeing
(whether through coincidence or influence) with the Greek
2
convention.

4.2.  In many cases the pausal forms within a clause appear to
be used for emphasis. They either draw attention to what
precedes, before some modifier is added, or they separate the
parts of a syntactic unit (such as the verb and its object) and so
cause suspense, and emphasise what follows (see 2.3). Inter­
jections may perform a similar function. The phrase n'm yhwb is
usually used at the end of a clause, but quite often occurs as an
interjection within a clause. In these cases it often occurs in
positions similar to those in which pausal forms are used within
clauses, and may plausibly be interpreted as having a similar
function.

6.  THE FUNCTION AND ORIGIN OF PAUSAL

FORMS: CONCLUSIONS

6. i. Pausal forms in the received text appear (with rare excep­
tions) in positions easily acceptable as the ends of sense units,
that is, at a division in the text which is either logical, or serves
some special purpose, such as emphasis. The units so terminated
consist of a clause, or a group of clauses or (less commonly), part
of a clause. The system of text division represented by these
pausal forms, that is the system of grouping phrases and clauses
into sense units, appears to follow conventions similar (in some
cases) to those which can be seen in the language of the prophets
(as it has come down to us), and possibly even in the punctuation
of the Mesha incription. It is scarcely probable that this similarity
in the logic behind the division of texts shown in these different
sources reflects any academic theory of punctuation, such as
arose from the Greek schools of rhetoric and textual criticism.
It seems virtually certain that in all these cases the basis of the
text division was the natural patterns of the language, the divi­
sion of speech into sense units delimited by intonation patterns.
Such intonation patterns, an integral part of the sound system

faithfully even in these apparently anomalous cases, and the fact
that the patterns which gave rise to them can still be recognised
constitutes an important testimony to the antiquity and reli­
ability of the masoretic tradition.

7. IMPLICATIONS FOR THE STUDY OF

BIBLICAL LITERATURE

7.1. The assumption that the pausal forms of the Bible (or
some of them) reflect a method of reading customary at some
time before that of the Masoretes has no doubt been made by
many others,1 and certainly the phenomenon of pause in Arabic
would support it. It is, however, of value to have such assump­
tions confirmed by a detailed investigation, and this investigation
has the merit of revealing the high probability of direct connec­
tion between the method of reading reflected by the pausal
forms and the patterns of speech or oratory of the biblical period.
This does-mot, of course, imply that the method of reading

7.2.  The significance of the conclusion that the pausal forms
reflect an early stage of the reading tradition is not limited to its
interest for the history of that tradition. These forms reflect the
way in which the text was commonly understood at some period
prior to that of the Masoretes, and can therefore provide inform­
ation significant for the history of the development of literary
units, from the forms identified by scholarly analysis as original,
to those of the received text.2

7.3.  The vast majority of the verses in the Book of Proverbs
are composed of two stichs, each ending (if the word can form
one) in a pausal form. In three anomalous cases (compared to 387
with pausal forms), the first stich ends in a contextual form
(xxiv. 4, 30, xxx. 21). In longer verses, however, contextual forms
occur much more commonly at the end of stichs (xxiii. 5,
xxx. 9, 14, 15, 16, 17 compared to 34 with pausal forms). This
suggests that these longer verses were also read as composed of
two stichs, as suggested by the accents, and not as three or four,
as suggested by the wording.3 In the Psalms, however, pausal
forms not uncommonly reflect the reading of verses as three
stichs (e.g. 1. 7, Ivi. 9, cxvi. 16, cxxxviii. 2).

7.4.  Pausal forms in poetical passages in the prophets simi­
larly sometimes reflect a reading of these passages in individual
stichs, as suggested by the wording, as in Isa. Ixv. 13. However,
verses 8, 9, 12, 15 and 19 of this chapter show stichs which
do not end in pausal forms. Presumably, then, the pausal forms
reflect a reading tradition which recognised a variety of literary
forms in this section, not a poem composed throughout in
stichs of more or less equal length.
