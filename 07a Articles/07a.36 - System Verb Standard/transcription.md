This article surveys the system of the verb in the Former Prophets starting
from the premises (advanced by others) that there are two indicative “tenses”,
each represented by a “simple” and a “consecutive” form in complementary
distribution, and one modal system, represented by the imperative, jussive
and cohortative forms, also in complementary distribution. The main thesis
is that the indicative and modal systems are fully independent because (!)
imperfect forms with modal value are distinguished from indicatives by their
position in the clause, (2) a perfect form with waw “consecutive” following
a modal has a value different from that of a modal in the same position.
The article begins with a survey of the indicative system intended to set this
thesis in its proper context by showing that (1) the value of any verb form
depends heavily on its syntactic/semantic context, (2) despite the distinct val­
ues of the “tenses”, in many contexts either may be used to represent the
same occurrence — the choice is determined by the viewpoint of the author,
or by the particular implications he intends to convey. The article concludes
with a survey of the way this system developed to the end of the Biblical
period.

scribed as using a set of indicative forms representing two categories
— here called QTL and YQTL — and a set of modal forms representing
one category. The various verb forms are used in this system as follows:

The Use of Indicatives

2.1  The meaning carried by the opposition of the two categories of
the indicative, QTL and YQTL, has been much debated. It seems to
me that it is most easily presented as one of time reference: QTL ‘past’
versus YQTL ‘present/future’. While the ease of such presentation is no
doubt partly due to the fact that English uses a tense system, there is
no real reason to suppose that Hebrew does not.

(a)  The fact that forms typically representing past time may repre­
sent future, and vice versa, under particular conditions, does not show
that the basic system is not one of tense (pace Driver, 1892, #1). The
same phenomenon occurs in English, as in the examples in Lyons
(1986), p. 239.

(b)  The criterion by which the value of a form must be judged is
not its historical origin, but its use within the corpus. No matter what
the origin of the waw consecutive verb forms, those which occur in the
corpus must be regarded as syntactically conditioned variants of the per­
fect and imperfect forms. This removes any obstacle to considering the
categories they represent as tense categories, as argued in Blau, 1970.
(c)  The Hebrew of post-biblical times certainly used a tense system,
and the same is widely assumed for the ancestor of the biblical language,
as vestigially represented by the ‘preterite’ (or waw consecutive) use of
the imperfect form. It seems likely, a priori, that the system of the in­
tervening period would also have been one of tense. The QTL:YQTL

The Identification of Modal Forms

13.  The modal system is represented by imperatives, used in affirm-
ative clauses with second person subject, and by the forms of the im-
perfect known as “jussive” and “cohortative” used where the clause is
negative, or where the subject is first or third person. Distinct modal
imperfect forms occur only where the subject is first person, 2m.s., or
3m./f.s. Any original difference between the modal and the indicative
forms of the imperfect has been lost where the subject is 2f.s., 2.pl, or
3.pl. (On forms with suffixes, see #14.3.) First person modal forms show
the affix ה—; the corresponding imperfect forms do not. Forms from
roots IIIA show the “long” imperfect form (identical with the indicative
imperfect form) where a first person modal is expected. In 2m.s. and
3m./f.s. forms, where the imperfect form shows i or u in a final closed
syllable, the corresponding modal form shows e or 0 respectively. Where
the final syllable of the imperfect form is open, modal imperfect forms
from roots IIIA omit the final vowel. The same form from roots IIP
is used in all situations. These forms from roots IIP, first person forms
from roots IIIA, and all other forms which show no modal/indicative
distinction, are referred to here as “neutral” forms. First person modal
imperfect forms are called “affixed”. Second and third person forms
are called “short” in contrast to the “long” indicative forms. A form is
said to have “modal value” if used in a position in which the standard
system permits the use of modal forms but not indicative forms, as after
the negative  .לא12

19.5  The waw consecutive perfect is translated as an imperative in ac-
cordance with English usage in this case, as in the others, but the use
of this form shows that the verb is a YQTL indicative, with connotations
different from those carried by an imperative or other rqodal form. A
modal form is used to present a forceful command, whether its force
reflects the fact that, although not first in a series of commands, it is
of major significance, or reflects the urgency of the situation, or the
social or moral status of the speaker relative to the person addressed.
The waw consecutive perfect (a YQTL indicative form) is used in the
same context to present a command which is less forceful, whether the
(relative) lack of force reflects the fact that the action to be taken is a
later consequence of action already forcefully commanded, or that it
is not urgent, or that there is no wish to mark any difference of social
or moral status between the speaker and the person addressed. It must
be admitted (with Jouon, 1947, 301) that the different values of the waw
consecutive perfect and imperative following an imperative are not al-
ways significant in the contexts in which they are used. Similarly, in En-
glish, the four possible variations of ‘I sang/was singing as I walked/was
walking’ could be used in the same context with no significant difference
of connotation. That does not mean, however, that the two past forms
have the same meaning. So also the simple equation “after an imper-
ative, waw consecutive perfect = imperative” is an oversimplification;
it hides the function of the waw consecutive perfect as presenting a
clause-initial indicative, with its particular connotations. Just as with
QTL and YQTL forms, which are typically used in different contexts
as having distinct values, and which present different connotations in
those contexts in which either can be used, so with the two forms with
present/future value: modal and YQTL indicative. In a context estab-
lished as one in which directions are given by a preceding imperative
(or otherwise as 2 Sam. 14:10, 1 Kgs. 13:31, quoted in #20.2), either
an indicative or an imperative form can be used, and the connotations
they present are different.

The system of the verb in standard biblical prose

25.  A verb can be marked as modal in the corpus by its form (imper­
ative, or short or affixed imperfect), by its position (initial in its clause)
or by co-occurrence (with following KJ or preceding 1?K). Any one of these
marks will serve to identify it, but it may have two, or even three of
them. A YQTL indicative is marked by a waw consecutive perfect form
in clause-initial position, or by an imperfect form (not in co-occurrence
with KJ or ,7K) within a clause.

It is possible that the different modal forms in Hebrew are ves­
25.1
tiges of more complete “imperative”, and “short”, and “affixed” imper­
fect paradigms, each of which had a distinct function. In standard He­
brew prose, however, they form a single set of modal forms, and in
many cases the same “neutral” imperfect form is used for both modal
and indicative functions. Due to the relation between the significance
of a constituent and its position in the clause (see #17), verbs with modal
value typically stood in clause-initial position. Consequently indicative
imperfect forms came to be restricted to position within the clause, so
that the value of a neutral imperfect was marked as modal if it began
the clause, as indicative if it did not (unless it was marked as modal by
a particle). The waw consecutive perfect (the origin of which is not
known) came to be used where a YQTL indicative verb was required
in clause-initial position, and so as a positional variant of the indicative
imperfect. While this pattern continued to govern the positions in which
the modal and indicative forms of the imperfect were used, the differ­
ence in their values was lost, so that the imperative eventually became
the only distinct modal form, as in Mishanic Hebrew. This development
may be foreshadowed in the partial blurring of the distinction between
the values of modal and indicative forms in the corpus. It was possibly
influenced, in its later stages, by the vernacular language. The devel­
opment resulted in the abandonment of the features which had distin­
guished modal imperfects from YQTL indicatives, including the use of
the waw consecutive perfect, because it took away their function. This

26.  A QTL indicative is represented in the corpus by a perfect form
within a clause, or at the beginning of a clause where the conjunction
waw is not used. Where the conjunction is used, the QTL indicative is
typically represented by the waw consecutive imperfect. This form de­

prose, to initial position in a clause which was part of a sequence, or
was presented as such, and so began with a conjunction. Here it acts
as a positional variant of the perfect, and functions to indicate the struc­
ture of narrative passages. The perfect form with a prefixed conjunction
represents QTL in a past context in a number of case^ in which the
action is considered as identical with, or as a facet of, that of the pre­
ceding clause. This is distinct from the waw consecutive use of the per­
fect (which is restricted to a present/future context) and is common en­
ough in the corpus that it can be regarded as a feature of standard us­
age. It is possible that, in an earlier phase, the waw consecutive imperfect
was the only representative of QTL used in clause-initial position with
a prefixed conjunction, and that this use of the perfect was the first
symptom of the change which eventually led to the complete replace­
ment of the waw consecutive imperfect by the perfect. However this may
be, the waw consecutive imperfect appears to have been used according
to the standard pattern throughout the biblical period and also in the
literary language of Qumran. In iQIsaa its replacement by the perfect
is rare in comparison to the number of cases in which the waw consec­
utive perfect is replaced by the imperfect (see Kutscher, 1974:354—5,

27.  The use of verbs in the corpus is, then, a complex, but generally
consistent system representing a more or less stable stage of usage char­
acteristic of the prose narrative of a particular period. Since it is a stage
in continuing development, it is not surprising that the usage is not en­
tirely consistent. Possibly some of the unusual features found in it are
vestiges of the usage of earlier stages, restricted, like English subjunc­
tives, to particular contexts. Others may well be early examples of usage
which became standard in the succeeding stage. No doubt some of the
anomalies it shows arose from errors in transmission. The standard pat­
tern of the usage is, however, clear. The most interesting feature
brought out by this survey is the grammatical function of the waw con­
secutive perfect, which marks a clause initial verb as a YQTL indicative.
