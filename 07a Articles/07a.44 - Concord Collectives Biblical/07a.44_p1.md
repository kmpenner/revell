1. Introduction.

1.1  For the purposes of this study, collectives are defined as nouns
which are morphologically singular, but which may have singular or
plural coreferents.  A coreferent of a collective is commonly described
as showing “grammatical” concord when singular, “notional” concord
(constructio ad sensum) when plural.  If this is taken as indicating
that concord is determined either by linguistic convention (a noun which
is singular in form has singular coreferents) or by the speaker’s perception
of reality (the plural is used to make reference to a collection of individ­
uals), a difficulty appears. A compound subject should have plural
coreferents both according to grammatical convention, and as a reflection
of reality, yet a compound subject of which the first component designates

1.2  There is regularity in the choice of number in the coreferents of
collectives in one limited area. An attributive which presents a quality
of the group designated by the collective (and not of the individuals
which form it) is singular.  As described, the basis of this is semantic,
so the value of the term “grammatical” for such concord is questionable.
Equally, one would expect that a speaker would be led to think of a
group performing particular actions as a number of individuals rather
than as a unit, and so to the “notional” use of a plural verb where the
subject was a collective. It is true that an active verb with a collective
subject is often plural, but there is no group of verbs which are charac­
teristically plural when the subject is collective. This might be held to
show that the “notional” plural is a free variant of the “grammatical”
singular, were it not that the use of the latter is, in one area at least,
clearly conditioned.

1.3  This paper is an attempt to determine what the terms “grammatical”
and “notional” represent when used in the way indicated. It is based on
the books of Judges, Samuel, and Kings, but is limited to collectives
which denote humans. This corpus provides some 400 cases in which a
collective is subject of a verb, or is modified by an attributive, and so is
marked for number, a sample large enough to provide a satisfactory
basis for conclusions. The language of these books is homogeneous,
and can reasonably be treated synchronically.  The method of study is

3.2  The correlation between high transitivity and the treatment of a
collective as plural can be investigated more precisely in terms of the
parameters of transitivity set up in P. J. Hopper and S. A. Thompson.
12
Such an investigation is, however, confronted with several problems.
The limits of our knowledge of Biblical Hebrew would reduce treatment
of parameters like “aspect” and “punctuality” largely to guesswork. In
addition, the boundaries of semantic categories are typically ill-defined.
Some examples are easy to assign to opposing categories, but for a
considerable proportion, assignment can only be arbitrary, as would be
the case if all humans were to be categorized as “tall” or “short.”
Moreover, a variety of factors typically condition the choice between
contrasting items which carry a semantic load, just as a number of
factors, such as sex and diet, would affect human stature. It is easy
enough to select examples to show that a particular factor is significant
in some cases, but the conclusion that it is significant in all cases must
be derived by argument. It cannot be demonstrated, because no one
factor is always dominant.

9. Conclusion.

38

9.1  This survey shows that, generally speaking, the choice of number
in coreferents of the collectives in the corpus follows regular patterns.
Where the collective is subject of a clause, the head of the predicate is
generally singular, as are other coreferents within the clause, and in
clauses subordinate to it. More distant coreferents, outside the syntactic
unit formed by the clause, are generally plural.  Where a collective
does not act as subject, coreferents are also usually plural; the singular
is common only within the same constituent as the collective. A nominal
attributive to a collective is generally singular, but this also has no
effect on other relationships within the clause. Where singular is expected,
the use of the plural is mainly determined by the prominence of the
collective or its clause in the narrative. Other factors, particularly high
transitivity, and the fact that the collective is actor, are also conducive
to the use of the plural. Certain passages, characterized above as “histor­
ical synopsis” or “prophetic register,” use an unusually high proportion
of singular cjoreferents. Outside these passages, the proportion of exam­
ples which must be described as anomalies, in that they clearly conflict
with the common pattern, is negligible.

9.6  The language of Qumran represents a late stage in the development
of the tendency to treat collectives as plural. The usage of the corpus,
where collectives are treated as singular in their primary syntactic rela­

in yet earlier stages, the singular was even more frequently used. It is
possible that successive generations of the ancients viewed the increasing
use of the plural as progressive abandonment of “classical” grammar
for “vulgar” usage, as was the tendency among more recent grammarians.

capacity. In other words it is an example of one of the ways in which
the expressive capabilities of languages are developed.
