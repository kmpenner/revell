Speaking of Speaking: Marking Direct Discourse in the He­
brew Bible. By Samuel A. Meier. Supplements to Vetus
Testamentum, vol. 46. Leiden: E. J. Brill, 1992. Pp. xvi +
383.

This book is concerned with the identification of direct dis­
course (DD) in Biblical Hebrew and with the features which
introduce or otherwise mark it. It presents a comprehensive de­
scription of these features, supporting the detailed study of the
Hebrew by the use of linguistic resources ranging from the
Amama letters to the New Testament, and others from beyond
the immediate area. The initial section provides an introduction
to the problems involved in the study of the topic and to the
assumptions which govern the work. The following sections deal
with the use of JMR, ‘say’, the use of other verbs to introduce DD,
DD in the Prophets, and the marking of divine speech. There is
a brief concluding summary, a set of appendices providing in­
formation on some details, a list of works cited, and an index of
Biblical passages. Both text and index show a high standard of
accuracy. This is a work of meticulous scholarship, providing a
great deal of information on its topic and on matters associated
with it. It provides a valuable reference tool of a sort much
needed for the study of the Biblical language.

Any student of Biblical Hebrew may choose to define it as
the language of the Bible as we know it (MT), or as the He­
brew language of the Biblical period, for which there is little
direct evidence. The problem with the choice of MT is that
other witnesses may vary, suggesting change since the Biblical
period. Meier rejects the choice on this basis, arguing (p. 15)
that “alternation between wyJmr and lJmr, for example, cannot
be assumed to be meaningful if alternate textual traditions indi­
cate that it is not quite clear which form, if either, was origi­
nal.” The chart on p. 27 does show “the chaotic nature of the
evidence” on a question of this sort if the Greek translation (G)
and MT are regarded as equally authoritative, but it can also be
read as showing that G either agrees with MT or uses the parti­
ciple (Xcycov). The use of this participle is evidently a feature
of Semitized Greek (Meier shows on pp. 136-37 that its use
differs from that of lJmr, the Hebrew form to which it often
corresponds), so G here may well reflect the influence of the
spoken language. Unless it can be shown that this is not the
case, the chart does not support Meier’s view.

Again, the author is reluctant to accept the repetition of a
verb of speaking within a speech as a “legitimate” feature of
Hebrew narrative (p. 80) in the majority of cases. On p. 80, he
argues that “many occurrences [of repeated verbs of speaking]
mask the activity of redactors” and, quoting Gen. 16:9-11 as an
example, suggests that the view is confirmed by the “awkward­
ness and density” of the usage. But is this really confirmation or
just an example of the differing usage of English and Hebrew?
Meier (who rejects appeal to aesthetic judgment on p. 16) does
not explain why a redactor should not merely add the required
words, rather than indulge in such awkwardness. Why, in any

case, are Biblical redactors not representative of Biblical He­
brew? In the majority of cases repetition of a verb of speaking
within a speech coincides with a shift in the speaker’s focus
from one topic (or one aspect of a topic) to another, as in Gen.
16:9-11. Meier accepts such repetition as “legitimate” where it
coincides with a shift in the speaker’s focus from one addressee
to another, as in Gen. 43:29 (p. 74), a situation not widely
different. Under views of the sort espoused by the author, the
question of whether an unexpected feature occurs in particular
situations, and so is likely to have a particular function, tends to
be obscured by the question of whether or not it is a feature of
“Biblical Hebrew.” Scholarly expectations must play a large
part in answering this second question, but they should be in­
formed by the answer to the first. Without this, they are inevi­
tably influenced by the usage of the non-Semitic, classical, and
spoken languages familiar to most scholars. This does not pro­
vide the firm basis for linguistic study for which Meier calls
(P- 15).

By using witnesses to the Hebrew text other than MT,
Meier intends to “determine the degree of confidence to be
placed in the stability of certain linguistic forms” (p. 17), not
to reconstruct the text. The effect on his evaluation of the He­
brew of MT is not marked. His presentation of the evidence is
not affected at all. Any who disagree with his conclusions will
find all the information needed to explore other possibilities.
Much more than a simple description of usage is provided.
Meier can point to usage which characterizes particular pas­
sages and so helps to delineate units of text. He is able to
show diachronic change, both in the use of individual roots
and in the general attitude to the marking of divine speech. He
provides extensive and knowledgeable discussion of the origin
and use of the so-called “messenger formula” and of the word
nJm, both widely used to mark divine speech. This book will
be of value «to many more than those concerned only with the
Hebrew language. Meier can be justly proud both of the con­
ception and of its execution.

under review here is intended to assist students in the mastery
of the basic elements of the language. The authors predict the
Handbook's usefulness for two groups of students in particular.
The first group would be those who, while using the Grammar
in a classroom setting, need some help in mastering the lesson
and doing the exercises. The second group would consist of
those who find it necessary to study Hebrew on their own,
either for the first time or for review.

Each of the thirty lessons corresponds to the lesson of the
same number in the Grammar, and usually contains five sections.
“Take Special Note” calls attention to particularly important or
difficult aspects of the lesson. “Review the Lesson” reviews the
basics. “Check Your Exercises” supplies an answer key to the
exercises at the end of a Grammar lesson, so students get imme­
diate feedback. Beginning with Lesson Thirteen of the Hand­
book, translations are no longer provided, since these can be
checked in standard translations. Instead, aspects of the Hebrew
text are highlighted, with direction to appropriate parts of the
Grammar for review. “You Should Know” reminds students of
the essentials of the lesson just listed. “Did You Get It?” offers
a few additional drills on material. Four “Practical Helps” are in­
cluded, to give instruction in areas of particular difficulty (“Be­
ginning Syllable Division,” “Root-Finding in Nouns,” “Verb
Identification,” and “Verb Description”).

I use Seow’s Grammar for a Biblical Hebrew course taught
at a Christian seminary—the audience Professor Seow had in
mind for his grammar. My sense is that students will welcome
the Handbook', although it covers aspects of the Grammar that
should be treated adequately in class, most students are more
than happy to have review guides (not to mention answer
keys). Review guides are particularly welcome when individ­
ual chapters of the main text are too long or too dense, an oc­
casional fault of Seow’s Grammar (lesson two, for example). I
doubt that the Handbook, with its requisite brevity, will prove
helpful to students studying Biblical Hebrew on their own,
particularly since Seow never intended his Grammar to serve
this audience (Seow, Grammar, viii). A text with this audi­
ence in mind would have to contain many more aids to pro­
nunciation, as well as help in the mechanics of writing.

Some more detailed comments on the handbook follow.
P. 4.: Statements such as “do not let this terminology con­
fuse you!” are unhelpful and, as a general rule, should not be
made (the same applies to a piece of advice given on p. 92:
“don’t try to memorize more than you have to.”

P. 5: The heading “long/short” (hireq, qibbus) of chart 1,

“Simple Vowels,” is not clear.

Pp. 9-12: Section two of this “Practical Help” needs more
examples. The statement on p. 9, “it pays to use Hebrew rather
than transliteration!” underscores one of the strengths of Seow’s
Grammar. I have used grammars that offered students the crutch
of transliteration for much too long; Seow’s Grammar and this
Handbook use a bare minimum.

P. 13: Under 2.c.ii, the holem of kohenim is cited as an ex-
ample of a vowel that cannot be reduced. This needs further
explanation, since holem does indeed reduce, elsewhere.

P. 17: לטקנמ to לטקמ is given as an example of assimila-
tion of nun. When possible, examples should be authentic and
pronounceable.

P. 28: The Handbook corrects a weakness in the Grammar,
Seow uses modem terminology for the verbal stems (“D” in-
stead of piel). Most seminary students still use BDB or some
other dictionary with traditional names for verbal patterns.
Seow does give a list of correspondences on p. 45 of his gram-
mar, but the Handbook reminds students of the correspon-
dences both here in lesson seven and when they encounter the
different verbal stems.

P. 37: There is a mistake in the chart on genitival relation-

ship. The construct of dabar is debar, not debar.

P. 48: There is a mistake in the chart, under 111־ו/י. The

2f.s. form of תמ should have a shewa under the taw.

P. 67: Again, beginning students should not be given forms

they cannot pronounce: wyqtl should be wayyiqtol.

P. 70: 1 כס  with the imperfect is not a negation of the imper-
ative; the Handbook seems to make Jal plus the imperfect and
1 כס  plus the imperfect interchangeable.

Egypt, Canaan, and Israel in Ancient Times. By Donald B.
Redford. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1992.
Pp. xxiii + 488. $39.95.

Donald Redford is one of the few contemporary scholars who
has the background to synthesize the history and culture of the
ancient world, from the Aegean to Mesopotamia, from neolithic
times to the fall of Jerusalem in the sixth-century b.c. This work
is a miniature Cambridge Ancient History, brought up to date
and filtered through one historian’s experience. Such a book
needs to be produced from time to time, a synthesis of history,
archaeology, linguistics, socio-economic and cultural history; it
wrestles with that most elusive problem of all: what is and what
is not a genuine reflection of intercultural borrowing or influ­
ence? Dealing with that question is Redford’s main goal, all else
being his panoramic description of ancient society within which
cultures variously affected each other.
