Raija Sollamo’s volume will be of interest to textual critics of the
Hebrew Bible and researchers of Septuagint translation technique (who,
incidentally, should be members of the same tribe). They may well wish,
however, that she had broadened the scope of her investigation to include
more of the Bible and of relevant non-canonical sources than she did so as
to furnish the basis for a more comprehensive analysis. On the whole, her
analysis and conclusions appear to be sound. 'There are, however, some
shortcomings in her work. I will pass over the errors and omissions of
lesser import to deal with more substantive issues.

First, Sollamo occasionally makes mistakes in the recording of data. In
Table 1 on page 81 (“The renderings of possessive suffixes in cases of two
coordinate items”), two in particular should be noted. (1) Sollamo indicates
that there are 47 instances of this phenomenon in Genesis despite the fact
that the sum of the subtotals in the table is 46. This is because only two of
the three “special cases” that she discusses in chapter two (pp. 25-26) are
accounted for in the table. (2) Similarly, although there are a total of 50
cases identified for Exodus in chapter three, the sum of the subtotals in
Table 1 is only 48. Here Sollamo overlooks the two instances in which the
Greek possessive pronoun is not repeated but occurs just once before the
first item in a chain of coordinate nouns (see pp. 32-33). Furthermore, in
the appendix, where she discusses the results of her nomen rectum study,
she makes the following puzzling admission: “I must apologize for my fig-
ures. ,They are not exact, a few cases may have escaped my notice...” (p.
97). One wonders why she has not checked her work so as to eliminate the
need for this caveat.

Second, Sollamo could have organized her material better. For example,
there is significant repetition in her discussion of Exod 18:6 on pages 36
and 94 and of Num 31:9 on pages 60 and 94 (see also pp. 41-42 and 93
[Exod 7:19], pages 48 and 91 [Lev 25:44], pages 53 and 92 [Num 30:5],
pages 56 and 92 [Num 14:22], and pages 62-63 and 92 [Num 4:9]). Yet

Foundation for Biblical Research in the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.
Pp. 270. Jerusalem: Magnes Press, 1994. Cloth, $17.00.

A short introduction to the book explains Kogut’s intentions and
methods. It also gives a concise exposition of the basic principles of the use
of the accent signs to demarcate the subdivisions of a verse and to indicate
their relationships to each other. This allows the use of a simple and un­
ambiguous terminology, facilitating clear presentation of the value of
accentuation in the subsequent discussion of Biblical passages.

This discussion (the remainder of the book) is divided into two sections,
dealing respectively with examples of exegesis which directly refer to the
accentuation of a biblical passage under consideration and with examples in
which this is not the case. The examples chosen either present an interpre­
tation which differs from that suggested by the accentuation (which then
typically represents the peshat, the “simple, natural, or plain” meaning of
the passage), or they present an interpretation which agrees with the accen­
tuation in differing from the peshat. The examples are selected from
sources traditionally recognized as authorities: the rabbis of the Mishnah,
the Midrash, and the Taimuds, Sa'adia Gaon, Rashi, Rashbam, Abraham
ibn Ezra, David Qimhi, Hezekiah b. Manoah, Ramban, and Obadiah
Sfomo. Kogut reasonably argues that the question of whether the rabbinic
tradition recognized the divisions within the verses marked by the ma-

The accents do indeed mark stress position, as described on pages 14-
15, but this may be unstable, especially in hollow root verb forms ending
in vowels and in waw consecutive perfect forms. Rashi regarded stress
position in forms like האב as clearly distinguishing perfect from participle
(see his comments on Gen 15:17; 29:6; and 46:26); since its inclusion in
Qimhi’s Mikhlol, this has been the standard view. However, diagnosis based
on the position of the masoretic accent may not always represent the inten־

been stated on page 14. This would have simplified his discussion of

examples of such forms. Neither Onqelos nor the Septuagint makes the
same distinction as Rashi in Gen 46:26. Presumably, the fixing of either the
stress position or the interpretation of it was a later development.

again, their diagnostic value should not be overestimated. He argues (Ex.
36, p. 66) that קר with the adversative value “but” has a disjunctive accent;
with the exclusive value “only” it has a conjunctive. This is generally true,
but some cases are ambiguous, and in others the rule does not hold (as in
Gen 50:8; Judg 19:20; 1 Kgs 3:2; 11:13; 14:8; and 15:14). In fact, the ac-
cents often do not follow basic syntax. In Gen 22:10, the atnah divides the
second clause, resulting in a much more expressive presentation of the
phrase “to slaughter his son,” which forms the second division, than would
separation of the two clauses. 1 Sam 3:3 (Ex. 50, p. 81) shows a similar
division, no doubt for different reasons, as Kogut makes clear. The point is

that the accentuation in both cases is intended to suggest an alignment of the
ideas expressed that is somewhat different from the simple syntactical re-
lationships of the words which represent them. Division within the verse
(like the division of the text into verses) has a semantic rather than a syn-
tactic basis, although the two, of course, often coincide.

The term peshat (exegesis consistent with the standard conventions of
the language) as opposed to derash (exegesis which ignores them, as any
piteacher may do for homiletic purposes) is useful as a general categoriza-
tion. However, where the motivation of exegesis is in question, as in this
book, more precise terminology is desirable. Conventions may change. In
Deut 26:5, all early interpretation takes דבא as a verb, but the Septuagint
and later Jewish tradition differ in their identification of its subject. This
suggests (as do some other factors) that, in early times, a participle in a
situation of this sort was routinely treated as a verb if this was not inconsis-
tent with the morphology (as I argued in Sefarad 48 [1988] 197-205). If
so, the treatment of דכא in Targum Onqelos (Ex. 35, p. 65) is consistent
with the understanding of the language at the time. The treatment of this
word as an attributive (Ex. 146, 155, and 190, pp. 192, 206, and 248)
represents a change in view in response to other language-based arguments.
Again, any exegete brings to his work certain assumptions about the text
and may feel that, in some cases, these naturally override linguistic consid-
erations. Qimhi’s discussion of ינדא with qames in Judg 6:15 shows that his
expectations about the logic and coherence of the narrative led him to con-
elude that the form does not refer to God, in opposition (as he notes) to
Targum Jonathan, despite the difficulty of explaining the use of that form
with other references. It seems probable that the interpretations of Exod
14:30 offered (in opposition to the accentuation) by Rashbam and ibn Ezra
(Ex. 147 and 156, pp. 194, and 207-9) are similarly based on their expec-
tations of narrative coherence. The accentuation represents a perfectly nat-
ural way to divide the clause in question, called into question only because
it was felt that Egyptian corpses would not have been “on the edge of the
sea.” To identify one of the interpretations as the peshat in such cases is a
value judgment which tends to obscure the motivation of the exegete.

Emphasis on the typical at the expense of the unusual is necessary in
introductory works, the category into which this book really falls. A
somewhat less rigid conception of the functions of the accents and the
nature of peshat is desirable, at least in the general discussion in the intro-
duction, but the book is of great value as it is. It provides an excellent
survey of traditional Jewish interpretation, including that represented by

INTERTEXTUALITY AND THE READING OF MIDRASH. By
Daniel Boyarin. Pp. xii + 161. Bloomington, IN: Indiana University Press,
1994. Paper, $9.95.

Classic rabbinic midrash presupposes a view of Scripture in which ev­
ery scriptural element is of moral, legal, or theological significance.
Understanding this essential stance of midrash helps define its purpose and
delimit the range of interpretation it permits, but it does not at all illumi­
nate the methods that the fashioners of midrashic discourse employed in
order to provide such interpretations. Indeed, certain midrashic interpreta­
tions seem to collapse the intuitive distinction between the peshat, the “plain
sense” of Scripture, and its midrashic interpretation. That being the case, it
should be clear that midrash is not necessarily distinguished from other
methods of reading texts by its relation to “plain sense” interpretation on
the one hand, nor by its goal of providing religiously meaningful scriptural
interpretation on the other, since religiously significant readings can be
provided by other means.

Thus, after reviewing certain medieval and modem approaches to
midrash and rightly pointing out their deficiencies, Daniel Boyarin pro­
poses to interpret certain parts of the Mekilta on Exodus 13-18 as
interpretation—plain but not simple.

I have read this text [i.e., the Mekilta] with the fore-understanding that midrashic
discourse is interpretation and then asked, “What in the Bible’s text might have
motivated this gloss on this verse? Can I explain this text in such a way that this
gloss makes sense as an interpretation of the verse?” The key to this inquiry is
literary theory and its questions: What does it mean to interpret a text? Is there
stable meaning in texts? If so, how is it discovered and disclosed? If not, then
what is there? (p. ix)

In the course of confronting these questions, Boyarin investigates a number
of different textual problems which in his view gave rise to various

midrashic interpretations now included in the Mekilta, a relatively early
rabbinic collection which he dates to the first through the third centuries.
Most are the result of those features of Scripture which result in a “gapped
text”: ambiguities, contradictions, redundancies, and the like. Indeed, in a
particularly illuminating chapter, he views the midrashic interpretation of
Exodus 16 and Numbers 30—the incident of the manna and quail—as a re­
sponse to the same problems which gave rise to nineteenth century source
critical approaches.

Early on, the author suggests that one definition of midrash would be

radical intertextual reading of the canon, in which potentially every part refers to
and is interpretable by every other part. The*Torah, owing to its own intertex-
tuality, is a severely gapped text, and the gaps are there to be filled by strong
readers, which in this case does not mean readers fighting for originality, but
readers fighting to find what they must in the holy text. Their own intertext—
that is, the cultural codes which enable them to make meaning and find mean­
ing, constrain the rabbis to fill in the gaps of the Torah’s discourse...in accor- x
dance with certain ideological structures, (p. 17)

Thus, what readers “must find” are readings which correspond to their
own “cultural codes” and “ideological structures.” But, he cautions,
midrash is not merely “a reflex of that ideology but a dialogue with the
biblical text conditioned and allowed by that ideology—and as such is no
different from any other interpretation” (p. 17). This is because, according
to current literary theory, all texts are intertexts. No texts are composed in
a cultural vacuum, and all contain echoes which resonate from intertexts
which have influenced the author.

To return to the questions with which Boyarin opens, the stability of
midrashic readings is assured, or rather, its instability is limited, by the
number of combinations of intertexts available within and without
Scripture. While at times the midrashic method may seem akin to free as­
sociation, the number of combinations, while potentially large, is not
infinite. Moreover, the requirement that the midrash solve a textual prob­
lem or problems limits the range of interpretation still more. The more
faithful the interpretation is to the problems of the text and its intertexts,
the more stable the resultant interpretation will be.

minating though not exhaustive. Nevertheless, the book contains superb
readings of midrashic texts, which, quite apart from the theory, are worth
