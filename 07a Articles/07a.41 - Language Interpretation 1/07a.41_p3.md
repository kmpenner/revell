The Frank Talmage Memorial Volume I

Language and Interpretation
in 1 Kings 20

E. J. Revell

This article offers an interpretation of 1 Kings chapter 20 based on an analysis of
the linguistic and structural features of the text.1 This interpretation is suggested
for consideration rather than fully argued. The primary purpose in presenting it is
to demonstrate the need for more careful consideration of linguistic usage. It is
argued that, when an unusual feature is used, it is necessary to ask not only what
it means of itself, but why a more common equivalent was not used. The answer
to the latter question must form part of the communication that the passage was
intended to make, or must enhance it in some way. It must reflect the “narrator’s
intention.”2

Commentators generally present their assumptions on what is here called
“narrator’s intention” (e.g., classification of genre, etc.) in terms not of the
passage as it stands, but of the sources from which it is believed to have been
composed. This attitude is questionable; if the sources themselves are not
available, we cannot know the extent to which they have been adapted to fit their
new context. If a passage is considered a literary unity - the work of a narrator
who intended it to convey some particular information - the wording must be
considered the narrator’s own (barring errors in transmission). If traces of
different sources are visible, it is to be assumed either that they were retained
with a purpose, or at least that they did not interfere with the narrator’s purpose.
It is reasonable to suggest that the compilers of a narrative might overlook
contradictions resulting from conflation of sources, but the probability of this
must be argued for any particular case to which it is applied.3 A passage should
not be treated a priori as a collection of independent sources. Unless it can be
shown that the sources have not been integrated into a unity, the narrator’s
intention - the overall purpose of a text which conditions the interpretation of its
individual features - must be determined on the basis of the whole, not on the
basis of the sources used. Consequently, the question of the extent to which any
passage can be considered a literary unit is of first importance.

The Question of Literary Unity In 1 Kings 20

The content of the chapter can be summarized as follows. Verses 112־ describe
an invasion of Israel by Ben Hadad, King of Aram, with a large force. After a
negotiation, Ahab, King of Israel, defies Ben Hadad, who orders an attack. In
verses 13-14, a prophet brings God’s assurance of victory to Ahab, and also
instructions on how to obtain it. In verses 15-21 Ahab, acting on these
instructions, defeats Ben Hadad soundly. In verse 22, the prophet warns Ahab
that Ben Hadad will invade again. Verses 23-27 describe the deliberations of the
Aramaeans, their preparations, and a second invasion of Israel. The troops of
Israel march out to meet the invading force, and the two armies camp opposite
each other. In verse 28 a “man of God” again brings God’s assurance of victory
to Ahab. In verses 29-34, battle is joined after a delay. Ahab wins a decisive
victory. As a result, he gains territorial and commercial advantages, and the two
kings make a treaty as equals. Verses 35-43 describe the activities of a prophet
who entraps Ahab, by means of a story, into condemning his own actions. The
prophet delivers God’s judgment, and Ahab returns home.

Many commentators regard the chapter as composed of two sources, one
“historical” and one “prophetic.” Opinions on the boundaries of these sources
differ, but verses 13-14, 22, 28, 35- 43, are generally among those designated as
“prophetic.”4 Parts of the repetitive material in verses 16-19 are also often held to
be secondary. If this material is not original, the question of unity concerns its
integration with the rest of the chapter. Was it the result of design or of accident?

The present positioning of the material named can be explained as designed to
raise or sustain interest in the narrative. The introduction of the prophet in verses
13 and 28, just at the point where the battle seems about to begin, delays the
narrative. The message he brings raises the question of how, or even if, the
promised victory is to be achieved. Both the delay and the question raise
suspense. The repetition of forms of 20:16-19) אצי), describing the sortie of the
troops of Israel, produces the effect of slow motion, which, again, increases the
suspense.5 The prophet’s warning in verse 22 sustains interest at a time when it
might well relax, as Ahab’s problem appears to be solved. This is also true of the
introduction of the prophet in verse 35. Here expectations are increased by the
fact that the preceding emissaries have played such an important role. The air of
mystery imparted to the actions of this prophet during the slow build-up towards
his denunciation of Ahab also heightens suspense.

Similar usage can be seen in other narratives. The repeated presentation of the
“arrival” of a messenger in 1 Samuel 4:12-14 with forms of 6אוב produces the
effect of slow motion, as does the repetition in 1 Kings 20:16-19. The delay of
the actual delivery of the message until 1 Sm 4:17 also helps to increase the
suspense. The technique of interrupting the narrative is used in 1 Samuel 17.
Goliath approaches David in verse 41, but David’s reaction is not given until
verse 48. The action is delayed by the insertion of material (the speeches of the

combatants) important for the narrator’s message. The delay, at this crucial point
in the narrative, heightens suspense. The introduction of the prophet in 1 Kgs
20:13, 28, appears to use the same’technique for the same purpose.

If the use of “secondary” materials in 1 Kings 20 could represent a technique and
purpose common to other narratives, they could have been placed where they
now stand in an intentional process of composition.7 The chapter is characterized
by the regular use of certain syntactic features, showing that its unity is, in fact, a
probability. Subject-verb order is used to introduce a main protagonist in a major
new section of narrative in vv. 1, 23, 35, and in the imbedded story in v. 39.
Where a previously unmentioned character is introduced within such a section,
the same order is used, with the subject introduced by הנהו, in v. 13 and in the
imbedded story in v. 39. A pronominal subject preceding its verb is used to mark
reversion to a preceding subject after digression on some other topic in v. 19
(הלאו), and v. 39 (אוהו); (cf. the anaphoric םישנאהו in v. 33). יהיו is used to
introduce the crucial event in a narrative section in vv. 12, 26, 29, 39, and, in the
imbedded story, in v. 40.

Features of this sort are common enough in the Former Prophets, but they are not
evenly distributed.8 The regularity of usage shown in 1 Kings 20 is unusual, and
clearly ought to be considered where the question of unity is raised. Such
regularity could well represent the style of a particular narrator, characterizing
the chapter as a literary unity. This study proceeds on the assumption that this
conclusion is correct.

The “Narrator’s Intention”

There is a wide consensus among historians that the events described in this
chapter did not take place in Ahab’s reign, and some uncertainty as to when they
did occur.9 If this is correct, it is difficult to believe that the narrator’s primary
intention was the simple recounting of historical events on a human level. If the
“historical” materials were taken from some form of secular history, as is often
suggested, the replacement of the original king by Ahab must surely show that
they are used here with a different purpose. A study of references to kings shows
that a designation of the type “king of Israel,” standard for the king in this
chapter, is normally used only by or to foreigners. Consequently, if this secular
history originated in the northern kingdom, as is often suggested, it has certainly
been changed for use in its present situation.

Long has pointed out the narrator’s interest in the “preparation,” the events
leading up to the battles, and the corresponding lack of interest in the events of
the battles themselves.10 The description of the preparation for both battles
fosters the expectation of Aramaean victory to the extent that this must be seen
as an important purpose for the narrator. Before the first battle, the reference to
the 32 kings allied to Ben Hadad (1 Kgs 20:16, repeating 20:1), contrasts with

the number 7,232, given in v. 15 as the total for Ahab’s force, suggesting
overwhelming Aramaean numerical superiority. Beyond this stress on the
disparity in numbers, the arrogance of Ben Hadad’s messages (vv. 3, 5-6, 10),
and the confidence which he displays in his drinking with his allies (v. 12), and
in his assumption that any “men” coming out of the city will easily be captured
(v. 18) also gives the impression that his victory is inevitable.11 Ahab himself
clearly feels that he has no chance against the invaders, as is shown by his ready
submission to Ben Hadad’s first demand.

Before the second battle, the expectation of Aramaean victory is raised by the
description of their serious preparation for the campaign, including the raising of
an army equal to the former one (v. 25). The disparity of numbers is further
emphasized by the striking simile in verse 27, as Long notes. The care expended
on emphasizing the superiority of Aram in human terms must surely have the
purpose of emphasizing the miraculous nature of Israel’s victory - victory due to
God, and not to the relatively insignificant forces at Ahab’s command.

The battle accounts, the core of the chapter, can, then, easily be understood as
intended primarily to demonstrate the power of God over human forces, and the
miraculous benefits which flow from obedience to him. The terminal section,
verses 35-43, is fully consistent with this view. After the second battle, Ahab
follows human wisdom. He regains territory, obtains commercial concessions,
and establishes a stable relationship with a neighbor he could not expect to
dominate in the long term. This is contrary to Ahab’s duty to God, so he is
punished for his disobedience. It is true that the reader is not told how Ben
Hadad should have been treated, but this is not necessary. The ancient audience
would know Ahab’s duty as clearly as he should have himself.12

The punishment for Ahab’s disobedience is to be death, in accordance with the
prophet’s story. It is possible that לוקשת in that story (1 Kgs 20:39) should be
translated “You will receive [as a reward],” not “You will pay [as a penalty].”13
In this case, the silver talent, far more than the value of a prisoner of war (cf. Ex
21:32, Lv 27:1-8), represents the great benefit which Ahab could have obtained
if he had done his duty.

Points of Detail

It appears, then, that the chapter is intended to illustrate the omnipotence of God,
the benefits of obedience to him, and the penalty of disobedience, by means of a
narrative of events in the career of Ahab. If this view is correct, the correct
interpretation of other features in the narrative will accord with this intention and
support it.14 Further details of the chapter are considered from this viewpoint.
Some features of the battle narratives can be interpreted as strongly supporting
this view of the narrator’s intention, so these are considered first. After this,
some questions in the opening verses of the chapter are considered.

Conclusion

It is easy, then, to understand 1 Kings 20 as the composition of an individual
with a particular purpose. A narrative about Ahab is here used to demonstrate the
power of God, and the value of obedience to him. The conclusion of Long
(whose careful study of the intention of the narrator is limited to 1 Kgs 20:134־)
is consistent with this view. The “desire to express dramatically a particularizing
vision of events” which Long sees (pp. 415416־) reflects the intention of
rendering the lesson taught by the narrative readily applicable not only to the
community Israel, but also to the individual reader.

The interpretation on which this conclusion is based is only superficially
presented, but enough has been said to show that it is firmly based on linguistic
features. As stated in the introduction, the primary purpose of the article is to
demonstrate the need for more careful consideration of such features, particularly
unusual usage. The understanding of such usage is, to a large extent, dependent
on the understanding of the context of the usage, and so is not objective.
Nevertheless, it can scarcely be argued that an interpretation which ignores
unusual usage is preferable to one which attempts to understand it. The problem
is similar to that encountered with lexical items such as “UH, and should be met in
the same way. The comprehensive study of grammatical features in their
contexts, and of parallels in cognate languages, is (as with lexical items) the best
insurance against undue subjectivity.

25.  Perfect forms are regularly used for states or conditions which originated in the past, but
continue in the present. יתנקז “I am old” (I have become old) in 1 Sm 12:2, which Long
(n. 6) offers in support of his views, belongs in this category; יתענמ with the suggested
meaning “shall I withold?” would not. Perfect forms are also often used for actions
originating in a decision made in the past, even when they have not taken place, as הרכמ
(Ruth 4:3) “Naomi is selling/will sell” (she has decided to sell). Here Ahab is asking for
advice on which to base a decision. Other uses of the perfect form in the Former
Prophets, including apparent anomalies, are described in Revell, “System of the Verb,”
#3-7. The description of Waltke and O’Connor, which has a wider basis and a different
viewpoint, includes categories to which Long’s interpretation might be assigned {Syntax
#30.5.1e).
