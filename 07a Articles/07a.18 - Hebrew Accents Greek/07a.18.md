Hebrew Accents and Greek Ekphonetic Neumes
At the end of the first millennium, Biblical (and other) texts in both
Hebrew and Greek were frequently provided with ‘accent’ signs1 to
regulate the chanting of the text. The demonstration of relation­
ship between Christian and Jewish liturgical music has led to
various attempts to discover a relationship between these accent
systems. The most recent of these, Gudrun Engberg’s article
‘Greek Ekphonetic Neumes and Masoretic Accents’ supports the
idea of a close musical relationship between the systems, and sug­
gests that the thesis there put forward be further investigated.2 The
present article arises from an attempt to follow this suggestion from
the viewpoint of a hebraist.

Direct comparison of the accent systems is possible because, as
far as we can tell, the use and function of the accents was the same
both in Greek and in Hebrew texts. These texts were divided up
into units — a word or a group of words — each of which was marked
by an accent sign: at the end in Hebrew, at both beginning and
end in Greek. Each accent had a ‘musical value’ in that it indicated
the cadence to which the textual unit was to be chanted.3 Each ac­
cent also had an ‘interpunctional value’ in that it indicated the re­
lationship between that textual unit and the others in the context.
The accent signs are generally supposed to have been first intro­

duced into texts in the fifth century, although we have definite

There are other indications that this is the correct view. The only

punctuation (sof pasuq, marking the end of a verse) is not connect­

and segolta, which mark the end of the main divisions of a verse,
far from being the earliest accents known and the most commonly
marked, are, in fact, the most frequently omitted, and the latest to
be marked with specific signs (see below). This is understandable if
the system has a basically musical significance (since there is con­
siderable possibility of variation at the beginning of such a division,
but not at the end) but not if it is a system of punctuation. There
is, moreover, no evidence of development, in the Hebrew system,
from one system using a few signs to mark punctuation towards
another using many signs to mark music. There is only evidence of
development in the detail and accuracy of the marking of the same
phenomena. The system in which the signs are used does not
change. This would seem to hold true for all Hebrew systems,
whether used in Biblical or Rabbinic texts, and is probably true for
the Greek system also. The Greek accent signs, it is true, may well
have developed from punctuation signs, but I can see no evidence of a
smooth process of evolution. I would assume that a system of signs
intended to mark musical phenomena was intentionally created,
using, in some cases, the same signs as were used as punctuation
markers.

It is, then, assumed in this paper that the basic function of the
accent signs was musical, and that their function as markers of re­
lationship was secondary.9 The signs are, moreover, regarded, not
as individual entities with an intrinsic meaning, but as items in a
system outside of which they are meaningless. The various types of
accentuation are compared on the basis of the systems thus dis­
played, not on the basis of a particular text which they are used to
punctuate. It is, after all, clearly true in all systems that the accen­
tuation, in showing the relationship of the units of text to each
other, shows which are to be emphasized, and to what extent. The
accentuation controls the text, not vice versa (see note 66).

THE HEBREW BIBLICAL ACCENTS

/. General
The basic unit of the Hebrew Biblical accentuation10 is, of course,
the verse. The length of a verse may vary from a few words to many,
and the syntactic structure relating those words from a part of a
clause to a group of clauses. Nevertheless, the accentuation of each
verse follows the same basic pattern, and is autonomous — inde­
pendent of anything in the preceding or following verses.

Averse is normally divided into two halves. The end of the first

half is marked by atnah, and the end of the second (and of the
whole verse) by silluq. Excepting this feature (and the use of segol·
tn, see below) the two halves have the same possibilities of accentu­
ation. They are composed of a terminal clause which may be pre­
ceded by one or more contextual clauses. The terminal clause is ac­
cented tebir־־* tiflia ־+ atnah/silluq, with tebir omitted from shorter
clauses. In longer clauses, the minor accents from the contextual
clause (rebia etc.) are used to mark divisions preceding tebir. The
end of the contextual clause is marked with zaqef. This may be the
only accent in the contextual clause, but most commonly it is pre­
ceded by pash^a. Divisions preceding pashta are marked by rebia,
wesh, pazer, legarmeh, or telisha, selected and arranged according
to the requirements of the situation. More than one contextual
clause may be used in one verse half. In the first verse half, the first
of two or more contextual clauses may be made to stand out by
the use of zarqa and segolta instead of pashta and zaqef to mark
the last two phrases. A typical verse, then, is divided into two halves,
each of which is composed of one contextual and one terminal
clause (e. g. Lam. 1:1,2). More than one contextual clause may
occur in the first half (Lam. 1:3,4), the second half (Lam. 1:10)
or both halves (Lam. 1:7), and, if required, the first of two or more
contextual clauses in the first verse half may be marked out by the
use of zarqa-segolta (Lam. 1:12). Either verse half may be composed
only of a terminal clause (Lam. 1:3,4, 5, second half, 1:6 first half).
If the verse is unusually short, it is not divided into halves by atnah.
It may then be composed of one contextual and one terminal
clause (Lam. 3:1), or simply of one terminal clause (Lam. 3:2, 3).

the Hebrew Biblical accentuation is the verse; characteristically
divided into two halves, each of which shows a terminal clause

2  . The Relation of the Rabbinic to the Biblical Accentuation

the question of the relationship of the systems of accentuation used
in the two types of text. Yeivin, indeed, suggests that the accentua­

types of text. The Biblical verse is (relatively) short, and is divided

long, and shows only one terminal clause, usually preceded by sev­

Neither the teleia unit, nor the paragraph, show the typical feature
of the Hebrew Biblical accentuation: division into two halves, each
of which has a terminal clause. On the contrary, we find a number
of contextual clauses (teleia units) followed by a single terminal
clause, just as in the Mishna texts. There can be no question but
that the Greek Biblical accentuation and the Hebrew Rabbinic ac­
centuation follow the same pattern. It is, of course, obvious that
the two types of accentuation show numerous differences. The
most striking of these is in the writing system. Hebrew accentuation
(of any type) marks only the end of a phrase. In the Greek accent-
i uation both the beginning and the end are marked. This difference
might be considered by some to be superficial, but it seems to me
so basic as to eliminate the possibility of any important influence
by one writing system on the other. It indicates a difference not
just in the method of marking, but in the nature of what is marked.
The Greek system is, it seems to me, marking a specific cadence to
which the textual unit is to be chanted. The Hebrew method marks
only the final motif, but (unless conjunctive accents are used)
leaves the beginning of the cadence, where a certain amount of
variation is possible, to the reader’s knowledge of the tradition.

Other obvious features in which the Greek Biblical accentuation

; differs from the systems used in Rabbinic texts are the use of an
I opening clause, and the more varied possibilities for the accentua­
tion of contextual clauses, marked by a larger number of signs, the
use of which is, to some extent, determined by the content of the
textual unit marked. It seems to me, however, that these differences
could easily be explained as the result of different developments
from a common original. Certainly the introduction of accents 9
and 10 into the Rabbinic accentuation suggests a development
towards a closer relationship between the accent signs and the con­
tent of the texts, as found in the Greek accentuation.51

THE SYRIAC ACCENTUATION

One cannot reasonably consider the problem of the relationship
of the Greek and the Hebrew systems of accentuation without
also considering the Syriac system. Syrian influence on the liturgy
and music of the early Byzantine church is well known, and
Syriac manuscripts provide the earliest known examples of accent
signs. Their example may well have stimulated both the Jews and
the Greeks to develop their own writing systems.

The Syriac accentuation is described by J. B. Segal in The Dia­
critical Point and the Accents in Syriac.56 It appears that the unit
of accentuation here is a short clause ending with pasoqa. The textual
unit so marked is similar in length to the teleia unit in the Greek
accentuation, and in Biblical texts, the two often coincide exact­

however, that further study of the Syriac accentuation, specifically
for the purpose of comparison with the Greek might yield interest­
ing results.

CONCLUSION

This brief survey suggests that, although the practice of chanting
Biblical (and other) texts was current throughout the Judaeo-
Christian world, several different forms of chant were in use. One
of these, the Hebrew Biblical chant, appears to have been unique.
It could be argued that the halving of Syriac pasoqa units reflects
a common basis, but the age of the Hebrew system, and the lack of
any parallels in the Syriac system beyond the simple division into
two parts, appear to deny this as a possibility. The Syriac system,
it seems to me, should be classified with the Greek and Rabbinic
systems, all of which are characterized by relatively short contextual
clauses with relatively simple accentuation. In all three systems the
texts were normally divided into paragraphs. In the Greek and Rab­
binic systems, but not in the Syriac, the end of these was marked

A Method for Indexing Byzantine Music

Since one of the fundamental aspects of Byzantine music is the
utilization of melodic formulae one might be led to believe that the
best way of musical identification of the hymns would be to pre­
pare an index (or repertory) of such melodic formulae. It appears
to us, however, that the traditional way of preparing catalogues of
incipits may actually be a better method for identification for the
Byzantine Chant. In Enrica Follieri’s comprehensive catalogue of
text-incipits a sufficient number of letters of the text gives the full
identification of the hymn texts. A sufficient number of neumes is
supposed to result in the same kind of full identification of the
single melodies of the hymns.

This method was tried out on thirty-five Dogmatic hymns fully
encoded in a computer.1 The machine was asked to take from the
encoded material the first twelve intervals of each hymn and the
corresponding neumes, the mode, the starting tone, and the refer­
ence to source as well as to Tillyard’s transcription. The result of
this inquiry may be seen in Ex. 1. The thirty-five incipits were now
arranged by four criteria: interval, neume, mode, and starting tone,
which taken together give twenty-four indexing possibilities.2 Only
four of these will be given here.

Ex. I shows musical incipits arranged by intervals. The intervals

are counted as steps, i. e. Ison = 0, Oligon = 1, Kentema = 2, etc.,
following the ’Dictionary of Intervals’(cf.Studies in Eastern Chant,
II, p. 135). The intervals are ordered from the most positive to the
most negative through all the eight modes. It is thus possible with
the help of this index to look up any melody including melodies
transcribed in Western notation (in MMB, Transcripta), and also
cases when the signature is not readable.
