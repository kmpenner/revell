DEḤIQ: EXCEPTIONS TO
THE STANDARD PATTERN

E. J. Revell
University of Toronto

  • 1. 1. Deḥiq typically occurs in the first letter of a word of which the first full vowel is stressed, and which follows a word ending in unstressed segol or qameṣ, where the two are joined by maqqef or by a conjunctive accent. It does not occur where the letter in question is waw, 'alef, he, ḥet, or 'ayin, (but it does occur in resh). Where the unstressed vowel at the end of the preceding word is followed by 'alef, deḥiq either does not occur, or is extremely rare, in biblical Hebrew.1 Consequently, a pair of words joined by maqqef or a conjunctive accent is called a "standard pair" for the occurrence of deḥiq where the first word ends in unstressed qameṣ or segol which is not followed by 'alef, and the second begins with a letter other than waw, 'alef, he, ḥet or 'ayin, and is stressed on its first full vowel. For purposes of this study, where the first word of a pair has the postpositive conjunctive accent telisha qeṭanna (so that its stress position is uncertain), the pair is considered standard where stress on that word would typically be

penultimate, not otherwise. In Exodus 20:3, 4, and the parallels in Deut 5:7, 8, two words which form a standard pair are joined by maqqef in one scheme of accentuation (the “upper”), but in the other (the “lower”) they are joined by a conjunctive accent which marks stress on the first word as final. These pairs also are not considered as “standard” here.2

2. As was shown in the paper I read at the last meeting of this society,3 the standard pattern of the occurrence of deḥiq can be described very simply.4

2.1 GROUP 1. Deḥiq occurs where the first word in a standard pair ends in unstressed segol, as עֶ֫זֶר־כְּנֶגְדּֽוֹ (Gen 2:18). The first word of the pair is usually an imperfect verb form from a root IIIh, as in the example. In a few cases it is a participle or other noun from such a root, or a pronoun (אֵ֫לֶּה, זֶ֫ה). There are 17 exceptions in 277 cases (6%).

2.2 GROUP 2. Deḥiq occurs where the first word in a standard pair ends in unstressed qameṣ and (i) never had final stress, as עָשִׂ֫יתָ זֹּאת (Gen 3:14), or (ii) has its typical final stress nullified by maqqef or nesiga, but has typical final stress in MT only due to a shift which occurred relatively recently in the history of the language, as אָ֫לָה־לִּי (Jer 5:5), and 2m.s perfect forms with waw consecutive (on which see #8.1). The first word is usually a verb form: 2m.s or 3f.s perfect, modal with affixed qameṣ he (1st person imperfect, or m.s imperative, including הָ֫בָה) or f.pl imperfect. It may also be a noun form with affixed qameṣ he “directional,” or with the ending -’ātāh (GK #90.g), a verb, noun, or preposition with a pronominal suffix, or a pronoun or other particle (חָלִ֫ילָה, לָ֫מָּה, מְאוּמָה, הֵ֫מָּה). In group 2 overall, there are 41 exceptions in 548 cases (7%). Of these exceptions, 17 occur in group 2.ii, where they form 9% of the total.

2.3 GROUP 3. Deḥiq does not occur where the first word ends in qameṣ which is unstressed through maqqef or nesiga, but (apart from such special features of the reading tradition) has always been typically stressed in recent history of the language, as תֹּרָה לָּךְ (Gen 4:6). The first word is most commonly a 3m.s perfect verb form from a root IIIh, as in the example. It may also be a noun with the typical feminine ending qameṣ he (including the unusual forms אֹ֫בְיָה Ps 120:5, לְהַנְזָקָה Dan 2:46). In Dan 2:29 it is the interrogative מָה; in Amos 1:11 it is שָׁמְרָה, the last syllable of which represents the 3f.s pronoun. There is one exception in 189 cases (1%).

Deḥiq: Exceptions to Standard Pattern

Regular Exceptions

3. When deḥiq is described in this way, it becomes clear that its use is much more regular than is generally supposed. Moreover, the description above gives gross figures for exceptions. In some cases, exceptions to the rule given include most, or all, members of a group which is defined by phonological or morphological features, and so can be considered as regular features of the overall pattern.

3.1 Deḥiq does not occur in the second word of a standard pair where this is a noun (stressed on its first syllable) with a prefixed preposition (-לְ or -בְּ). It is noteworthy that all thirteen pairs showing this exception occur in group 2. In group 1, deḥiq does occur in initial prepositional lamed even where followed by a noun in יִהְיֶה־לְעָם (Gen 48:19). It is noteworthy that in standard pairs of which the first letter of the second word is followed by shewa, but does not represent a preposition, the word is a noun with initial mem or shin.

3.2 Dehiq does not occur in L in eight of nine cases in which the second word of a standard pair has dagesh in its second letter (שָׁמָּה, מָּה). In L, dagesh following מָה is omitted from mem and shin and some other letters under the same conditions, as pointed out by Dotan.5

3.3 Dehiq does not occur where the first word of a standard pair is a feminine plural imperfect or imperative form in which the stressed penultimate vowel is segol, (except where the following nun has dagesh as in Mic. 7:10 (תִּרְאֶינָה בָּהּ).

3.4 In the Aramaic sections of L, dehiq occurs where the first word of a standard pair ends in unstressed qames followed by 'alef where this word is a first person plural perfect verb (as אֲמַרְנָא לָהֶם Ezra 5:4, 9, also 5:10). It does not where the first word in the pair is the negative particle לָא (Dan 3:12, 27, 6:14, 25), or a noun in the determined state (as וְעִבָידְתָּא דָה Ezra 5:8, also Dan 4:9, 18, 7:8) with the exception of דִּלָא מָטָא (Dan 4:25).

Irregular Exceptions

4. If these "regular exceptions" are treated as features of standard usage, the largest group of cases in which dehiq is expected but does not appear belong to division (ii) of group 2: pairs in which the first word is a verb form (or, in two cases, Gen 24:42, Ps 109:27, a noun with a 2m.s pronominal suffix) which shows final stress in MT (in its typical medial form) as the result of a shift from penultimate stress which occurred relatively recently. It is not surprising to find that, in a phenomenon which is conditioned by stress position, exceptions occur where this has recently changed.

4.1 In group 2.ii, dehiq almost always occurs where the second word of the pair has the form CV or CVC. This occurs

with a noun in 17 cases, a preposition with a pronominal suffix in 92 cases, an adverb in 4 cases, and with the particle נא in 34 cases. Three exceptions occur.

4.1.1 Where the second word of the pair is a preposition with a pronominal suffix in Exod 21:4 נָתַן־לוֹ - the same pair shows dehiq in Gen 21:3 and elsewhere; Jer 20:10 וְנָוּכְלָה לוֹ - there is no exact parallel to this, but a similar pair occurs in Ps 83:13 נִירְשָׁה לָּנוּ (also 1 Kgs 17:17, Jer 5:5); Job 5:23 הָשְׁלְמָה־לָּךְ. The closest parallel structure in L is שָׂחֲקָה־לָּהּ (Ps 122:3). These cases appear to be anomalies. British Library MS Or. 4445 (B) agrees with L in Exod 21:4, as do A and C in Jer 20:10. However A shows dagesh in the case in Job 5:23, so its absence in L may be due to error.

4.1.2 Dehiq does not occur where the second word is the noun רָע in אֹהֵב־רָע Prov 21:10 (also Prov 20:22). There are no cases in this group in which dehiq occurs in resh, so possibly its absence in these cases is conditioned by the phonological characteristics of this consonant.

4.2 Where the first word is a verb, and the second is a preposition with a suffix composing a word of form CVCV, dehiq occurs in all nine cases, as in נִירְשָׁה־לָּנוּ (Ps 83:13). Where the second word is a noun of form CVCV or CVCVC, dehiq occurs in only five of nine cases.

4.2.1 Dehiq occurs where the noun is the object of the preceding verb, which is preceded by its subject or a modifier. The clause is marked with revia’ mugrash and silluq, and forms the second and final half of a verse of poetry, as Job 5:16 וְעֹלָתָה קָפְצָה פִּיהָ (also Ps 26:10, 107:42, Prov 31:16), or is marked with deḥi and atnaḥ following ‘ole we-yored, and forms the first division of the second part of the verse (Prov 30:16). Dehiq also occurs in נַעֲמָדָה יָּחַד (which forms a clause ending the first half of Isa 50:8), the only case with a verb of this form.

4.2.2 Dehiq does not occur in subject - verb - object structures which begin the verse in Ps 84:4, Job 24:15 (the latter is not a complete clause). It does not occur in accentual units of the type described in #4.2.1 where the first word of the pair is preceded by another verb (not by a subject or modifier) in Prov 30:20 אָכְלָה וּמָחֲתָה פִּיהָ, nor in the similar structure which

begins Isa 56:12, nor in אֶמְלְלָה־גָּפֶן (Isa 24:7), the second of two two-word clauses of structure verb - subject. That is, the situations in which deḥiq does occur differ from those in which it does not occur. The specific differences could produce prosodic features which could condition its use.

4.3 Deḥiq also does not occur in group 2.ii where the second word has shewa under its first letter; the word is a preposition with a pronominal suffix in אֶחָי־לָךְ (1 Kgs 21:2, 6), a noun in תְּנָה־בְנִי (Prov 23:26). There are no cases of this sort in which deḥiq does occur.

4.4 It seems clear, then, that, in group 2.ii, the occurrence of deḥiq is conditioned by the form of the second word, and by its relationship to the first. It appears that the occurrence of deḥiq is inhibited where the second word is “long” in that the stressed vowel is followed by another vowel (#4.2), or preceded by a consonant with shewa (#4.3). It also seems probable that it is inhibited where the initial consonant of the second word is resh. On the question of relationship, the fact that a word of form CVCV always shows deḥiq where it is a preposition with a pronominal suffix, but not always where it is a noun, suggests that syntactic/semantic relationship of the sort which affects the occurrence of nesiga was significant here also. It is assumed (as with nesiga) that it was prosodic patterns reflecting these relationships which actually conditioned the use of deḥiq.7

effect of any particular feature, such as the prosodic pattern reflecting the relationship between the two words, or the presence of resh or a consonant with shewa at the beginning of the second, is dependent on the other features with which it is combined. No feature conditions such variation independently, as is already indicated, for deḥiq, by the fact that the standard pair is characterized by a combination of features, not by one alone.

5. The remaining cases in which deḥiq fails to occur where expected in a standard pair can be treated together. Most occur in category 1.

5.1 Deḥiq fails to occur in יְהֹוָה־קֹדֶשׁ (Lev 27:33), but does occur here in B, as it does in L in the same pair in Lev 27:9, 10, 32, (and in 17 other cases in a pair composed of a verb followed by a segolate noun). It fails to occur in חָמְדָה־לָּנוּ (1 Kgs 20:25), but occurs in לָּךְ in 14 other cases. Deḥiq also fails to occur in תוֹעֵבָה־הִיא (Jer 22:22). Deḥiq does occur in fourteen other cases in nouns of form CVC, including nouns with initial resh in Prov 11:21, Job 39:9. There are no other examples in which the vowel is followed by pataḥ furtive, but it seems probable that both this and the other cases treated in this paragraph are isolated anomalies.

5.2 Where the first word of a standard pair is the pronoun אֵלֶּה, deḥiq occurs in five cases in which the second word of the pair is a preposition with a pronominal suffix, as מִי־אֵלֶּה לָּךְ (Gen 33:5, and similar structures 2 Sam 16:2, Ezek 24:19, 37:18), and אֲשֶׁר־אֵלֶּה לּוֹ (Gen 38:25). Deḥiq does not occur in the pair וְאֵלֶּה שְׁמוֹת (Gen 46:8, Exod 6:16. B agrees with L). There is no other example of a standard pair in which (as here) the second word is a construct noun. The first word in such cases (i.e. a word preceding a construct noun) typically has a disjunctive accent (as in the other cases of ואלה שמות showing that the second word of the pair is more closely related to

what follows than to what precedes (i.e. the first word). Presumably this relatively weak relationship of the two words in the pair וְאֵלֶּה שְׁמוֹת prevented the development of dehiq, despite the fact that the words were joined by a conjunctive accent, as also occurs with nesiga. The other two cases in which dehiq fails to occur in a standard pair of which the first word is אֵלֶּה, אֵלֶּה פֹה are אֲנַחְנוּ אֵלֶּה (Deut 5:3), and שַׁחֲרִי־אֵלֶּה רָעַ (Isa 47:9). In both cases, the two words forming the pair belong to different constituents of the clause. It seems possible that here, too, the prosodic features reflecting the syntactic/semantic relationship of the words forming the pair inhibited the development of dehiq. Again parallels can be found in the occurrence of nesiga.8

5.3 There is no close parallel to בָּרְרָה עֵדְוֹתֶיךָ שִׁעְשָׁעוּ (Ps 119:14). There are cases in this group (2.i) in which dehiq is shown in a pair of which the first word ends a phrase (as Deut 23:6, 2 Sam 9:10, Ps 61:5), and in pairs in which the first word modifies a following verb (as Ezek 40:4, 44:5), but no other example shows both features. It seems possible that the prosodic features reflecting this pair of relationships also inhibited the development of dehiq.

5.4 Dehiq fails to occur in L in Gen 23:9, יִתֶּן־לִי - dagesh occurs in closely similar pairs in 1 Sam 18:21, 21:10. Dehiq also fails to occur in וּבְאָזְנֶיךָ שָׁמָע (Ezek 3:10), where it occurs in A and C, as in all three MSS in the same pair in Ezek 40:4, 44:5. Consequently this case appears to be due to error in L. That in Gen 23:9 may also be due to error, but B is not available for comparison.

  • 6. Dehiq occurs in two cases in the second word of a standard pair where it is contrary to the standard pattern.
    • 6.1 The use of dehiq in יַרְדָּה מַּיִם (Lam 1:16) contrasts with its absence in לְמַטָּה לּוֹ (Isa 40:10), קָצְרָה כֹּחַ (Isa 59:19). Features of Tiberian pointing strongly suggest that a participle was treated as a perfect verb (with consequent minor adjustment of stress position and vowel pattern) if both its morphological features and its syntactic/semantic context would allow this.10 The participle in Lam 1:16 could have been treated in this way (it is followed by perfect verbs) while those in Isa 40:10, and Isa 59:19, which stand in a present/future context, were not. The failure to use dehiq in these two cases is expected after nouns (group 3).11 It must be admitted, however,

6.2 In וְעָנְתָה שָּׁמָּה (Hos 2:17), the use of dehiq is atypical of L (see #3.2) although not of A and C. This may simply represent error or inconsistency in the naqdan of L.

8. Dagesh may, then, occur in almost any letter under conditions related to those typical for dehiq, but occurs most commonly in lamed, mem, and nun. As is well known, these

letters show other unusual uses of dagesh in some non-standard forms of pointing. As remarked in #7.1 and note 16, some other cases of "deḥiq-related" dagesh seem equally to be related to uses of dagesh which bear no obvious relation to deḥiq. This suggests that what is here called deḥiq is merely the most common case of phenomenon which occurred in a considerably wider context. A great deal of work is needed before this wider context can be fully understood. More work is needed even to establish whether the use of dagesh in the cases listed in ##7.1-3 is conditioned in some way, or is merely sporadic.17 There is, however, no doubt about the regularity of the standard form of deḥiq. The cases which are certainly anomalies (the seven pairs designated as anomalous in ##5.1, 4, 6.1, 2) form less than 1% of the total. Whether these arose in the process of transmission, or represent a feature of the tradition which is not understood, they form a proportion far too small to affect the conclusion that the standard form of deḥiq arises from automatic phonological conditioning.

9. The necessity of mentioning shifts in stress position in the definition of group 2.ii of the standard pattern shows that deḥiq has implications for the history of the language. These cannot be explored in full, but their importance can be illustrated by one point. Deḥiq occurs consistently after 2m.s perfect forms with waw consecutive where these have maqqef, or a conjunctive accent with penultimate stress, just as it does after the same forms with no prefixed conjunction. That is, in the matter of deḥiq, waw consecutive perfect forms act as members of group 2.ii. In the other forms in this sub-group, the penultimate syllable, which was open with a short vowel, was stressed until a period relatively recent in the history of the Tiberian tradition, as is well known. The fact that 2m.s perfect forms with waw consecutive act in the same way seems to me
