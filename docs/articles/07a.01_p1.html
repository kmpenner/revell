THE ORDER OF THE ELEMENTS IN THE VERBAL STATEMENT CLAUSE IN i Q SEREQ W

I. — In many languages, exempli gratia in English, the order of the elements in a syntactic structure is of considerable impor­ tance. This is true in the verbal clause in Hebrew, a fact noticed by most grammars (2). This paper seeks to describe this facet of Hebrew syntax as it occurs in the dialect represented by the Rule of the Community (= 1 Q S). The material provided by this single text is severely limited, but the limitation can be defend­ ed on the ground that it is desirable that a study of this sort be, in so far as is possible, synchronic (3).

  1. — The Verbal Element (V). The simplest form of the verbal element is a single verb form (v). Any verb form consists of a verbal base augmented by a bound pronominal element (5). These pronominal elements form two paradigms. In one the bound pronouns are all suffixed to the verbal base. In the other the pronominal elements appear either as prefixes, or as a combination of prefix and suffix. In this way verb forms in 1 Q S can be morphologically defined. The verb forms marked out by this definition differ from other words in their syntax as well as in morphology (6).

  2. — The verbal element in a verbal clause appears in two ways other than as a single verb form. The verbal element may be made up of two verb forms joined by a conjunction (c). In this case any other elements forming part of the same verbal clause show concord with, or modify, both verb forms (7).

  1. — The symbol V is used to indicate the verbal element. As described above, the exponent of V in the verbal statement clause in 1 Q S may be a single verb form (V is v), two verb forms joined by a conjunction (V is v cv), or a verb form preceded by a negative (V is neg. v). A verbal element appears in every verbal clause, and may constitute a verbal clause by itself (9). Only one verbal element appears in any one clause (10).

  2. — The Noun (n). The noun is the most common class of word in 1 Q S. It may therefore be expected to play a major part in the verbal statement clause. The nouns in 1 Q S can be isolated morpho­ logically by the fact that they are capable of augmentation by some or all of a paradigm of suffixes (11). The base to which these augments are bound may itself occur without inflectional augment as a noun. In such cases it can be recognized as a noun by the syntactic positions which it occupies.

  3. — Nouns are very often modified by other words or word groups. The noun is then the “headword” of the structure formed by itself and its modifiers. Any structure of which the headword is a noun has syntactic functions identical with those of the noun. Id est it can substitute for a noun in any structure in which a noun can occur, without changing the grammatical meaning of that structure. Hence it is called a “noun substitute” (12).

  1. — A noun substitute of another type is the pronoun. Pro­ nouns form a class of limited membership the members of which do not conform to the morphological characteristics of the noun. They cannot take the paradigm of inflectional affixes by which noun forms are augmented. They do, however, themselves form a paradigm comparable to that formed by the nominal affixes. They also occupy the syntactic positions permissible to nouns. They are therefore to be regarded as noun substitutes, or, perhaps more conveniently, as a subclass of the noun (13).

10 . — The symbol N indicates a noun or noun substitute.

As indicated above, any noun or pronoun, any structure the head­ word of which is a noun or pronoun, or any other structure the syntactic function of which is the same as that of a noun or pro­ noun, is considered to be an exponent of N in a verbal statement clause.

. — The Nominal Elements (Ns, N, pN) (14).

II Exponents of N function in 1 Q S in four ways. One of these has already been mentioned. Verb forms contain an exponent of N in the form of a bound pronoun. These bound pronouns are allomorphs of the personal pronouns. These allomorphs occur only in this position, and the position can be occupied by no other type of exponent of N. The position of these bound pronouns in relation to the verbal base has no effect on, or relation to, the position of the other elements in the verbal statement clause. Consequently their position can be ignored in the present study.

  1. — The N element. Where a noun or noun substitute modifies the verb, concord is not a relevant factor. Such nouns or noun substitutes are indicated by the symbol N. More than one exponent of N can occur in any verbal statement clause.

  2. — The pN element. The fourth way in which nouns and noun substitutes function in the verbal statement clause in 1 Q S is in the pN element. Here they are preceded by a preposition (p), and modify the verb. Pre­ positions form a class of limited membership the members of which are recognizable only from their syntactic function. They stand before exponents of N and infinitives, and cannot be sepa­ rated from them.

  3. — Among the exponents of p are two morphemes, both written ’t. These two morphemes are formally distinguished only when they occur bound to a bound pronoun. In this case one appears as ’wt- (15), and the other as ,t- (16). These two morphemes are not otherwise grammatically distinguished in 1 Q S. Consequently, although there is some doubt as to whether ’t/’wt- is a true preposition (17), all instances of ’τ, ’t-, and ’wt- in 1 Q S must be treated as exponents of p (18).

  4. — The pN element is, therefore, composed of an exponent of p followed by an exponent of N. More than one exponent of pN can occur in any one clause.

  5. — The Infinitive (i). Infinitives form a class of unlimited membership, but appear in only a small number of forms. These forms show morpho­ logical connection with the verbal bases. The infinitive functions in two ways in the verbal statement clause in 1 Q S. Most com­ monly, it occurs in the pl element described below. In a few other cases it functions as a noun substitute. In these latter cases it is, of course, indicated by the symbol N (19).

  1. — The Infinitival Element (pl). Where the infinitive does not function as a noun substitute, it occurs in the pl element. In this structure, the infinitive is preceded by a preposition, and modifies the verb. The infinitive itself is usually modified by exponents of N, and/or pN, and/or pl (20). The pl element, then, consists of a preposition followed by an infinitive, or by any structure the headword of which is an infinitive. More than one exponent of pl can occur in any one clause.

  2. — The Particles (pt). Under this title are listed the words ’z, ’hr, btrm, trm, kkh, kn, cwd, sm, and tmyd. These words all occur in verbal clauses in 1 Q S, but they do not occur in sufficient numbers to permit their division into smaller classes (21). Particles rarely occur between the elements already described. They are not, therefore, of great interest to the discussion of the positions permissible to the major clause-building elements in relation to each other, but only for their position in relation to the beginning of the clause.

  3. — Other particles do, of course, occur in verbal clauses. They, however, either form part of a clause-building element (22), or mark the relation of the clause to other clauses in the text (23). The former have been described as far as is necessary, and the latter, since they do not affect the order of the elements in the clause, are of no concern to this study (24).

  4. — The verbal statement clause in 1 Q S is, then, made up from the elements V, Ns, N, pN, pl, and pt. As was suggested above, the order in which these elements occur in the verbal clause is of considerable syntactic importance. V is the only element which is indispensable to the verbal clause. It therefore seems most logical to study the order of the elements in relation to V.
