THE SYSTEM OF THE FINITE VERB IN CLASSICAL BIBLICAL HEBREW

Peter J. Gentry

University of Toronto

This article provides a comprehensive description of the verbal system of classical biblical Hebrew, explaining how tense, aspect, and modality are signaled and correlating form and function in a way that accounts adequately and simply for the entire range of usage. A sensitive balance is maintained between synchronic and diachronic analysis. Also considered are communicative constraints, such as discourse grammar, pragmatics, and contextual meanings versus implicature. The base forms are primarily aspectual, with temporal deixis a function of aspectual default and discourse setting. Integrated within the model is a fresh proposal for understanding so-called volitives in classical biblical Hebrew. Normally command forms in Hebrew are considered a unified system of volitives consisting of cohortative, imperative, and jussive. Following up on the work of Niccacci, Revell and more recently Shulman on (so-called) modal forms in Hebrew, it is argued that the volitives should not be viewed as a unified system. Rather, projective modality apart from imperative forms is signaled by position of the verb. This in itself is not new. It is further argued, however, that the -āh suffix normally considered to be the marker of the cohortative functions in a manner similar to the Akkadian ventive and has nothing to do with projective modality. The explanatory power of this proposal is such that it can explain the distribution of the -āh suffix on all verb forms in the Hebrew Bible as well as forms usually eliminated from discussion as extrasystemic or corruptions in the text. Striking support, moreover, is found in the Amarna Canaanite texts.

New approaches and disciplines as well as refinements of the philolog- ical tradition, whether applied in combination or separately, continue to advance our understanding of the verbal system of classical biblical Hebrew. The discipline of linguistics offers new approaches, categories, and models. Cross-linguistic studies provide a framework for describing how human languages work. Categories such as tense, aspect, and modality have been clarified and refined as well as the way in which different human languages code these parameters. Discourse grammar has focused on fea- tures of the language operating beyond the level of the individual sentence on the discourse as a whole. Pragmatics has delineated ways in which lan- guages encode in their structure the relationship between context and lan- guage.5 A consensus concerning the Hebrew verb is beginning to emerge from applying such tools to classical biblical Hebrew. In addition, similar tools are being applied to cognate languages and as the corpus of inscrip- tional texts increases, so does the power of comparative linguistic study.

This paper attempts to describe the Hebrew verbal system by (1) giving a linguistic orientation for understanding the verb, (2) interacting with past theories and building on recent studies, (3) correlating form and function in a model which accounts adequately and simply for the range of usage.6

FORM AND FUNCTION IN A LINGUISTIC MODEL OF THE HEBREW VERBAL SYSTEM

No space can be given here for a detailed history of previous theories. It is sufficient to note that for a long time the system of the verb was understood to signal primarily two categories of tense: past (suffix) and non-past (prefix).13 This understanding was abandoned for about a century and a half in favor of an aspectual theory also based on two categories: perfective (suffix) and non-perfective (prefix).14 Both approaches have proponents in recent studies, although a high proportion of them represent a return to a system based primarily on tense.15

Sequential and Non-Sequential are categories of discourse grammar and pragmatics described and refined helpfully by Buth.22 Initial position is topical. In contrast to focus, a device for marking important information in a clause, topic marks the narrator’s intended perspective for contextualizing the clause, that is relating it to the discourse. The Sequential forms, wayyiqtol and wəqatal, are employed to encode continuity or to foreground information in the discourse. By definition, the forms require initial position for the verb; hence the foreground of a narrative is the chain of events. Non-Sequential signals a break in the event-line indicated by both a clause constituent preposed to the verb and a different pair of verbs marking aspect and tense, that is, [x] qatal and [x] yiqtol.23 Thus Non-Sequential functions pragmatically to signal background, a category which Buth describes succinctly:

TENSE AND ASPECT

The categories of tense and aspect as applied to the Hebrew verbal system by Buth and others may be refined somewhat. Tense and aspect represent different points of departure in the human description and representation of time in language.

Tense may be defined as the presentation of time as points in a sequence; hence the focus is on notions of precedence and subsequence. Gropp’s description is to the point: “‘Tense,’ as generally understood, is a deictic category.”26 That is, it is an element within the text that points to the speech situation. If the point of reference is the moment of speaking, we have a system of absolute tense; if the point of reference is not identical to the moment of speech, we have a system of relative tense.

Aspect, as Hendel states, is concerned with the differing perceptions of an event, either seen from without as a bounded whole (perfective) or from within as an unbounded process (imperfective).27 Aspect, then, may be defined as the relationship between an event and the frame within which it is

This interpretation of Judg 3:23 (see also 2 Sam 13:18) is bound to be questioned by some. Longacre argues that wəqatal may mark climactic or pivotal status here rather than a ‘frequentative’ meaning.29 His apparently alternative interpretation does not disprove the present hypothesis, but rather supports it. The approach here can be defended as follows. First, from a synchronic point of view, [x] yiqtol and wəqatal are in complementary distribution and so must consistently function the same way, in historical narrative just as in direct speech. It is standard theory to distinguish a conjunctive wə + qatal from wəqatal, with the former functioning as [x] qatal and the latter as [x] yiqtol, but contextually or syntactically no clear or simple set of signals have been identified to prove such a distinction for classical biblical Hebrew.30 Unless we adopt an approach which bleaches the form of any semantic value,31 we ought to assume the form has the same basic meaning everywhere. Second, to assert that the form in historical narrative is imperfective and past does not mean that the meaning is always frequentative or progressive. Imperfective aspect may either (a) subsume several types of imperfective aspect32 or (b) signal a different discourse function as, for example, in Greek.33 Strong cross-linguistic evidence abounds as well as evidence from within classical biblical Hebrew that the tense-aspect of a verb may code not only lexical-semantic and propositional-semantic features, but also discourse-pragmatic features.34 If the discourse framework is conversation, the perfective normally defaults for past tense while the imperfective defaults for non-past tense.

MODALITY

Some discussions of the verbal system in Hebrew classify usage as either indicative or modal.46 This terminology is somewhat misleading, since it suggests that the indicative is a non-modal category. The present study considers language as a flexible tool in the mouth of the speaker, by which he or she describes his or her experience of reality or attempts to manipulate reality. Thus, the indicative is not to be crudely equated with the real world or with events in the real world; rather it indicates what the speaker asserts to be real or an event in the real world. Broadly speaking, modality may be divided into three categories: (1) assertive, (2) epistemic modality, and (3) deontic modality.47 Hendel’s definitions are useful:

Deontic modality (from Greek deon, “that which is binding”) involves the speaker’s will, as in statements of wish, command, permission, or obligation. These kinds of utterances express the speaker’s desire or decision concerning an action. Epistemic modality (from epistēmē, “knowledge”) involves that speaker’s opinion or knowledge about a proposition, as in statements of doubt, belief, or other shades of expectation or opinion. Epistemic utterances relate to the speaker’s state of knowledge, rather than to the speaker’s will.48

Two basic categories are defined for the system of the finite verb in classical biblical Hebrew in the model being proposed: (1) Assertive,49 and (2) Projective.50 Assertive forms are employed for both assertive and epistemic modality, the latter use being frequently distinguished from assertion by additional particles, rather than distinct forms. The projective category covers deontic modality.

Recent research by Niccacci, independent studies by Revell for Judges-Kings, and a doctoral dissertation by Revell’s student Ahouva Shulman all indicate that apart from imperatives, which are distinguished by form, Hebrew marks projective modality for first and third person verbs, that is cohortatives and jussives, by position. Consideration of the prehistory of the verb, as researched by scholars such as Moran and Rainey and evaluated by Huehnergard, reveals that there were separate prefixed forms: yaqtul was perfective and yaqtulu was imperfective. As Huehnergard suggested, projective modality was marked at the level of the phrase; the linguistic strategy employed for this was word order. Thus, for prefix forms, initial position in conversation is projective; non-initial position is assertive.

Description of the Function of Projectives in Hebrew

Second Person Projectives

The only projective verbs in classical biblical Hebrew formally marked as such are forms which are exclusively second person and inflected for gender and number. Commonly called imperative, this form is aspectually perfective and normally in initial position. If a second person projective with imperfective aspect is required, a long prefix in initial position is used to mark it. This, indeed, is rare. For a sequence or series of second person projectives, wə + imperative is used for perfective aspect, while wəqatal is employed for imperfective.

The imperative verb form is not used with negatives. Instead, vetitives are indicated by אל plus short prefix (for perfective aspect) while prohibitions (for imperfective aspect) are signaled by לא plus long prefix. Since distinguishing short and long prefix forms is impossible in most roots (that is, the forms are neutral), it is the negative (אל or לא) which clearly identifies the form as either short or long prefix.

This article surveys the system of the verb in the Former Prophets starting from the premises (advanced by others) that there are two indicative “tenses”, each represented by a “simple” and a “consecutive” form in complementary distribution, and one modal system, represented by the imperative, jussive and cohortative forms, also in complementary distribution. The main thesis is that the indicative and modal systems are fully independent because (1) imperfect forms with modal value are distinguished from indicatives by their position in the clause, (2) a perfect form with waw “consecutive” following a modal has a value different from that of a modal in the same position. The article begins with a survey of the indicative system intended to set this thesis in its proper context by showing that (1) the value of any verb form depends heavily on its syntactic/semantic context, (2) despite the distinct values of the “tenses”, in many contexts either may be used to represent the same occurrence — the choice is determined by the viewpoint of the author, or by the particular implications he intends to convey. The article concludes with a survey of the way this system developed to the end of the Biblical period.

THE USE OF INDICATIVES

2.1 The meaning carried by the opposition of the two categories of the indicative, QTL and YQTL, has been much debated. It seems to me that it is most easily presented as one of time reference: QTL ‘past’ versus YQTL ‘present/future’. While the ease of such presentation is no doubt partly due to the fact that English uses a tense system, there is no real reason to suppose that Hebrew does not.

  • (a) The fact that forms typically representing past time may represent future, and vice versa, under particular conditions, does not show that the basic system is not one of tense (pace Driver, 1892, #1). The same phenomenon occurs in English, as in the examples in Lyons (1986), p. 239.
  • (b) The criterion by which the value of a form must be judged is not its historical origin, but its use within the corpus. No matter what the origin of the waw consecutive verb forms, those which occur in the corpus must be regarded as syntactically conditioned variants of the perfect and imperfect forms. This removes any obstacle to considering the categories they represent as tense categories, as argued in Blau, 1970.
  • (c) The Hebrew of post-biblical times certainly used a tense system, and the same is widely assumed for the ancestor of the biblical language, as vestigially represented by the ‘preterite’ (or waw consecutive) use of the imperfect form. It seems likely, a priori, that the system of the intervening period would also have been one of tense. The QTL:YQTL

THE IDENTIFICATION OF MODAL FORMS

13. The modal system is represented by imperatives, used in affirmative clauses with second person subject, and by the forms of the imperfect known as "jussive" and "cohortative" used where the clause is negative, or where the subject is first or third person. Distinct modal imperfect forms occur only where the subject is first person, 2m.s., or 3m./f.s. Any original difference between the modal and the indicative forms of the imperfect has been lost where the subject is 2f.s., 2.pl, or 3.pl. (On forms with suffixes, see #14.3.) First person modal forms show the affix הָ—; the corresponding imperfect forms do not. Forms from roots IIIh show the "long" imperfect form (identical with the indicative imperfect form) where a first person modal is expected. In 2m.s. and 3m./f.s. forms, where the imperfect form shows î or û in a final closed syllable, the corresponding modal form shows e or o respectively. Where the final syllable of the imperfect form is open, modal imperfect forms from roots IIIh omit the final vowel. The same form from roots IIIʼ is used in all situations. These forms from roots IIIʼ, first person forms from roots IIIh, and all other forms which show no modal/indicative distinction, are referred to here as "neutral" forms. First person modal imperfect forms are called "affixed". Second and third person forms are called "short" in contrast to the "long" indicative forms. A form is said to have "modal value" if used in a position in which the standard system permits the use of modal forms but not indicative forms, as after the negative אַל.12

MODAL VERBS AND THE waw consecutive perfect

17. The material in #15-16, which lists the cases in which the use of imperfect forms marked as modal or indicative appears to be inconsistent with the conclusion reached in #14, provides no serious reason to doubt that conclusion. Consequently it can be stated that an imperfect form in the corpus, which begins its clause has modal value; an imperfect form which stands within its clause (and is not preceded by אל or followed by נא) has indicative value. Clearly, then, the waw consecutive perfect could only be a free variant of the modal imperfect, since it also begins its clause. It could, however, be a conditioned variant of the indicative imperfect; that is, it could have the grammatical function of representing a YQTL indicative verb in clause-initial position. That this would be a useful function is obvious. The main factor conditioning word order in biblical Hebrew is ‘thematization’. The components of a clause are arranged in the order of their significance for the speaker or narrator.20 Modal verbs, which present commands, requests, etc. are typically the most significant component of their clause, and so stand first in it. However the significance of other verbs also often requires that they stand first in their clauses, particularly where sequences of events are depicted. Hence the need to distinguish modal from indicative verbs. The fact that the waw consecutive perfect is used to distinguish a clause-initial verb as a YQTL indicative is suggested by the following considerations.

17.1 In conditional sentences in which the apodosis begins with a waw consecutive perfect verb, the only function of the particle waw is to mark

17.2 A waw consecutive perfect form follows an imperative in only about a quarter of the possible cases in the corpus, and follows a modal imperfect in little more than a tenth.22 In sharp contrast, clause initial verbs which carry on a sequence of indicatives are regularly waw consecutive in form. A waw consecutive perfect following a modal verb cannot, then, stand in the same sort of relationship to it as it does to a preceding indicative. Consequently it is reasonable to argue that the waw consecutive perfect represents the YQTL indicative as a conditioned variant of the imperfect, as suggested in #1. If this view is correct, a waw consecutive perfect following a modal verb must have connotations different from those of a modal verb in the same position. A survey of the use of the waw consecutive perfect suggests that this is indeed the case.

19.5 The waw consecutive perfect is translated as an imperative in accordance with English usage in this case, as in the others, but the use of this form shows that the verb is a YQTL indicative, with connotations different from those carried by an imperative or other modal form. A modal form is used to present a forceful command, whether its force reflects the fact that, although not first in a series of commands, it is of major significance, or reflects the urgency of the situation, or the social or moral status of the speaker relative to the person addressed. The waw consecutive perfect (a YQTL indicative form) is used in the same context to present a command which is less forceful, whether the (relative) lack of force reflects the fact that the action to be taken is a later consequence of action already forcefully commanded, or that it is not urgent, or that there is no wish to mark any difference of social or moral status between the speaker and the person addressed. HISTORICAL CONSIDERATIONS

23. The above survey shows that a plausible case can be made in support of the contention that waw consecutive perfect forms represent clause-initial YQTL verbs, and that indicative verbs carry a meaning distinct from that of modals. That this case is valid, and not merely plausible, can be argued from the changes which occurred in the verb system of later Biblical Hebrew, as shown in the books of Daniel, Ezra, and Nehemiah. The use of verbs in these books can be briefly described as follows.

23.1 The use of QTL forms does not differ significantly from that of standard prose, but there is a morphological innovation. First person imperfect forms with waw consecutive commonly show affixed ָה-. This is a late usage which became standard in the Hebrew of Qumran (see Qimron, 1986, #310.122, and Revell, 1988).

23.2 Imperfect forms in clause-initial position are regularly short, affixed, or neutral; those used within clauses are regularly long or neutral. However this formal contrast does not mark any semantic difference, as is shown by the fact that second person imperfect forms may begin clauses (Dan. 9:25, 12:13, also Neh. 9:28 in a past context).26 There are some exceptions. Affixed first person forms fail to occur where expected in Neh. 2:18, 5:12, 6:10, 10:32 (cf. #15.1). A long 3rd person imperfect form begins a clause which is closely related to the preceding in Dan. 12:12 אשרי המחכה ויגיע ‘Blessed be he who waits and reaches...’; also Dan. 12:4; and, in a past context, Neh. 3:14. Presumably the close semantic relationship (possibly reflected in intonational contours) shows that the verb is indicative (see #15.3, 4). The same usage may account for the long qere form in Dan. 11:10 ישב ויתגרה ‘He will return and wage war’. A long clause-initial imperfect occurs where no such relationship obtains in Dan. 12:13 (2m.s.) and Neh. 3:15 (qere, 3m.s., with past value). However a long form is used after אל in Neh. 3:37 where it does not begin the clause.

An imperative follows a jussive (marked by following נא) in Neh. 1:11. A jussive (marked by אל) follows an imperative in Neh. 3:37, as does a neutral imperfect in Dan. 1:12. The perfect with waw consecutive is not used in sequence with imperatives.

23.4 (Apart from use after imperatives) the waw consecutive perfect and the imperfect with conjunctive waw are free variants in clause initial position, as can be seen from the mixture of forms in sequence in Dan. 11:5, 7, 10, 15, etc.27

It has been shown above that the semantic fields of the two indicative categories overlap in a number of cases, so that either can be used to represent an action where such overlap occurs. The categories are, however, distinct in meaning; each carries connotations different from the other. Consequently, the possibility of choosing either category in such a situation allows the narrator to extend his range of expression beyond the limitations of morphology, and present a range of temporal or aspectual nuances comparable to that achieved in Indo-European languages through the greater variety of forms. The use of YQTL forms in sequence with modals, or in other situations in which they appear to present commands, etc. is an example of the same phenomenon. The fact that modal and indicative forms are occasionally used in the corpus without appreciable difference in meaning may reflect the beginning of the development which led to the complete loss of distinction between them, but not more than the beginning. The indicative and modal categories in the corpus are no less distinct in meaning than are the QTL and YQTL categories.

24.2 The distinction of the modal or indicative value of neutral imperfect forms by their position in the clause must have been a firmly fixed feature of the language, since it still governs the position in which short and affixed imperfect forms, or long imperfect forms, are used in Daniel, Ezra, and Nehemiah, even though the forms do not differ in meaning. THE SYSTEM OF THE VERB IN STANDARD BIBLICAL PROSE

25. A verb can be marked as modal in the corpus by its form (imper- ative, or short or affixed imperfect), by its position (initial in its clause) or by co-occurrence (with following נא or preceding אל). Any one of these marks will serve to identify it, but it may have two, or even three of them. A YQTL indicative is marked by a waw consecutive perfect form in clause-initial position, or by an imperfect form (not in co-occurrence with נא or אל) within a clause.

25.1 It is possible that the different modal forms in Hebrew are ves- tiges of more complete “imperative”, and “short”, and “affixed” imper- fect paradigms, each of which had a distinct function. In standard He- brew prose, however, they form a single set of modal forms, and in many cases the same “neutral” imperfect form is used for both modal and indicative functions. Due to the relation between the significance of a constituent and its position in the clause (see #17), verbs with modal value typically stood in clause-initial position. Consequently indicative imperfect forms came to be restricted to position within the clause, so that the value of a neutral imperfect was marked as modal if it began the clause, as indicative if it did not (unless it was marked as modal by a particle). The waw consecutive perfect (the origin of which is not known) came to be used where a YQTL indicative verb was required in clause-initial position, and so as a positional variant of the indicative imperfect. While this pattern continued to govern the positions in which the modal and indicative forms of the imperfect were used, the differ- ence in their values was lost, so that the imperative eventually became the only distinct modal form, as in Mishanic Hebrew. This development may be foreshadowed in the partial blurring of the distinction between the values of modal and indicative forms in the corpus. It was possibly influenced, in its later stages, by the vernacular language. The devel- opment resulted in the abandonment of the features which had distin- guished modal imperfects from YQTL indicatives, including the use of the waw consecutive perfect, because it took away their function. 26. A QTL indicative is represented in the corpus by a perfect form within a clause, or at the beginning of a clause where the conjunction waw is not used. Where the conjunction is used, the QTL indicative is typically represented by the waw consecutive imperfect. This form derives from the original “preterite”, which early became restricted, in prose, to initial position in a clause which was part of a sequence, or was presented as such, and so began with a conjunction. Here it acts as a positional variant of the perfect, and functions to indicate the structure of narrative passages. The perfect form with a prefixed conjunction represents QTL in a past context in a number of cases in which the action is considered as identical with, or as a facet of, that of the preceding clause. This is distinct from the waw consecutive use of the perfect (which is restricted to a present/future context) and is common enough in the corpus that it can be regarded as a feature of standard usage. It is possible that, in an earlier phase, the waw consecutive imperfect was the only representative of QTL used in clause-initial position with a prefixed conjunction, and that this use of the perfect was the first symptom of the change which eventually led to the complete replacement of the waw consecutive imperfect by the perfect. However this may be, the waw consecutive imperfect appears to have been used according to the standard pattern throughout the biblical period and also in the literary language of Qumran. In 1QIsaa its replacement by the perfect is rare in comparison to the number of cases in which the waw consecutive perfect is replaced by the imperfect (see Kutscher, 1974:354–5, 357–8).

27. The use of verbs in the corpus is, then, a complex, but generally consistent system representing a more or less stable stage of usage characteristic of the prose narrative of a particular period. Since it is a stage in continuing development, it is not surprising that the usage is not entirely consistent. Possibly some of the unusual features found in it are vestiges of the usage of earlier stages, restricted, like English subjunctives, to particular contexts. Others may well be early examples of usage which became standard in the succeeding stage. No doubt some of the anomalies it shows arose from errors in transmission. The standard pattern of the usage is, however, clear. The most interesting feature brought out by this survey is the grammatical function of the waw consecutive perfect, which marks a clause initial verb as a YQTL indicative.
