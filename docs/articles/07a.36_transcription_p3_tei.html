The System of the Verb in Standard Biblical Prose

E.J. Revell
University of Toronto

This article surveys the system of the verb in the Former Prophets starting from the premises (advanced by others) that there are two indicative “tenses”, each represented by a “simple” and a “consecutive” form in complementary distribution, and one modal system, represented by the imperative, jussive and cohortative forms, also in complementary distribution. The main thesis is that the indicative and modal systems are fully independent because (1) imperfect forms with modal value are distinguished from indicatives by their position in the clause, (2) a perfect form with waw “consecutive” following a modal has a value different from that of a modal in the same position. The article begins with a survey of the indicative system intended to set this thesis in its proper context by showing that (1) the value of any verb form depends heavily on its syntactic/semantic context, (2) despite the distinct values of the “tenses”, in many contexts either may be used to represent the same occurrence — the choice is determined by the viewpoint of the author, or by the particular implications he intends to convey. The article concludes with a survey of the way this system developed to the end of the Biblical period.

1.1 The basic system of the verb in standard biblical prose is best described as using a set of indicative forms representing two categories — here called QTL and YQTL — and a set of modal forms representing one category. The various verb forms are used in this system as follows:

THE USE OF INDICATIVES

2.1 The meaning carried by the opposition of the two categories of the indicative, QTL and YQTL, has been much debated. It seems to me that it is most easily presented as one of time reference: QTL 'past' versus YQTL 'present/future'. While the ease of such presentation is no doubt partly due to the fact that English uses a tense system, there is no real reason to suppose that Hebrew does not.

  • (a) The fact that forms typically representing past time may represent future, and vice versa, under particular conditions, does not show that the basic system is not one of tense (pace Driver, 1892, #1). The same phenomenon occurs in English, as in the examples in Lyons (1986), p. 239.
  • (b) The criterion by which the value of a form must be judged is not its historical origin, but its use within the corpus. No matter what the origin of the waw consecutive verb forms, those which occur in the corpus must be regarded as syntactically conditioned variants of the perfect and imperfect forms. This removes any obstacle to considering the categories they represent as tense categories, as argued in Blau, 1970.
  • (c) The Hebrew of post-biblical times certainly used a tense system, and the same is widely assumed for the ancestor of the biblical language, as vestigially represented by the 'preterite' (or waw consecutive) use of the imperfect form. It seems likely, a priori, that the system of the intervening period would also have been one of tense. The QTL:YQTL

2.2 It is important to note that the time reference of the two categories in relation to the speaker/narrator is not absolute, but is conditioned by the time reference of the context in which the verb form is used. This, like the categories themselves, can be categorized as ‘past’ or ‘present/future’.6

THE USE OF INDICATIVE FORMS: QTL

3.1 QTL forms characteristically present an event which has occurred in the past from the point of view of the speaker or narrator, as in

  • Judg. 1:7 כאשר עשיתי כן שלם לי אלהים ‘As I have done, so God has repaid me.’ As this example shows, a QTL form may represent a single event, as שלם, or events which were repeated, habitual, or continuous over a period, as עשיתי (as noted in Blau, 1976, #59). Such durative reference is sometimes made explicit by an adverbial, as in
  • 2 Sam. 16:23 ועצת אחיתפל אשר יעץ בימים ההם ‘The advice of Ahitophel which he used to give (perfect) in those days,’ or by a parallel clause, as in
  • 1 Kgs. 6:27 ... ותגע כנף האחד בקיר וכנף הכרוב השני נגעת ‘The wing of one cherub touched (waw consecutive imperfect) the wall, while the wing of the other cherub touched (participle) the other wall.’

3.2 The time reference of a QTL form may be past with reference to a present context, as that of the speaker in the example from Judg. 1:7, or that of the narrator in the example from 1 Kgs. 6:27. It may equally be past with reference to a past context, as is common in subordinate clauses in narrative, as

  • Judg. 1:20 ויתנו לכלב את חברון כאשר דבר משה ‘They gave Hebron to Caleb, as Moses (had) said.’

3.3 A QTL form may also represent the past relative to a future context, also usually in subordinate clauses, as

3.4 As the translations show, English can use verbal phrases which accurately reflect these different temporal relationships. Where the context is future, however, a simple present form ‘unless I tell you,’ ‘the land to which they are carried’ would be more usual, and a simple past form ‘I did’ would be adequate in most cases where the context is present or past. The difference between the usages possible in English is basically social. The use of more or less precise forms would carry information about the educational background of the speaker, or the social context of the speech. Similar differences are possible in Hebrew. An action which is past in relation to a future event may be represented by a QTL form, as in the examples noted above. It may also be represented by a YQTL form, as

  • Judg. 6:26 והעלית עולה בעצי האשרה אשר תכרת ‘You will send up an offering with the wood of the Asherah which you will (have) cut down.’
It is at least reasonable to suggest that the verb form is chosen to reflect one of the possible views of the temporal relationship of the event. A YQTL form shows that it is perceived in relation to the speaker, and so as future; a QTL form shows that it is perceived in relation to its context, and so as past. The choice of forms could no doubt be similarly justified if the QTL:YQTL contrast were explained in terms of ‘complete:incomplete,’ or ‘punctual:durative’ action. What is significant for this paper is the fact that the choice of verb forms does not depend wholly on the time or nature of the action. Where the action can be understood as belonging to either of the contrasting categories, either form can be used, according to the impression of the time or of the nature of the action that the speaker wishes to convey.

4.1 In a present/future context, a QTL form may be used to represent an event which was initiated in the speaker’s past, but continues in the present. This is usual with verbs representing a condition, quality or state, where English would use a present form, as in Judg. 16:15 אהבתיך ‘I love you,’ Judg. 18:23 נזעקת ‘(What is your problem that) you are mustered?,’ and with special cases like היה ‘be,’ ידע ‘know’ and יכל ‘be able.’

QTL forms used in this way are sometimes called ‘performatives’: i.e. “utterances which themselves describe the speech act which they perform” (Leech, 1981, p. 322). Talstra (1982, col. 28), adapting Leech’s definition of the English usage to Hebrew, argues that the ‘perfect tense’ is characteristic of the equivalent usage in Hebrew. The resulting definition of the Hebrew performative is perfectly acceptable, but (like that of Leech for English) is very narrow. The case in 1 Kgs. 3:12 (which is translated as a performative) is the only one in the above set of examples which clearly fits it. In Judg. 1:2, the pronoun in בידו might well be regarded as the indirect object of נתתי. If this is the case, this example must be excluded along with the others. Yet the basis for the use of the perfect form would appear to be the same in all the examples. Consequently, there seems no reason to accept the claim in Talstra, 1982, col. 27, that “performative speech in Hebrew should not be explained as an aspect of any ‘basic function’ of the perfect tense.” The performative use of QTL forms is simply a subclass of the category described in this section, just as performative usage is a subclass of the use of the simple present form in English.

THE USE OF INDICATIVE FORMS: YQTL

8. In terms of tense, YQTL forms refer to the present or future. Thus, in quoted speech, in a present context,

Judg. 1:1–2 מי יעלה ... יהודה יעלה ‘Who will go up? ... Judah will go up.’ Where the context is past, a YQTL form may represent an event which is present or future relative to the past context, as

Judg. 17:8 וילך האיש... לגור באשר ימצא ‘The man went to live where he could find a place.’ The English translation represents the different time reference of the two verbs by the use of the modal ‘could’ for the

second, but it sees both actions from the viewpoint of the speaker, as past. The Hebrew represents the second action from the viewpoint of the first. If the first action were future, the second Hebrew verb would not change in form, because the relationship of the two actions to each other would still be the same. However, the English translation must change to ‘He will go to live where he will/can find a place’ to indicate the different temporal relationship of the second action to the speaker.

9. YQTL forms, like QTL, are used both for single actions, as in the examples in Judg. 1:1-2, and for actions which are repeated, habitual, or constant. Thus, in a present or future context,

Judg. 13:14 כל אשר יצא מגפן היין ‘Everything which comes from the grape vine.’

2 Kgs. 9:20 כי בשגעון ינהג ‘He drives furiously.’ Other examples occur in Judg. 16:15; 1 Sam. 5:5, 21:12; 2 Sam. 11:20, 17:12; 2 Kgs. 18:32; and elsewhere. In a past context, such verbs present actions as repeated or habitual in the past, as in

Judg. 2:18-19 וכי הקים יהוה להם שפטים והיה יהוה עם השפט והושיעם ... כי ינחם The verbs of an English translation must indicate the fact that the events are past in relation to the speaker. ‘When the Lord had raised up a judge for them, he used to support the judge, and to rescue them, because he was moved by their pleas.’ If the introductory clause provided a present or future context, the following YQTL forms would be translated in English as present or future: ‘When the Lord raises ... he supports ...’ or ‘When the Lord will raise ... he will support ...’ In Hebrew, the future context would be indicated by the change of the initial verb to יקים, but the other verbs would not change, since the YQTL forms used for these verbs reflect the relation of the actions they represent to the context marked by the introductory verb, and this relationship does not change. Other examples occur in Judg. 6:4, 10:4, 11:40, 12:5, 14:10, etc. Where the context is past, QTL forms can be used instead of YQTL to represent these habitual actions (see #4, and cf. e.g. Judg. 2:18-19 with Judg. 3:6). As in the categories described in #4, #5, and #6, the form used is presumably chosen according to the particular implications which the speaker or narrator wishes to convey.

THE IDENTIFICATION OF MODAL FORMS


13. The modal system is represented by imperatives, used in affirm-
ative clauses with second person subject, and by the forms of the im-
perfect known as “jussive” and “cohortative” used where the clause is
negative, or where the subject is first or third person. Distinct modal
imperfect forms occur only where the subject is first person, 2m.s., or
3m./f.s. Any original difference between the modal and the indicative
forms of the imperfect has been lost where the subject is 2f.s., 2.pl, or
3.pl. (On forms with suffixes, see #14.3.) First person modal forms show
the affix ָה-; the corresponding imperfect forms do not. Forms from

MODAL VERBS AND THE waw consecutive perfect

17. The material in #15–16, which lists the cases in which the use of imperfect forms marked as modal or indicative appears to be inconsistent with the conclusion reached in #14, provides no serious reason to doubt that conclusion. Consequently it can be stated that an imperfect form in the corpus, which begins its clause has modal value; an imperfect form which stands within its clause (and is not preceded by אל or followed by נא) has indicative value. Clearly, then, the waw consecutive perfect could only be a free variant of the modal imperfect, since it also begins its clause. It could, however, be a conditioned variant of the indicative imperfect; that is, it could have the grammatical function of representing a YQTL indicative verb in clause-initial position. That this would be a useful function is obvious. The main factor conditioning word order in biblical Hebrew is ‘thematization’.20 The components of a clause are arranged in the order of their significance for the speaker or narrator. Modal verbs, which present commands, requests, etc. are typically the most significant component of their clause, and so stand first in it. However the significance of other verbs also often requires that they stand first in their clauses, particularly where sequences of events are depicted. Hence the need to distinguish modal from indicative verbs. The fact that the waw consecutive perfect is used to distinguish a clause-initial verb as a YQTL indicative is suggested by the following considerations.

17.1 In conditional sentences in which the apodosis begins with a waw consecutive perfect verb, the only function of the particle waw is to mark

the perfect form as ‘consecutive’. An apodosis which does not begin with a waw consecutive perfect is typically not introduced by a conjunction.21

17.2 A waw consecutive perfect form follows an imperative in only about a quarter of the possible cases in the corpus, and follows a modal imperfect in little more than a tenth.22 In sharp contrast, clause initial verbs which carry on a sequence of indicatives are regularly waw consecutive in form. A waw consecutive perfect following a modal verb cannot, then, stand in the same sort of relationship to it as it does to a preceding indicative. Consequently it is reasonable to argue that the waw consecutive perfect represents the YQTL indicative as a conditioned variant of the imperfect, as suggested in #1. If this view is correct, a waw consecutive perfect following a modal verb must have connotations different from those of a modal verb in the same position. A survey of the use of the waw consecutive perfect suggests that this is indeed the case.

19.5 The waw consecutive perfect is translated as an imperative in accordance with English usage in this case, as in the others, but the use of this form shows that the verb is a YQTL indicative, with connotations different from those carried by an imperative or other modal form. A modal form is used to present a forceful command, whether its force reflects the fact that, although not first in a series of commands, it is of major significance, or reflects the urgency of the situation, or the social or moral status of the speaker relative to the person addressed. The waw consecutive perfect (a YQTL indicative form) is used in the same context to present a command which is less forceful, whether the (relative) lack of force reflects the fact that the action to be taken is a later consequence of action already forcefully commanded, or that it is not urgent, or that there is no wish to mark any difference of social or moral status between the speaker and the person addressed. It must be admitted (with Joüon, 1947, 301) that the different values of the waw consecutive perfect and imperative following an imperative are not always significant in the contexts in which they are used. Similarly, in English, the four possible variations of ‘I sang/was singing as I walked/was walking’ could be used in the same context with no significant difference of connotation. That does not mean, however, that the two past forms have the same meaning. So also the simple equation “after an imperative, waw consecutive perfect = imperative” is an oversimplification; it hides the function of the waw consecutive perfect as presenting a clause-initial indicative, with its particular connotations. Just as with QTL and YQTL forms, which are typically used in different contexts as having distinct values, and which present different connotations in those contexts in which either can be used, so with the two forms with present/future value: modal and YQTL indicative. 21. As is well known, לא with the imperfect is used in early materials to present a particularly forceful prohibition.25 However it is obvious that, both in early materials and in the corpus, לא with an imperfect can present a statement, as well as a command. In

The translation of one verb as a command and the other as a statement is a matter of English usage, reflecting an understanding of the content of the clauses and their context. There is nothing in the form of the verbs or their syntax which requires translation in either way. Similar usage is found in Akkadian, where la taparras can present either a command or a prediction. The same is true of ‘you will not do’ in English. In the latter case, the two values are distinguished by intonation patterns, as well as by the context in which they are used. In written texts, only the latter criterion is available. As for the relative forcefulness of the indicative and imperative patterns, this depends on the conventions of the users. My own impression is that in English, as in Hebrew, the statement ‘You will not do’ typically presents a more forceful prohibition than the imperative ‘do not’, whereas the imperative ‘do’ presents a more urgent command than the statement ‘you will do’.

HISTORICAL CONSIDERATIONS

23. The above survey shows that a plausible case can be made in support of the contention that waw consecutive perfect forms represent clause-initial YQTL verbs, and that indicative verbs carry a meaning distinct from that of modals. That this case is valid, and not merely plausible, can be argued from the changes which occurred in the verb system of later Biblical Hebrew, as shown in the books of Daniel, Ezra, and Nehemiah. The use of verbs in these books can be briefly described as follows.

23.1 The use of QTL forms does not differ significantly from that of standard prose, but there is a morphological innovation. First person imperfect forms with waw consecutive commonly show affixed ־ה. This is a late usage which became standard in the Hebrew of Qumran (see Qimron, 1986, #310.122, and Revell, 1988).

[Page 30]

30 E. J. REVELL [30]

23.2 Imperfect forms in clause-initial position are regularly short, affixed, or neutral; those used within clauses are regularly long or neutral. However this formal contrast does not mark any semantic difference, as is shown by the fact that second person imperfect forms may begin clauses (Dan. 9:25, 12:13, also Neh. 9:28 in a past context).26

23.3 The use of imperative forms follows the standard pattern, including that of the sequence of modals (#14.4). One imperative follows another in 22 cases. An imperative follows a jussive (marked by following נא) in Neh. 1:11. A jussive (marked by אל) follows an imperative in Neh. 3:37, as does a neutral imperfect in Dan. 1:12. The perfect with waw consecutive is not used in sequence with imperatives.

23.4 (Apart from use after imperatives) the waw consecutive perfect and the imperfect with conjunctive waw are free variants in clause initial position, as can be seen from the mixture of forms in sequence in Dan. 11:5, 7, 10, 15, etc.27

24.1 In the form of language represented by Daniel, Ezra, and Nehemiah, then, modal forms are still generally restricted to initial position

26
[Page 31]

in the clause, and indicative forms to medial position, but it is clear that the contrast in form and position no longer marks any semantic distinction. It could be argued that the anomalies listed in #16.3, 4, show that the language of the corpus was already close to this situation, and that the different connotations ascribed to modal and indicative forms in many other cases are illusory. Thus שוב עמי ואשתחוה (with an imperfect in modal position 1 Sam. 15:25) and שוב עמי והשתחויתי (with a YQTL indicative, 1 Sam. 15:30) could carry exactly the same message ‘Return with me and I will worship’ (although they could show a shift from modal to indicative to express greater politeness/humility; see #20, especially Judg. 6:17 in #20:2). However the fact that different grammatical categories may, in some cases, be used without distinction does not show that they are never distinct. The difference of implication exists potentially in all cases, even though it may not be significant in all.

It has been shown above that the semantic fields of the two indicative categories overlap in a number of cases, so that either can be used to represent an action where such overlap occurs. The categories are, however, distinct in meaning; each carries connotations different from the other. Consequently, the possibility of choosing either category in such a situation allows the narrator to extend his range of expression beyond the limitations of morphology, and present a range of temporal or aspectual nuances comparable to that achieved in Indo-European languages through the greater variety of forms. The use of YQTL forms in sequence with modals, or in other situations in which they appear to present commands, etc. is an example of the same phenomenon. The fact that modal and indicative forms are occasionally used in the corpus without appreciable difference in meaning may reflect the beginning of the development which led to the complete loss of distinction between them, but not more than the beginning. The indicative and modal categories in the corpus are no less distinct in meaning than are the QTL and YQTL categories.

24.2 The distinction of the modal or indicative value of neutral imperfect forms by their position in the clause must have been a firmly fixed feature of the language, since it still governs the position in which short and affixed imperfect forms, or long imperfect forms, are used in Daniel, Ezra, and Nehemiah, even though the forms do not differ in meaning. The use of verbs in these three books also differs from that standard in the corpus in some other respects. The waw consecutive perfect is not used after imperatives. Elsewhere, it is a free variant of clause-initial imperfects, and so has no special function. Second person

[Page 32]

imperfects are used in clause-initial position, and an indicative imperfect form may be used where an imperfect is medial in a semantic unit, even where it is initial in a clause. These features suggest a considerable development from the pattern standard in the corpus towards the usage characteristic of Mishnaic Hebrew. It seems clear that a major cause of this development was the loss of distinction between the modal and indicative values of the imperfect.

THE SYSTEM OF THE VERB IN STANDARD BIBLICAL PROSE

25. A verb can be marked as modal in the corpus by its form (imperative, or short or affixed imperfect), by its position (initial in its clause) or by co-occurrence (with following נא or preceding אל). Any one of these marks will serve to identify it, but it may have two, or even three of them. A YQTL indicative is marked by a waw consecutive perfect form in clause-initial position, or by an imperfect form (not in co-occurrence with נא or אל) within a clause.

25.1 It is possible that the different modal forms in Hebrew are vestiges of more complete “imperative”, and “short”, and “affixed” imperfect paradigms, each of which had a distinct function. In standard Hebrew prose, however, they form a single set of modal forms, and in many cases the same “neutral” imperfect form is used for both modal and indicative functions. Due to the relation between the significance of a constituent and its position in the clause (see #17), verbs with modal value typically stood in clause-initial position. Consequently indicative imperfect forms came to be restricted to position within the clause, so that the value of a neutral imperfect was marked as modal if it began the clause, as indicative if it did not (unless it was marked as modal by a particle). The waw consecutive perfect (the origin of which is not known) came to be used where a YQTL indicative verb was required in clause-initial position, and so as a positional variant of the indicative imperfect. While this pattern continued to govern the positions in which the modal and indicative forms of the imperfect were used, the difference in their values was lost, so that the imperative eventually became the only distinct modal form, as in Mishanic Hebrew. This development may be foreshadowed in the partial blurring of the distinction between the values of modal and indicative forms in the corpus. It was possibly influenced, in its later stages, by the vernacular language. The development resulted in the abandonment of the features which had distinguished modal imperfects from YQTL indicatives, including the use of the waw consecutive perfect, because it took away their function. This

[Page 33]

process is quite advanced in the language of Qumran (see Qimron, 1986, #400.03*; Kutscher, 1974:357–8).

26. A QTL indicative is represented in the corpus by a perfect form within a clause, or at the beginning of a clause where the conjunction waw is not used. Where the conjunction is used, the QTL indicative is typically represented by the waw consecutive imperfect. This form derives from the original “preterite”, which early became restricted, in prose, to initial position in a clause which was part of a sequence, or was presented as such, and so began with a conjunction. Here it acts as a positional variant of the perfect, and functions to indicate the structure of narrative passages. The perfect form with a prefixed conjunction represents QTL in a past context in a number of cases in which the action is considered as identical with, or as a facet of, that of the preceding clause. This is distinct from the waw consecutive use of the perfect (which is restricted to a present/future context) and is common enough in the corpus that it can be regarded as a feature of standard usage. It is possible that, in an earlier phase, the waw consecutive imperfect was the only representative of QTL used in clause-initial position with a prefixed conjunction, and that this use of the perfect was the first symptom of the change which eventually led to the complete replacement of the waw consecutive imperfect by the perfect. However this may be, the waw consecutive imperfect appears to have been used according to the standard pattern throughout the biblical period and also in the literary language of Qumran. In 1QIsaa its replacement by the perfect is rare in comparison to the number of cases in which the waw consecutive perfect is replaced by the imperfect (see Kutscher, 1974:354–5, 357–8).

27. The use of verbs in the corpus is, then, a complex, but generally consistent system representing a more or less stable stage of usage characteristic of the prose narrative of a particular period. Since it is a stage in continuing development, it is not surprising that the usage is not entirely consistent. Possibly some of the unusual features found in it are vestiges of the usage of earlier stages, restricted, like English subjunctives, to particular contexts. Others may well be early examples of usage which became standard in the succeeding stage. No doubt some of the anomalies it shows arose from errors in transmission. The standard pattern of the usage is, however, clear. The most interesting feature brought out by this survey is the grammatical function of the waw consecutive perfect, which marks a clause initial verb as a YQTL indicative.
