FIRST PERSON IMPERFECT FORMS WITH WAW CONSECUTIVE

by

E. J. REVELL

Toronto

First person imperfect forms with waw consecutive have recently received some attention in connection with the question of the authorship of the books of Ezra, Nehemiah, and Chronicles.1 A few details of these forms were overlooked in that discussion. It seems worth while to present these, in case similar interest in these forms is aroused in some other connection.2 Forms with pronominal suffixes, which show no variations that might give clues to date or authorship, are not included in the following description.

(1) Where alef is the final root letter, the same 3 ms. form is regularly used with waw consecutive as without. This is also true for 1st person forms in 56 cases (including the qere in Josh. xxiv 8). The four exceptional forms, wʾšlḥh (Neh. ii 13), wʾqrʾh, (1 Sam. xxviii 15), and the ketib forms wʾbʾh (Josh. xxiv 8), and wʾmṣʾh (Ezra viii 17), all appear to reflect the use of the affix -h. This affixed form of the imperfect thus occurs in waw consecutive forms from roots with final alef much less frequently than in forms from any other roots except those with final he. This is to be expected, since this use of the affix is late (see below), and the differences between the two types of final weak root had been lost by the end of the biblical period.3

The use of the affix -āh in categories (3) and (4) is common only in later materials. It occurs in 61 % of the possible cases in Daniel, Ezra and Nehemiah (55 of 90 cases), compared to about 4 % in the Pentateuch (4/92), and the Latter Prophets (6/154). This use of the affix -āh on 1st person forms with waw consecutive is characteristic of a post-exilic form of literary (biblical) Hebrew which became standard at Qumran, and influenced the Samaritan Pentateuch much more strongly than it did that of the MT.7 Ben Hayyim has connected this usage with a tendency to use the affixed form without waw consecutive (the “cohortative”) with time reference other than future.8 Of the examples of the usage that he gives, however, only that in Ps. lxvi 6 is atypical for an imperfect form, and in Daniel, Ezra and Nehemiah the use of the affixed

The use of affixed 1st person forms with waw consecutive represents, then, a syntactic or semantic development which was standardized in a post-exilic form of literary Hebrew. It is possible that some parts of the Bible were written in a Hebrew of this sort in which the standardization of this usage was either less complete than at Qumran, or has been obscured by later change. In the book of Nehemiah, however, the use of these forms distinguishes sections different from those of the usual literary division based on content and style. If the latter division is justified, the use of affixed 1st person forms with waw consecutive shown in the MT must have arisen during the process of transmission. This conclusion must apply to the use of these forms throughout the Bible. They cannot be taken, in any book, as certainly representing the language of the author.

The long form of the 1st person imperfect from roots IIIh is regularly used where a cohortative form is expected. The use of long 1st person forms from these roots with waw consecutive is more common in later materials, and shows a distribution generally similar to that of affixed 1st person forms from other roots with waw consecutive. Such long forms are not used with waw consecutive in any of the five possible cases in Neh. ii 10-20 and iv. They are used in one of two cases in Neh. i 1-ii 9, and in all four cases in the rest of the book. They occur in 1/2 cases in Judges, in all five in 2 Samuel, in 2/3 cases in Ps. lxix (there are no cases in the other Psalms listed above) and in 1/2 cases in the poetry of Job. Consequently the use of the long form of the 1st person imperfect with waw consecutive in roots IIIh can reasonably be regarded as representing the same development as does the use of the affixed form in other roots. The use of long 1st person forms is, however, much more common generally than is that of affixed forms. Long

It seems clear that the stress patterns of these forms reflect a natural phonological development in which stress position was conditioned by syllable structure. The exception in Neh. iv 3 forms a negligible proportion even of 1st person forms. The vowel patterns suggest a correspondingly regular development. The vowel of a final closed syllable in these forms originally acted as short. In hollow root forms, and in hifʿîl forms from other roots, the vowel of this syllable changed in quality in all forms but those in which both penultimate and antepenultimate syllable were open (with the few exceptions noted). Where the vowel of this final closed syllable was stressed u or i, it was, in later times, often marked with a vowel letter, but there is no way of knowing to what extent the use of these letters in any part of the MT represents that of the work in its original form.

The use, in category (3), of long first person singular forms with waw consecutive is thus due to a phonological development quite distinct from the syntactic/semantic development which led to the use of affixed first person forms, both singular and plural, in categories (1), (3), and (4), and most probably also the use of long first person forms, also both singular and plural, in category (2). Both developments appear to have occurred towards the end of the biblical period, but there is no adequate basis for dating them either absolutely or relative to each other. The lack of relationship between the two developments is made clear (apart from the fact that both occur in category (3)) by the occurrence of two exceptional long 1st person forms with waw consecutive in Neh. iv 3 and iv 9, in a passage characterized by the failure to use affixed forms. Less striking confirmation is provided by the regular use of vowel letters in the final closed syllable of forms from category (3) in 1 Samuel (x 18, xii 1, xxviii 21) where an affixed form occurs in only one of twelve possible cases (or 4/15 if long IIIh forms are included).

This survey has, then, the negative merit of stressing the dangers of using 1st person forms with waw consecutive as criteria of authorship. The use of affixed forms cannot be attributed with any confidence to any biblical author. The use of long 1 s. forms from hollow roots (and others in category (3)) was characteristic of later biblical Hebrew generally. 27 Stress position in perfect forms with waw consecutive is, then, conditioned by the intonation patterns characteristic of the speech units into which the text was divided (in one form of the reading tradition) according to the syntactic, (other) semantic, and rhythmic factors described. Whether or not these intonation patterns induce final stress in a verb form at a given position in a unit is determined by the phonological structure of the unit. Because our information comes from a written source which does not represent these intonation patterns (see #12), it is not possible to provide a comprehensive, precise, and objective description of the boundaries of the units, let alone a description of their structure. However, the relation of stress position to the syntactic, semantic, and rhythmic factors is sufficiently clear that there can be no doubt that the variation in stress position in these forms represents one of the many facets of the influence of these intonation patterns on the vowelling and stress position of the words of biblical Hebrew.
