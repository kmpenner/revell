CONCORD WITH COMPOUND SUBJECTS AND RELATED USES OF PRONOUNS

Introduction

  1. This study is based on the relatively homogeneous corpus of narrative formed by the books of Judges, Samuel, and Kings. The study of compound subjects in this corpus is restricted to those representing human beings, and to those of which one component is singular. Where a compound (as defined here) acts as subject of a verbal clause, the verb may precede or follow the compound sub­ ject; it may be singular or plural in either position. It is argued here that the position of the verb, and its number, is deliberately chosen in accordance with the function of the clause, and of its sub­ ject, in the narrative context. That is, this article is based on the view that concord in Biblical Hebrew is not simply a means of marking grammatical relationship. Where variation is possible, it is used in a manner similar to the use of the pronouns “tu” and “vous” in French. The variations, contrasting with the simple grammatical basis of concord, are used to add expressive content to the narrative.

  1. The choice of number in the verb is usually seen as governed by its position relative to the compound subject. However, this fac­ tor does not govern concord in other cases in which choice of number (as with collective subjects) or choice of person (as in deferential speech) is available. Grammars point out that a verb preceding a feminine or plural subject may be masculine singular in form, but it seems unlikely that this phenomenon has any rele­ vance for compound subjects as defined here. Levi suggests that a verb with a following compound subject is likely to be plural where an object or adverbial precedes it, or stands between it and its sub­ ject, but these features can have little significance. In Judges ix, where closely similar clauses differ in separating the verb from the following subject, singular verbs are used in both (ix 32, 34, quoted in §10). In 1 Sam. xxviii 1, the verb is both preceded and followed by an adverbial, but there, too, it is singular. There is, then, no strong reason to believe that the choice of number in a verb is deter­ mined by its position relative to a compound subject.

The singular verb emphasizes the fact that David is principal actor. It is probably true to say that, in a narrative clause of this sort, the use of the singular is “unmarked”; a singular verb is used unless the information provided by the plural is required. Even so, the use of the singular can still carry the meaning suggested. The use of vous in addressing someone superior in age or status does not lose its connotations of deference because it is “unmarked” in the situa­ tion. A plural verb could be used before a compound subject; the use of the singular represents a choice. This choice emphasizes the fact that the initial component of the compound is principal actor, a fact already shown by the structure of the compound.10 The par­ ticular significance of such emphasis is shown by the context. It is often continued by the use, in following clauses, of singular subject pronouns, co-referent with the compound (see below §22).
