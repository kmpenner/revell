THE OCCURRENCE OF PAUSAL FORMS

EJ. Revell

UNIVERSITY OF TORONTO

Abstract

The common opinion maintains that the accents determine the occur­ rence of pausal forms, despite various problems with this view. E.g. any disjunctive accent may be marked on a pausal form, and a few conjunc­ tives are also so marked. Moreover, it is easy to find both contextual and pausal forms of a word marked with the same accent. Syntactic (or more correctly, semantic) structure provides a solider theoretical base for the development of pausal forms than do the accents, a base with fewer problems. The vast majority of pausal forms stand at the end of a clause. The remainder (some 15%) stand within a clause in equally standardized positions, mostly at the main division of the clause as marked by the accents. Consequently, it should be agreed that the occurrence of pausal forms is determined by the syntactic/ semantic units making up the text.

  1. Pausal, Contextual and Neutral Forms

A pausal form is a form of a word used in particular positions, which differs in features of vowel pattern and/or stress position from the (generally) more common contextual form which is used in other positions.1 Most words do not form contrasting pausal and contextual forms. Such words are referred to here as ‘neutral’ forms. A few words produce two different pausal forms contrasting with the contextual form: a ‘full’ pausal form which shows the full difference from the contextual form both in vowel pattern and in stress position, and a ‘minor’ pausal form which typically shows the pausal stress position, but the contextual vowel pattern.2

2 It appears that a minor pausal form reveals the pausal stress pattern induced by the position in which it stands, but that this position has not affected the vowel pattern as has occurred in the ‘full’ pausal form.

  1. Accents and Pausal Forms

This paper is mainly concerned with the twenty-one ‘prose’ books of the Masoretic Text of the Hebrew Bible.3 There are different opinions on the reasons why pausal forms are generated in the particular positions in which they occur in these books. The most common view attributes the occurrence of a pausal form to the accent with which it is marked.4 I assume that this view derives directly from the simplified description commonly used in earlier times: pausal forms occur with atnah and silluq and sometimes with zaqef However, attention to the details of the text shows clearly that this is simply a generalization. It ignores details important for the understanding of the occurrence of pausal forms.

Atnah and Silluq are regularly marked on Pausal forms, but also

occasionally appear on contextual forms.5

Any disjunctive accent may be marked on a pausal form: see note 13, below. Conjunctive accents are also sporadically marked on such forms. It is easy enough to find the same accent marked either on the pausal form, or on the contextual form, of the same word.6 Thus zaqef is often marked on the pausal form רמאיו in Gen. 18:29, 20:4, 28:17, etc., and on the corresponding contextual form רמאיו in Gen. 3:11, 9:26, Exod. 4:1, etc. Some explanation of these problems is needed if the view that pausal forms are induced by individual accents is to be maintained/ This has not been provided. Consequently it is assumed here that no pausal form is induced by the accent which is marked on it.8

  1. Accents and Vowelling in General

Dresher (1994) argues that the accent system has a prosodic basis. Within this, pausal forms mark intonational phrases. Thus far the argument is convincing. However, Dresher goes on to state that the pausal forms which end these intonational phrases are marked by disjunctive accents, which show that the word marked ‘is final in its phrase’ (Dresher 1994: 3). This is usual, but is not always true. Some 15 pausal forms are marked with conjunctive accents, which, in Dresher’s view, show that the word so marked ‘is in the same phrase as the word which follows it’ (ibid.). Consequently, the assumption that vowels and accents combine harmoniously in the marking of intonational phrases is invalid. This, and other examples, shows that the vowels and the accents of the Masoretic Text do not form a uni­ form, self-consistent system. Supporting examples are not difficult to find.9 Such examples are not numerous — there are only about 15 pausal forms marked with conjunctive accents out of some 7,000. However, these few cases do show that it cannot be assumed, as a matter of course, that the accentuation represents the relationship of words which gave rise to the vowelling.

  1. Pausal Forms and Clause Structure

It has also been suggested that pausal forms stand at the end of a clause, or of some other similarly important syntactic structure.10 This view is generally presented as a supplement to the common view, to be called on where a pausal form is not marked with one of the major accents, atnah, silluq, or others which may be considered their equivalents, and so also called ‘Caesars’, or ‘pausal accents’ (as Ben David 1995: §0.1, 5-7). Pausal forms delimit the intonational phrases made up of the semantic units of the text.   1. Pausal Forms within a Clause

Pausal forms which stand within clauses (a little over 900 in number, some 15% of the total) are used only in a limited range of positions, and are used sporadically rather than regularly. A pausal form is often used at the end of an item, or a group of items, in a list. A pausal form is sometimes used at the end of an Introduction to Speech’ (ISP, see below §9). Lists and ISPs constitute special situations gov­ erned by their own conventions. ‘Ordinary’ clauses, those which do not include lists or ISPs, are made up from a small number of con­ stituents: a verb, an adverb, an infinitival phrase, a nominal phrase acting as subject, as a term of address (vocative), as modifier, alone, or with an introductory preposition, as a prepositional phrase. Rela­ tive clauses are here considered to form part of the same constituent as the noun they modify. Other subordinate clauses are treated as independent. A pausal form within a clause most commonly stands before the terminal constituent. It may also stand at the end of the initial constituent of the clause, or before two or more constituents which end the clause. It rarely appears in other positions. In which­ ever of the three main positions a pausal form is used, it typically stands at the main division of the clause. That is, it is marked by the accent which marks the main division of the clause. The accent which marks a pausal form within a clause is most commonly atnah. Zaqef and tifha are also frequently used. Many of the other disjunctive accents sometimes mark a pausal form within a clause, although they are less commonly used.15 The following examples illustrate the way pausal forms are used within clauses.

  1. Pausal Forms in Clauses and Verses

It seems clear that the use of a pausal form within an ordinary clause is related to the use of such a form within a verse. Indeed, the two are often identical, as in Gen. 11:1, Deut. 6:9, 1 Chron. 29:26, etc. The pausal form typically marks the main division in the clause, just as it usually does in a verse. The pausal form marking the main divi- sion of an ordinary clause may be marked with a variety of accents, but most commonly a strong accent is used: atnah, zaqef, or tifha, accents which also mark a pausal form at the main division of a verse. However, a pausal form marking the main division of a clause may be marked with some other disjunctive accent. This makes it clear that the occurrence of a pausal form is not determined by the individual accent marked on it, but by its position in the language of the text. It is determined by the fact that it ends, or marks the main division of, a significant semantic or syntactic unit. A word does not appear in pausal form because it is marked by atnah or

  1. All Pausal Forms are Equally Significant

This survey has shown that the system of pausal forms is not simply a matter of their significant regular use when marked with strong accents, such as silluq and atnah, and their occasional, relatively unimportant, use elsewhere. In fact, pausal forms are used in a regular way, at the main division of a semantic unit, and at its end, in almost all cases. Thus, a pausal form is used at the end of a clause, unless that clause forms a semantic unit with one or more other clauses. It may also be used at the main division of a clause, and occasionally elsewhere within it. The use of pausal forms within clauses is sporadic in that it fails to occur in many clauses, but is regular in that (with rare exceptions), the pausal form is used at the main division of the clause, or to end some other significant semantic unit.   1. Conclusion

This survey can, then, conclude that the ocurrence of pausal forms is not determined by the accents which mark them, but by the syntac­ tic, or, more properly, the semantic structure of the text. Most pausal forms stand at the end of a clause. A proportion stand at the main division, or, occasionally, also at subsidiary divisions of a clause. These positions account not only for the greater number of pausal forms, which are marked with silluq and atnah, but also for the others, no less important, which may be marked with any other disjunctive accent. Pausal forms, then, mark the end and the middle of clauses in much the same way as they do the ends and middle of verses, and so can be said to mark the so-called dichotomy much as do the accents. This suggests that the tendency to mark ‘dichotomy’ was a feature of the Hebrew language, not simply of the biblical chant. It also suggests that the vowelling, or, some features of it, was fixed in the text before the accentuation. This is not to suggest that minor details of either system, such as conjunctive accents, and composite or simple shewas, were all fixed in place at this time, but simply that pausal forms delimiting the basic units of the text were fixed in place before the accentuation was introduced. It seems most likely that, although the pausal forms were preserved in the traditional vowelling when the accents were introduced, they were regarded, by that time, simply as free variants of the contextual forms. This is shown by the marking of some forms with conjunctive accents, or other accents inconsistent with the pausal vowelling (see §14 and n. 29). If the accentuation was in place first, and conditioned the vowelling, as is commonly sup­ posed, the vowelling should be consistent with the accentuation.
