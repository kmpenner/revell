CLAUSE STRUCTURE IN THE PROSE DOCUMENTS OF QUMRAN CAVE 1 (1)

1. — My previous article on Qumrani clause structure (2) presents few or no facts which were previously unknown. Its novelty lies in the point of view from which these facts are regarded. The study of Hebrew clauses as composed, not of words, but of 'clause builders' permits very much easier description and comparison of clause structure. It is necessary, however, to justify the establishment of the clause builder class somewhat more fully than can be done merely by demonstrating its validity in the verbal clauses of one text. This study attempts to do this by analyzing all the clauses in the prose texts of Qumran Cave I in terms of clause-builders.

2. — The purpose of this study is, then, to analyze the structure of the clauses in the language used to write the theological texts of Qumran Cave I. In order to ensure that it is based on the simplest form of the language commonly in use, the study is restricted to prose texts (3). Quotations from the Old Testament in these texts, which are explicitly marked as quotations, are not, of course, included in this study. However, the Qumran texts are full of allusions to the Old Testament. These allusions are, for the purposes of this study, considered to be typical of the form of language used to write the theological texts of Qumran Cave I, and native to it. Such allusions are not, therefore, excluded from

3. — In order to clarify the point of view from which this study is written, a short account of the syntactical structure of the Hebrew language as here envisaged must be given. Most Hebrew grammars are written in terms of two levels of structure, ‘words’, and ‘clauses’. However a much more exact and detailed analysis can be carried out if further levels of structure are isolated for individual investigation. It is well known that words are formed of smaller units, known as morphemes (5). Morphemes are the basic units of syntactic structure. They combine to form the next larger unit, the word. The term ‘word’ is used here with the meaning ‘member of a grammatical word class’ (6). Word classes which can be most easily distinguished are: verb (v), noun (n) (7), pronoun, infinitive (i) (8), article (h), conjunction (c), and preposition (p). The remaining particles are more difficult to classify. The outline of a new scheme, with reasons for rejecting the old, is given below in § 7.

These are: V, the verbal element,—Ns, a nominal element showing concord with the bound pronoun in the verb form (10)—N, any other type of nominal element,—pN, a nominal element preceded by a preposition,—pI, an infinitive and its modifiers preceded by

5. — Clause builders form clauses (12). These clauses form larger structures. Some of these could be described as 'complex clauses'; groups of clauses bound to each other by certain particles (13). Other structures formed by clauses could be called 'bound structures', as they are bound together by chains of pronouns, all of which refer back to the same antecedent.

6. — In this view of the structure of Hebrew, all members of a lower level of structure are considered as being used in the one above it. Thus all morphemes form part of, or act as, words. All words form part of, or act as, clause-builders. All clause-builders are equally considered to form part of clauses, or to act as clauses themselves.

7. — Some particles fit into this scheme in a different way from other words. The term 'particle' refers to the third great division of traditional Semitic Grammar, containing all words which are not nouns (and pronouns), or verbs (and infinitives). Only a small number of these are members of the clause-builder class 'particle'. The term 'particle' has been used for this clause-builder class, despite the possibility of confusion, to avoid putting forward new terminology before a new classification of the Hebrew particles is firmly established. However a new classification must be made. The traditional terminology, borrowed from the European languages, is altogether too vague (14). 8. — Thus, although they are not all used on the word level of structure, all particles find a place in this scheme. They do not, therefore, give any cause to doubt the statement that all members of a lower level are used in the level above. Thus all clause-builders form part of, or act as, clauses. Since clause-builders can—at least in theory—act as clauses, it follows that clauses cannot always be recognized by their structure. In some cases they can only be recognized by their use (21). In fact only the verbal clause is easy to define. The definition of 'clause' on which this study must proceed is, then, 'any structure composed of one or more clause-builders which is used on the same structural level as verbal clauses'. The Verbal Clause

9. — As already stated, the only type of clause easy to define is the verbal clause. The simplest form of verbal clause is a verb form. This can be expanded by the addition of an exponent of N8 showing concord with the pronominal element in the verb form, and/or by the addition of one or more exponents of any of the other clause builders as modifiers of the verb. The majority of such clauses which occur in 1 Q S have been described in "Order". For the reasons given in "Order" note 4, however, clauses containing imperative verb forms were not included in that study. Some dozen imperative forms occur in the material studied in this paper. The clauses in which they occur do not differ in structure from other verbal clauses. They are therefore treated here as ordinary verbal clauses (23).

20. — The two parts of clauses of this type are usually called 'subject' and 'predicate'. It is, however, possible to justify the use of these terms in less than 25% of the clauses. The use of such terms to denote grammatical units is only justifiable if it can be shown that the units are distinguished by some grammatical marker. Thus the 'predicate' of a verbal clause is distinguished by the fact that it contains the verb. Where no such grammatical marker exists, it is obvious that the grounds on which 'subject' is distinguished from 'predicate' cannot be grammatical. They cannot, therefore, be treated as grammatical units.

21. — As type 1 shows, it is not necessary that either part of a two part nominal clause be an exponent of N (46). Consequently, even where one part of the clause does consist of an exponent of N, as in clause types 2-6, it is not necessarily the subject (47). Consequently one must admit that in clause types 1 to 6, the grammatical structure of the clauses provides no means of distinguishing subject and predicate.

22. — In clause type 7, where both parts of the clause are exponents of N, there are two possible ways in which subject and predicate might be grammatically distinguished. The traditional view of such clauses would suggest that the part of the

clause marked as definite should be the subject, and the part showing concord should be the predicate. As regards the first possibility, type 7g shows that both parts of a clause can be marked as definite. It cannot, therefore, be claimed that wherever one part of the clause is marked as definite it must be the subject (48).

23. — As regards the second possibility, the showing of concord does seem to be a true mark of the predicate. This occurs most commonly, of course, where the headword of one part of the clause is a participle or adjective. The participle or adjective (in the predicate) shows concord with the headword of the other part of the clause (the subject), which is normally marked as definite. However pronouns can also show concord, and the zH HSrk type of clause is probably to be included among those the predicate of which is marked by the fact that it shows concord with the subject. In this type of clause, the form of the pronoun (here zH, masculine singular) is determined by the following noun (here srk, masculine singular). This noun is marked as definite. The structure is therefore the same as in the typical nominal clause with participle or adjective as predicate. zH is predicate, and HSrk subject. This analysis seems perfectly logical, yet BROCKELMANN considers zH to be the subject in this type of clause (49). If he is right, this is a case of 'subject' showing concord with 'predicate'. Concord would not then be valid as indicator of the predicate.

24. — If BROCKELMANN is correct, therefore, there is no grammatical indicator which can be used to distinguish subject and predicate. If he is wrong, concord serves as an indicator of the predicate in about 25% of the clauses. In most nominal clauses, then, there is no grammatical marker within the clause which distinguishes 'subject' from 'predicate'. The conclusion that the division of such clauses into 'subject' and 'predicate' is irrelevant is inevitable. The two part nominal clause is, then, a structure formed of two clause-builders the relationship between which is shown by the context in which they occur, and not by any syntactical marker within the clauses themselves.

Multipartite Clauses

25. — A number of nominal clauses contain more than two parts. In three part clauses of this type, the second part is often a condensed repetition of the first, in many cases simply a pronoun. The second and third parts of a clause of this type could be taken as a two part clause. For instance, wkly MLḤMWTM / HMH / MWR'M I Q p Hab VI, 4-5.

26. — Some scholars in the past treated the pronoun in such clauses as a ‘copula’; a lexically negligible mark of the syntactic structure involved (50). However this explanation is not fitting. The term ‘copula’ carries with it the idea of ‘verb’, and the pronoun certainly has no connection at all with this function. Every verbal clause containing an exponent of Ns is, in fact, a tripartite clause of this type (51). In such clauses a nominal structure, Ns, is repeated by a pronoun, the pronoun in the verb form, which is followed by the third part of the clause, the verbal predicate. Compare kly MLḤMWTM / HMH / MWR'M with H'YŠ HNS'L / Y- / -DBR BTRW (I Q S VI, 11). The similarity of the two structures forbids the suggestion that the pronoun acts the part of copula in the one when it obviously does not in the other. Further cause the rejection of this theory can, if needed, be given by the fact that the second part of the clause need not be a pronoun, but can be a larger nominal structure. For example hdgl hmtqrb / kwlm / šB' m'rkwt (I Q M IX, 4). So also hwnm I Q S IX, 8.

27. — The second part of a clause of this type cannot, therefore, be considered a mere copula, but must, from a syntactical point of view, be equal in importance to the other two parts. Some scholars, realizing this, have interpreted such clauses as being basically bipartite. According to their analysis the first part of such a clause is a subject which is followed by a predicate which is itself a complete clause composed of subject and predicate (the second and third parts) (52). This view is somewhat complicated by the following considerations.

38. — A similar type of structure is found in nominal clauses. 39. — The remaining material in these texts falls into the category of one part clauses. These are single clause-builders in an unbroken context, which cannot be said to form part of other clauses. These two structures are, then, parts of a whole, but it seems simplest to treat them as two one part clauses joined by a conjunction.

'YN and YŠ Clauses

44. — One group of clauses, often set aside as a separate type, has not yet been treated. These are clauses containing 'YN and YŠ. They are set aside as a separate type on the grounds that these two particles have some verbal characteristics, and deserve the name 'predicate particles'. The most significant basis for this claim is probably the fact that they take 'verbal suffixes'. Id est personal pronouns, when bound to these particles assume a form used with the 'imperfect' form of the verb. However other particles, for which no verbal characteristics are claimed, take the same forms of bound pronoun (76). As far as syntax goes, the clauses in which 'YN and YŠ are used do not differ from other nominal clauses.

45. — 'YN should probably be classed as a negative (77). It is characteristic of the negatives in Qumrani Hebrew that they stand immediately before the word or structure that they modify, and do not bring about any change in the regular order of the clause building elements (78). If 'YN is treated simply as a neg-
