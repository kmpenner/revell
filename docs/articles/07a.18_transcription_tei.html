Hebrew Accents and Greek Ekphonetic Neumes

At the end of the first millennium, Biblical (and other) texts in both Hebrew and Greek were frequently provided with ‘accent’ signs¹ to regulate the chanting of the text. The demonstration of relationship between Christian and Jewish liturgical music has led to various attempts to discover a relationship between these accent systems. The most recent of these, Gudrun Engberg’s article ‘Greek Ekphonetic Neumes and Masoretic Accents’ supports the idea of a close musical relationship between the systems, and suggests that the thesis there put forward be further investigated.² The present article arises from an attempt to follow this suggestion from the viewpoint of a hebraist.

Direct comparison of the accent systems is possible because, as far as we can tell, the use and function of the accents was the same both in Greek and in Hebrew texts. These texts were divided up into units — a word or a group of words — each of which was marked by an accent sign: at the end in Hebrew, at both beginning and end in Greek. Each accent had a ‘musical value’ in that it indicated the cadence to which the textual unit was to be chanted.³ Each accent also had an ‘interpunctional value’ in that it indicated the relationship between that textual unit and the others in the context.

The accent signs are generally supposed to have been first introduced into texts in the fifth century, although we have definite

However this may be, it is certain that, for the Hebrew Biblical text at least, the phenomena indicated by the accents were in existence much earlier. The division into textual units was already established, in much the same form as later became traditionally fixed, in the second century B. C., and it is highly probable that there was equally little change in the music to which these units were chanted.4 Before the introduction of written accent signs, the particular cadence required was indicated by a manual sign (cheironomy). It seems most likely that the written signs were, in fact, derived from these manual signs.5 The analysis of the Biblical text, and of the music to which it was chanted, was, therefore, well established before the introduction of written signs to mark it. It is highly likely that this was the case for the Rabbinic texts6 and for the Greek Biblical texts as well.

Of the two main functions of the accent signs:7 the marking of the cadence to which a textual unit was to be chanted, and the marking of the relationship of that unit to the others in the context, the latter appears generally to be considered the original.8 It has also been, almost exclusively, the basis of discussions by non-Jewish scholars of the Hebrew Biblical accents—presumably because it is the only function of the accent system significant for the study of the Biblical text. This has led to a tendency to regard these accents merely as ‘disjunctive’ markers of separation between phrases, or ‘conjunctive’ markers of connection between the words in a phrase, so that the interrelationship of the accents is scarcely regarded, save

insofar as they are said to mark ‘greater’ and ‘lesser’ pauses. The cheironomic signs must, however, have had a mainly musical significance, and, if the written signs are derived from them, their significance must be basically musical also.

There are other indications that this is the correct view. The only sign in the Hebrew Biblical text which is indisputably a mark of punctuation (sof pasuq, marking the end of a verse) is not connected with the accent system. The ‘major pausal accents’ silluq, atnaḥ, and segolta, which mark the end of the main divisions of a verse, far from being the earliest accents known and the most commonly marked, are, in fact, the most frequently omitted, and the latest to be marked with specific signs (see below). This is understandable if the system has a basically musical significance (since there is considerable possibility of variation at the beginning of such a division, but not at the end) but not if it is a system of punctuation. There is, moreover, no evidence of development, in the Hebrew system, from one system using a few signs to mark punctuation towards another using many signs to mark music. There is only evidence of development in the detail and accuracy of the marking of the same phenomena. The system in which the signs are used does not change. This would seem to hold true for all Hebrew systems, whether used in Biblical or Rabbinic texts, and is probably true for the Greek system also. The Greek accent signs, it is true, may well have developed from punctuation signs, but I can see no evidence of a smooth process of evolution. I would assume that a system of signs intended to mark musical phenomena was intentionally created, using, in some cases, the same signs as were used as punctuation markers.

It is, then, assumed in this paper that the basic function of the accent signs was musical, and that their function as markers of relationship was secondary.9 The signs are, moreover, regarded, not as individual entities with an intrinsic meaning, but as items in a system outside of which they are meaningless. The various types of accentuation are compared on the basis of the systems thus displayed, not on the basis of a particular text which they are used to punctuate. It is, after all, clearly true in all systems that the accentuation, in showing the relationship of the units of text to each other, shows which are to be emphasized, and to what extent. The accentuation controls the text, not vice versa (see note 66).

THE HEBREW BIBLICAL ACCENTS

1. General

The basic unit of the Hebrew Biblical accentuation10 is, of course, the verse. The length of a verse may vary from a few words to many, and the syntactic structure relating those words from a part of a clause to a group of clauses. Nevertheless, the accentuation of each verse follows the same basic pattern, and is autonomous — independent of anything in the preceding or following verses.

A verse is normally divided into two halves. The end of the first half is marked by atnaḥ, and the end of the second (and of the whole verse) by silluq. Excepting this feature (and the use of segolta, see below) the two halves have the same possibilities of accentuation. They are composed of a terminal clause which may be preceded by one or more contextual clauses. The terminal clause is accented tebir → tifḥa → atnaḥ/silluq, with tebir omitted from shorter clauses. In longer clauses, the minor accents from the contextual clause (rebia etc.) are used to mark divisions preceding tebir. The end of the contextual clause is marked with zaqef. This may be the only accent in the contextual clause, but most commonly it is preceded by pashṭa. Divisions preceding pashṭa are marked by rebia, geresh, pazer, legarmeh, or telisha, selected and arranged according to the requirements of the situation. More than one contextual clause may be used in one verse half. In the first verse half, the first of two or more contextual clauses may be made to stand out by the use of zarqa and segolta instead of pashṭa and zaqef to mark the last two phrases. A typical verse, then, is divided into two halves, each of which is composed of one contextual and one terminal clause (e. g. Lam. 1:1, 2). More than one contextual clause may occur in the first half (Lam. 1:3, 4), the second half (Lam. 1:10) or both halves (Lam. 1:7), and, if required, the first of two or more contextual clauses in the first verse half may be marked out by the use of zarqa-segolta (Lam. 1:12). Either verse half may be composed only of a terminal clause (Lam. 1:3, 4, 5, second half, 1:6 first half). If the verse is unusually short, it is not divided into halves by atnaḥ. It may then be composed of one contextual and one terminal clause (Lam. 3:1), or simply of one terminal clause (Lam. 3:2, 3).

There are, then thirteen basic accents, four of which are confined to terminal clauses (silluq, atnaḥ, tifḥa and tebir), two to ordinary

contextual clauses (zaqef and pashṭa), and two to special context- ual clauses (segolta and zarqa). The remaining accents (rebia, geresh, pazer, legarmeh, and telisha) appear most commonly in the ordinary contextual clause, but can be used in any clause. These accents are known as ‘disjunctive’ because they are represented by signs which are placed on the last word in a phrase, and thus mark the divisions between the phrases into which the verse is divided. The words with- in these phrases are, in most manuscripts, marked with ‘conjunctive’ accents, which indicate their interrelationship within the phrase. In a similar way, the disjunctive accents mark the interrelationship of the phrases within the verse, for, although, as described, wide variations are possible in the accentuation of verses, nevertheless certain definite patterns must be followed, and these serve to indi- cate the relationship of any one phrase to the others. These patterns are so definite that, in most cases where the first members of a group of accents are known, the remainder can be accurately pre- dicted. As shown above, this system of patterns covers the whole verse, so that each accent except that on the last word of a verse (silluq) implies something to come. The accentuation of a verse is a unit which cannot be broken up.

There is no indication, in the Hebrew Biblical accentuation, of the existence of units larger than the verse. Biblical texts do show a system of paragraphing, but this is not connected with the accents. In some present day communities a minor variation is used in the chanting of the final terminal clause of some large sections, but variations of this type are not marked in the accent system of any known manuscript. We may conclude, then, that the only unit of the Hebrew Biblical accentuation is the verse; characteristically divided into two halves, each of which shows a terminal clause which may be preceded by one or more contextual clauses.

The Tiberian system uses a different sign for each of the thirteen basic disjunctive accents, and goes beyond this in using further signs to indicate musical variants of some of them (zaqef gadol, yetib, etc.) The actual forms of the signs vary slightly among the manuscripts, but in all Tiberian manuscripts, the four disjunctive accents characteristic of the terminal clause are all written below the line. 3. The Palestinian Accentuation This form of accentuation is known only from a few manuscripts. This paucity of information is bound to leave gaps in our knowledge of the system. It is therefore doubly unfortunate that the most accessible description of it was written at the very beginning of the study of the Palestinian texts. As a result, an altogether distorted view of this accentuation is current in the scholarly world. It is generally viewed as an extremely primitive system which was in use only prior to the invention of the Tiberian accentuation. The claim that ‘in the Old-Palestinian notation there was only one sign – a single dot over the line’ for the accents rebia, zaqef, and segolta (an important point in the thesis of Engberg referred to above) is, unfortunately, an example of this. In fact, different signs are used for zaqef and rebia in all Palestinian manuscripts. The signs used are:

segolta.20 These two manuscripts (which are peripheral to the Palestinian tradition as we know it, and of which the accent systems differ in other features) might be held to show support for Engberg’s suggestion that zaqef, segolta, and rebia were originally not distinct. It is far more likely, however, considering the situation in the majority of Palestinian manuscripts, that it was customary not to use specific signs for atnaḥ and segolta (perhaps because no signs had been developed) and that the traditions which these manuscripts represent chose to use the zaqef sign for this purpose, rather than the more usual tifḥa or atnaḥ signs.21

We may, then, properly describe the situation in the Palestinian manuscripts by saying that the thirteen basic disjunctive accents were always marked (silluq usually by the verse end marker, sof pasuq, rather than by a real accent sign). While most accents were marked by a specific sign, however, some were marked with the sign for some other accent. It is notable that the accents of which the identity is most consistently obscured in this way are the major disjunctive accents: silluq, atnaḥ, and segolta. These signs come at the ends of clauses, and mark the characteristic ends of cadences. Their presence is, as was noted above, predictable. Consequently they are, in their musical significance, the least necessary of all the accent signs.22 The common failure to use different signs for zarqa and legarmeh might indicate that they were considered to be a single accent, but zarqa is not always marked by the legarmeh sign, and is, in one manuscript, given a specific sign of its own. It is more probable, in my opinion, that, just as it was not necessary to use specific signs for the major pausal accents because of their predictability, so it was not necessary to use a specific sign for zarqa because of its rarity. Zarqa characterized the ‘special contextual clause’ however, and had, therefore, to be distinguished from pashṭa and tifḥa, so — for whatever reason — legarmeh was usually used to mark it.

ment in the Palestinian system, while signs for silluq and segolta never were developed. To this extent one can say that the Palestin- ian writing system was more ‘primitive’ than the Tiberian. The question of ‘primitiveness’ is not, however, so easily settled. All the sophistications of the Tiberian system appear in Palestinian manuscripts, and, since different signs are used to mark these fea- tures, there can be no question of copying from one system to an- other.23 TS 12:197, and TS NS 246:22 outdo the Tiberian system in the detailed marking of certain features, as both distinguish three different conjunctives (as described in later grammatical works) where the Tiberian system uses only munaḥ.24 These manuscripts, however, only use eight different signs to mark the thirteen basic disjunctives. The only reasonable explanation is that there was a deliberate attempt to limit the number of different signs used. The apparent ‘primitiveness’ of the system is, then, in many cases, really a mark of sophistication. The men of the Palestinian community were obviously unwilling to add more than was absolutely necessary to the Biblical text, and so used a limited number of signs with great ingenuity to convey a great deal of information. For the same reason, no doubt, they tended to use their vowel and diacritical signs sporadically. The Tiberian scholars, on the other hand, held a different point of view, and opted for the clear marking of detail by the use of a much larger number of different signs.

A detailed study of the Palestinian accentuation has convinced me that it derives, in fact, from the same origin as does the Tiberian. Even in the most widely variant accentuation, the Palestinian dis- junctive accents still follow the rules of use derived by Wickes (op. cit.) from the study of Tiberian texts. The system of conjunctives appears to differ somewhat from the standard Tiberian, but similar anomalies can be found in Tiberian texts as late as the fifteenth century. A further mark of their common origin is the fact that, in both systems, the signs marking the terminal clause are written be- low the line, the other signs above. Even the different signs used in the two systems for the same accent can, it seems to me, be traced,

in many cases, to the same original.25 It seems highly likely (as noted above) that this original was the hand movement used in cheironomy to mark the cadence later marked by the written sign. These movements, then, formed the common basis of the Tiberian and Palestinian systems, and of other ‘Western’ systems (represented by even fewer manuscripts than is the Palestinian) which use a mixture of Palestinian and Tiberian sign forms. The different written signs perhaps originated in different communities, and certainly developed differently, but represent the same basic phenomena.

It is, then, my belief, which, I would claim, can be supported beyond any reasonable doubt, that the Palestinian and Tiberian accent systems derive from the same origin, and were in parallel, not successive, use.26 The apparent ‘primitiveness’ of the Palestinian system is the result of differing cultural priorities in the two communities, not of earlier date or simpler structure. The two systems are different ways of indicating the same basic phenomena, used in different communities at the same time.

4. The Babylonian System

The Babylonian accentuation is known from many manuscripts. The description of A. Spanier27 shows that several systems were in use. All of these, however, show certain characteristic differences from the Tiberian and other ‘Western’ systems. 1) The Babylonian accent signs are, characteristically, all written above the line, and mostly take the form of letters not strokes or dots. 2) Tifha before atnah and tifha before silluq are marked with different signs. Presumably, then, the terminal cadences of the two verse halves differed in their last two motifs, not, as in the Western systems, only in the final ones. 3) Each verse half can have only one contextual

THE ACCENTUATION OF RABBINIC TEXTS

1. General Description
Besides Biblical texts, a large number of other Hebrew texts are provided with accent signs. Some of these (the Targums, Ben Sira, etc.) show the system used in Biblical texts, but a large body of material, known generally as ‘Rabbinic Literature’ (Mishna, Talmud, Midrash) shows a completely different system. I. Yeivin has described a wide range of texts with this type of accentuation in his article ‘Prosodic Accents to Rabbinic Literature’, which has been most useful to this study. My own investigation of this accentuation has been based on texts of the Mishna, since the use of the different sets of accent signs can be most easily compared in texts of the same type — indeed, it is often possible to compare the use of different sets of signs in the same passage.

1a. The Basic System
In the Rabbinic texts, the basic unit of accentuation is the paragraph. This ‘paragraph’ is characteristically made up of a number of contextual clauses, all of which have the same possibilities of accentuation, followed by a terminal clause with different accents. The terminal clause is, in its characteristic form, divided into four phrases, marked by four accents, 1 (the last), 2, 3, and 4 (see Ex. 5:14). Ib. Special Situations

  • (i) Where a number of units in a contextual clause require equal emphasis

These seven accents used in this way constitute the basic system. In some manuscripts, however, in particular situations these basic accents are used differently, or further accents are marked. It may happen that several units within a contextual clause require the same emphasis. If they are relatively long, they are usually marked with accent 7, and, in many cases, a normal extended variation of the basic contextual clause is produced. In some cases, however, accent 6 is omitted — a situation frequent in lists where the con- textual clause ends with the last item in the list — so that the clause is accented 7 ..... 7 → 7 → 5 (as in the original accentuation of Ex. 1:3b). If the units requiring equal emphasis are relatively short, as with one-word items in a list, a new sign, accent 8 may be used to mark them. This sign may be used before accent 5, 6, or 7 (see Exx. 1:3a, 3:2a, 4:2, and 4:6). It may be repeated as often as is required, although single examples frequently occur.

There do not appear to be any rigid prescriptions for the use of these accents. The same passage may be accented in one manuscript

as a list, in another as a normal contextual clause. In some cases one finds that one manuscript uses accent 8 where another uses 7, and in some manuscripts both are used on the same word (see Ex. 1:3b), suggesting that accent 8 was a new introduction, and was sometimes added to manuscripts which had been accentuated before it was in use. It is possible that it did not indicate any innovation in the chant, but merely that no sign of cadence (or pause) should occur, even though (with the end of a syntactic unit) one might be expected.35 Similarly, it is possible that the repetition of other accent signs: 3, 6, or 7, may not indicate the repetition of a particular cadence, but the maintenance of it. This seems particularly likely in the case of accents 3 and 6, which are not normally repeated. It is equally possible, however, that the repetition of these signs indicated that the cadence was to be repeated, and that accent 8 was a particular cadence (or group of cadences) characteristic of (and used to mark) situations requiring a minor pause.

(ii) Where particular contextual clauses require special emphasis

In some manuscripts, certain contextual clauses are marked with special signs which differentiate them from normal contextual clauses. These indicate accent 9, which marks the end of the clause (corresponding to accent 5 in an ordinary clause) and accent 10 which marks the preceding division (corresponding to accent 6). Accent 10 may be repeated once, but any preceding divisions are marked with accent 7, as in the ordinary contextual clause.36 This type of accentuation is most commonly used in questions (see Ex. 5:13), although many questions are not marked in this way.37 It is not, however, restricted to questions, but can also mark sharp contrast, or emphasis (see the example given in note 36). Consequently it seems best to regard it as marking a contextual clause as requiring special emphasis of some sort or another.38

2. The Relation of the Rabbinic to the Biblical Accentuation

A number of the accent signs used in the texts of the Mishna are identical in form to signs used in Biblical texts. This naturally raises the question of the relationship of the systems of accentuation used in the two types of text. Yeivin, indeed, suggests that the accentuation used for Biblical texts was originally (at the ‘primitive’ stage of the Palestinian accentuation) identical to that used in Rabbinic texts, but was later developed to a higher stage.43 As we have seen, however, there is a basic difference in the articulation of the two types of text. The Biblical verse is (relatively) short, and is divided into two halves, each with its terminal clause, and, possibly, preceding contextual clause(s). The Rabbinic paragraph is (relatively) long, and shows only one terminal clause, usually preceded by several contextual clauses.

If it is assumed — as I think it must be — that both types of text were articulated, at the time when the rabbinic texts were produced, in the same way as they are in the medieval manuscripts, the conclusion that a different type of accentuation and chant was used for each seems to me inevitable.44 It would be possible, however, to regard the Rabbinic accentuation as a modification of Biblical accentuation. The accentuation of the Rabbinic paragraph could either be considered an extended form of the accentuation of shorter Biblical verses, in which (as described above) only one terminal clause is used, or it could be considered as developed from the accentuation of the standard Biblical verse in the same way as the accentuation found in lengthy verses of the Targum, in which more than one atnaḥ can be used.45

As a preliminary to the discussion of these possibilities, it must be pointed out that some manuscripts of classes 1, and 2, and all those of classes 3, 6, and 7, can be definitely associated with particular types of Biblical manuscripts, all of which use ‘Western’ accentuation.46 We know, then, the exact significance of these signs

in terms of Biblical accentuation. In these terms, in all classes shown in table I, the accent which marks the beginning of the terminal clause (accent 4 in classes 1-6) is tebir, and accent 1, which marks the end is (where marked) silluq. This suggests a strong connection with the terminal clause of the Biblical accentuation. Accents 2 and 3, however, are also marked with accent signs used in the associated Biblical texts (except for accent 3 of class 6), but these signs are never, in Biblical texts, used between tebir and silluq (except accent 2 in classes 4 and 5). A further point of difference from the terminal clause of the Western Biblical texts is that it has four phrases, not three, and that, in most manuscripts, one of the signs is written above the line. The signs marking the contextual clause in class 1 would be read, in the Tiberian Biblical accentuation, as zaqef → tifḥa → atnaḥ, suggesting the accentuation of the first half of a Biblical verse, and therefore that this system is comparable to the ‘extended verse’ accentuation found in long verses of the Targums. The atnaḥ divisions in the Targums are, however, very much longer and more complicated than is the typical contextual clause in the Mishna, and zaqef marks a major clause, not an introductory phrase as so often in the Mishna accentuation.47 It seems most unlikely that this is because the accentuation of the Mishna has retained the ‘primitive’ form of accentuation originally used for the Bible as well, for, where accents are added to the basic ten described, they are not disjunctives, expressing more subtle phrase division, but conjunctives.48 It is also noteworthy that the accents of the contextual clause can not be read zaqef → tifḥa → atnaḥ in any other class, which would be surprising — considering the almost complete homogeneity of the use of these signs in the different manuscripts — if they did originate from the accentuation of the Biblical half-verse. Finally, if the accentuation of class 1 were a true parallel to the ‘extended verse’ accentuation of the Targums, we would expect the standard terminal cadence to read zaqef → tifḥa → silluq, but, as already noted, this is never the case. In fact we may say that the fact that the initial accent(s) of the basic terminal clause are never marked with the same signs as those of the contextual clause in any class of manuscripts effectively disposes of the ‘extended verse’ as a possibly parallel form of accentuation.

In class 7, the accents marking the contextual clause would be read as rebia → pashṭa → zaqef, suggesting the contextual clause of the Biblical accentuation, and therefore the ‘extended half verse’ pattern as a parallel. It is again noteworthy that this is the only class in which the accents of the contextual clause can be read in this way, and again, if the ‘shorter verse’ of the Biblical accentuation is a true parallel one would expect a different accentuation in the terminal clause.

The accent signs marking the contextual clause in the remaining classes are all found in Biblical texts, but cannot be used there in the order in which they are used in the Mishnaic texts. Various arguments could be discussed, but it seems to me that only one conclusion is possible. If the same accent signs do indicate the same musical motifs in Mishnaic as in Biblical texts, we can say that the same motifs were used, but were used in a quite different way. If the same signs do not indicate the same motifs in the two types of text, there is no reason at all to suppose a close relationship between Biblical and Mishnaic chant. It is impossible, in my opinion, that the same signs should have had the same interpunctional value in the two types of text. There is no evidence to suggest that the Biblical accentuation as we know it was preceded by a stage in which verses (or paragraphs) were accentuated as a series of simple contextual clauses followed by a single terminal clause. The accentuation of the two types of text, therefore, could not have been the same in origin. My own opinion would be that some motifs were common to both types of chant, and that they are related in a few other features as well, but that the accentuation and chant used for Rabbinic texts was never either at its origin or during its development closely similar to that used for the Biblical text, and had, in fact, a completely different basic structure. This conclusion is, I would suggest, confirmed by the quotation ‘Whoever reads (the scriptures) without cantillation (naʿimah) or recites (the Mishna) without chant (zimrah) ...’ in which the use of two different words strongly suggests the use of two different types of chant.49

THE GREEK BIBLICAL ACCENTUATION

The Greek Biblical accentuation is described by Carsten Höeg in La notation ekphonétique.50 The unit of accentuation in this system is not the verse, as in the Hebrew Biblical accentuation, but the

[Page 159]

HEBREW ACCENTS AND GREEK EKPHONETIC NEUMES 159

paragraph. This is made up of a number of units divisible as follows: (1) an opening clause — usually rather short, and with restricted possibilities of accentuation — ending with teleia (2) a number of medial clauses, each also ending with teleia. Their accentuation is similar to that of the opening clause, but has more varied possibilities. (3) A closing clause accentuated differently from the opening and medial clauses (and so not ending in teleia), and with strictly limited possibilities of variation in this accentuation.

It is immediately apparent that the basic structure of this accentuation is the same as that of the systems used in Rabbinic texts. Neither the teleia unit, nor the paragraph, show the typical feature of the Hebrew Biblical accentuation: division into two halves, each of which has a terminal clause. On the contrary, we find a number of contextual clauses (teleia units) followed by a single terminal clause, just as in the Mishna texts. There can be no question but that the Greek Biblical accentuation and the Hebrew Rabbinic accentuation follow the same pattern. It is, of course, obvious that the two types of accentuation show numerous differences. The most striking of these is in the writing system. Hebrew accentuation (of any type) marks only the end of a phrase. In the Greek accentuation both the beginning and the end are marked. This difference might be considered by some to be superficial, but it seems to me so basic as to eliminate the possibility of any important influence by one writing system on the other. It indicates a difference not just in the method of marking, but in the nature of what is marked. The Greek system is, it seems to me, marking a specific cadence to which the textual unit is to be chanted. The Hebrew method marks only the final motif, but (unless conjunctive accents are used) leaves the beginning of the cadence, where a certain amount of variation is possible, to the reader's knowledge of the tradition.

Other obvious features in which the Greek Biblical accentuation differs from the systems used in Rabbinic texts are the use of an opening clause, and the more varied possibilities for the accentuation of contextual clauses, marked by a larger number of signs, the use of which is, to some extent, determined by the content of the textual unit marked. It seems to me, however, that these differences could easily be explained as the result of different developments from a common original. Certainly the introduction of accents 9 and 10 into the Rabbinic accentuation suggests a development towards a closer relationship between the accent signs and the content of the texts, as found in the Greek accentuation.51

As with the contextual clauses, so with the terminal clause, it is possible to suggest that the standard form in the Greek Biblical accentuation is a development from the same origin as the terminal clause in the Rabbinic texts. As described by Carsten Höeg, all terminal clauses have the last three phrases marked with bareiai diplai, kentemata, and, finally, apostrophoi. The shortest terminal clauses consist only of three phrases marked in this way, but these are not common.52 Longer terminal clauses are built up as follows (starting from the end).

  • (i) Apostrophoi one phrase
  • (ii) Kentemata one phrase
  • (iii) Bareiai diplai one to four phrases, most commonly two.
  • (iv) Oxeiai diplai one phrase, or – if (v) is not used – rarely two.
    or kathistai kathistai53 one phrase
  • (v) Other accents.

A terminal clause may begin with item (iii), (iv), or (v).

Occasional minor variations from the pattern described do occur,54 but it is clear that this is the basic pattern. It appears to coincide with the basic pattern of the rabbinic terminal clause to a remarkable degree. Four accents are characteristic of the Greek terminal clause: apostrophoi, bareiai diplai, and oxeiai diplai (items i, iii, iv) because they are confined to it, and kentemata (item ii) because it is always used in it, although it may occur in contextual

clauses as well. In the Rabbinic accentuation likewise, four accents are characteristic of the contextual clause. Most commonly, it is true, the signs used are all confined to the terminal clause, but in some manuscripts the sign for accent 2 is also used in the contextual clause (classes 3 and 6), and this is the only one that ever is. In both systems (taking the Rabbinic classes 1-6 as standard) the shortest possible terminal clause has three phrases, marked with accents 1, 2, 3 / items (i), (ii), (iii). In both systems the terminal clause can be extended almost indefinitely, in which cases the initial phrases are marked by the accents of the contextual clause. In the Greek accentuation considerable variation is possible in the accentuation of phrases before the last three. Bareiai diplai (item iii) is nearly always repeated, and other accents need not to be used at all.55 In the Rabbinic accentuation, accent 4 is nearly always used in a clause of four or more phrases, and phrases before the last four are normally marked with the accents of the contextual phrase. In a number of manuscripts, however, accent 3 is repeated (see note 32), showing, perhaps, sporadic use in the Hebrew chant of a feature which became standard in the Greek. Such phenomena as this may possibly be coincidental if isolated, but several such points of contact exist in this situation. These, allied with the similarity of the basic structure of the Greek accentuation to the Rabbinic, strongly suggest that they are different developments from the same origin.

THE SYRIAC ACCENTUATION

One cannot reasonably consider the problem of the relationship of the Greek and the Hebrew systems of accentuation without also considering the Syriac system. Syrian influence on the liturgy and music of the early Byzantine church is well known, and Syriac manuscripts provide the earliest known examples of accent signs. Their example may well have stimulated both the Jews and the Greeks to develop their own writing systems.

The Syriac accentuation is described by J. B. Segal in The Diacritical Point and the Accents in Syriac.56 It appears that the unit of accentuation here is a short clause ending with pasoqa. The textual unit so marked is similar in length to the teleia unit in the Greek accentuation, and in Biblical texts, the two often coincide exact-

ly.57 As with the teleia unit, then, the pasoqa unit is generally shorter than the Hebrew Biblical verse, and rarely coincides exactly with it. The possibilities for the accentuation of the pasoqa unit are extremely varied, and much more closely connected with the content of the text even than the accents of the Greek system. There is, however, a strong tendency to divide the unit in two halves, although the accentuation is not otherwise similar to that of the Hebrew Biblical verse.

Paragraphs are often marked in Syriac manuscripts, but by methods not connected with the accent system. I know of no evidence that a special terminal cadence was used at the end of such a division. This is possibly connected with the fact that the Syriac accentuation was used for all types of texts, and was not confined to texts intended for liturgical (or similar) use. This fact, together with the fact that most of the accents occur sporadically, not in regular patterns, and that many of them seem to be intended to bring out particular shades of meaning in the text,58 suggests that the Syriac accentuation perhaps reflects a form of reading which was less musical, and more closely similar to the tones of ordinary speech, than is the case with the Hebrew and Greek. It is my opinion, however, that further study of the Syriac accentuation, specifically for the purpose of comparison with the Greek might yield interesting results.

CONCLUSION

This brief survey suggests that, although the practice of chanting Biblical (and other) texts was current throughout the Judaeo-Christian world, several different forms of chant were in use. One of these, the Hebrew Biblical chant, appears to have been unique. It could be argued that the halving of Syriac pasoqa units reflects a common basis, but the age of the Hebrew system, and the lack of any parallels in the Syriac system beyond the simple division into two parts, appear to deny this as a possibility. The Syriac system, it seems to me, should be classified with the Greek and Rabbinic systems, all of which are characterized by relatively short contextual clauses with relatively simple accentuation. In all three systems the texts were normally divided into paragraphs. In the Greek and Rabbinic systems, but not in the Syriac, the end of these was marked

[Page 163]

by a terminal clause. Despite this difference, and the differences which separate the Greek and the Rabbinic systems, it seems possible to me that all three derived from the same origin. This would have been a rather simple form of chant (or ‘elevated reading style’) used for the public (and not necessarily liturgical) reading and oratory. There is no difficulty in supposing that very similar systems would be in use over the whole Syria-Palestine area, both for Hebrew and for the grammatically similar Aramaic/Syriac language.59 This form of chant was restricted in the Greek culture, to which it was foreign, to use with Biblical texts, but in that sphere it seems to have been developed to considerable musical complexity. In the Jewish culture it was used for Rabbinic texts, which were also fixed in form, and as enshrining the ‘Oral Law’, had a value similar to that placed upon the Bible. Here the chant seems to have undergone a development rather similar to that of the Greek chant, but (as it was used in pedagogical more than in liturgical situations) to a lesser degree of complexity. Among the Syrian Christians, where the chant was used for the public reading of any text, it seems to have remained musically rather simple, but to have been developed so as to bring out the meaning of a text, and its emotional overtones.

It seems, then, that if the Greek Biblical accentuation did develop from some form of Hebrew accentuation, it must have developed from the type used for the Mishna and similar texts. If some of the motifs used in Biblical chant were also used in Mishnaic chant, as suggested above, this would account for the occurrence of similar motifs in European music.60 We do know, however, that, in pre-Christian times, the Greek Bible was, in some communities, chanted to the same melody as was used for the Hebrew Bible,61 so it is surprising to find that this usage was not continued by the Christians.

It is possible, however, to suggest an explanation. There was no form of chant traditional for New Testament texts, and these did not, anyway, receive, at first, a veneration equal to that for the Old Testament. They would naturally have been rendered in the form of chant in general use for public reading; i. e., the type of chant

used for Rabbinic texts.62 The Jewish Biblical chant may, indeed, have been used at first for the Old Testament lessons, but, as time went on, these became less numerous, and less important in the liturgy. It is easy to suppose that, as a result, the chant used for New Testament lessons would come to be considered as the characteristic Biblical chant, and used for the Old Testament also.63

Gudrun Engberg notes that previous efforts to show relationship between the Greek and the Hebrew Biblical accentuation have not been fruitful.64 She suggests that such relationship can be shown, but investigation from the point of view of a Hebraist suggests that her results must be the effect of coincidence; apparent but not real. This does not, in fact, seem to me at all unlikely. As Engberg points out,65 the Greek and Hebrew Biblical texts are theoretically identical, so it must be expected that they will produce divisions of similar content and relationship, and consequently that there will be a certain amount of coincidence between the major pausal accents in both systems. With this in mind, I do not find the proportion of coincidence of apostrophos with zaqef very impressive, even within the framework set up by Engberg. Because of this, and certain weak points (as I would consider them) in the framework itself,66 I would argue that Engberg’s attempt to demonstrate this relationship is no more fruitful than that of her predecessors. I would further suggest that any such attempt must fail because no such relationship existed, save indirectly, to the extent that the Hebrew Biblical and Rabbinic

A Method for Indexing Byzantine Music

Since one of the fundamental aspects of Byzantine music is the utilization of melodic formulae one might be led to believe that the best way of musical identification of the hymns would be to pre- pare an index (or repertory) of such melodic formulae. It appears to us, however, that the traditional way of preparing catalogues of incipits may actually be a better method for identification for the Byzantine Chant. In Enrica Follieri's comprehensive catalogue of text-incipits a sufficient number of letters of the text gives the full identification of the hymn texts. A sufficient number of neumes is supposed to result in the same kind of full identification of the single melodies of the hymns.

This method was tried out on thirty-five Dogmatic hymns fully encoded in a computer.1 The machine was asked to take from the encoded material the first twelve intervals of each hymn and the corresponding neumes, the mode, the starting tone, and the refer- ence to source as well as to Tillyard's transcription. The result of this inquiry may be seen in Ex. 1. The thirty-five incipits were now arranged by four criteria: interval, neume, mode, and starting tone, which taken together give twenty-four indexing possibilities.2 Only four of these will be given here.

Ex. 1 shows musical incipits arranged by intervals. The intervals are counted as steps, i. e. Ison = 0, Oligon = 1, Kentema = 2, etc., following the 'Dictionary of Intervals' (cf. Studies in Eastern Chant, II, p. 135). The intervals are ordered from the most positive to the most negative through all the eight modes. It is thus possible with the help of this index to look up any melody including melodies transcribed in Western notation (in MMB, Transcripta), and also cases when the signature is not readable.

As was emphasized above, the syntax does not control the accentuation. The accentuation controls the emphasis to be put on any particular syntactic unit. ↩
