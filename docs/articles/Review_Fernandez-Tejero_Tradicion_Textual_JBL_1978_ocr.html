La Tradition Textual Espanola de la Biblia Hebrea. El manuscrito 118-Z-42 (Ml) de la Biblioteca de la Universidad Complutense de Madrid, by Emilia Fernandez-Tejero. Textos y Estudios “Cardenal Cisneros,” 14. Madrid: Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Cientificas, 1976. Pp. lvii+633. N.P. (paper).

This book, originating in a thesis under F. Perez-Castro, is a study of the textual affinities of the famous MS 1 of the Madrid University Library (Ml, written in Toledo in 1280). This MS has been chosen as a collation base for the text of the Hebrew Bible in the Biblia Poliglota Matritense. This study investigates its relationship to the best Tiberian MSS, as well as to the other MSS from Toledo, the source of the finest and most accurate Spanish MSS of the Hebrew Bible.

The relationship of Ml to the Tiberian MSS is studied in terms of the ben-Asher (bA) ben- Naftali (bN) variants. Two bases of comparison are used: the edition of Mishael b. Uzziel’s Kitab al- Khilafby L. Lipschuetz (Textus 2 [1962] Hebrew pages 1-58), and the list ofvariants from the margins of Ml. The variants noted in these two lists are compared with the readings of Ml, Leningrad MS B19a (L), the Cairo Codex of the Prophets (C), and British Museum MS Or 4445 (B), the three best available representatives of the bA tradition. Also compared is MS 44a of the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York (Hil), written in Toledo in 1241, and said in a colophon to have been corrected against the Codex Hilleli. M1 is shown to be less close to the bA of these lists than is L, as close as B, and considerably closer than C. In relation to the individual bA MSS, M1 is closest to L (70% agreement), while it agrees with B and C in only about 55% of the cases. Not surprisingly, M1 is closest of all to Hil (83% agreement). This study, then, shows the text of M1 to be representative of a Spanish form of the received tradition which is still quite close to the tradition of Aharon b. Asher (as represented by L), but differs from it in many small details. The relationship of Ml to other Toledan MSS is gauged by comparison of their texts in an arbitrary selection of 390 verses (10 from each Biblical book). The text of Ml is presented in full, with an apparatus of variants from L, Hil, two other Toledan MSS, and other sources. The feature which most commonly shows variation, the metheg, is then studied in the framework of Yeivin’s description of its use in The Aleppo Codex of the Bible (Jerusalem: Hebrew University, 1968), and the relationship of the MSS is established on this basis. No attempt is made to justify the assumption that relationship established on this basis will hold true for all other features of the tradition, but since other types of variation are neither numerous nor consistent, it can be accepted as reasonable.

This comparison again shows M1 as generally close to L, but with considerable variation in small details—a typical high quality MS of the period after 1100. The Toledan MSS taken as a group do not appear to represent a homogeneous tradition in the use of the metheg, and this study gives no support to the idea of a single Toledan scriptorium, or the use of one particular model text in Toledo. (The author has pursued this subject, one of the interests which led her to undertake the present study, in a recent article in Sefarad.)

Müller then proceeds to analyze the content and structure of various Pauline prophetic sermons, grouping them in three categories: (1) the proclamation of the imminence of the eschaton as part of the prophetic speech of admonition (Rom 13:11-14; 1 Thess 5:1-11; 1 Cor 7:29-31); (2) prophetic judgment speeches (1 Cor 5:3-5; Rom 16:17-20; Phil 3:17-4:1; Gal 1:6-9); and (3) prophetic proclamations of salvation for the purpose of consolation (1 Thess 4:13-14; 1 Cor 15:51-52; Rom 11:25-26).

This summary does little justice to this ambitious and ingenious attempt to describe and analyze the Gattung of the prophetic sermon in Revelation and Paul. Müller, however, is too ingenious. He assumes, rather than demonstrates, that the models for early Christian prophetic speech were derived from a living, oral prophetic tradition which may be traced through early Jewish apocalyptic literature back to the OT. Three problems accompany this assumption: (1) Models for prophetic speech are not easily discernable in early Jewish apocalyptic literature; most, if not all of the examples cited by Müller are from paraenetic sections of apocalyptic literature which, though they have eschatological frameworks, are not necessarily examples of prophetic speech. (2) The possible influence of Greco-Roman oracular speech forms is excluded without consideration; H. D. Betz has clearly demonstrated such influences on 2 Cor 12:9 in his article, “Eine Christus-Aretalogie bei Paulus,” ZTK 66 (1969) 288-305. Other examples could be cited. (3) The use of OT form critical categories in the analysis of early Jewish and early Christian speech forms is frequently more a hindrance than a help. The inappropriateness of such categories is evident when Müller’s analysis reveals that most instances of prophetic speech forms in Revelation and Paul are variations of two or more Grundformen. Though Müller is dissatisfied with the form critical analysis of Rev 2-3 in F. Hahn’s article, “Die Sendschreiben der Johannesapokalypse: Beitrag für Bestimmung prophetischer Redeformen,” Tradition und Glaube: Festgabe für K. G. Kuhn (eds. G. Jeremias, H.-W. Kuhn, and H. Stegemann; Göttingen: Vandenhoeck & Ruprecht, 1971) 357-94, Hahn’s analysis must be judged far more satisfactory than Müller’s. Hahn’s structural analysis is derived from the letters themselves, while Müller struggles to impose alien categories upon them.

J. Bjerkelund’s superb study, Parakalö: Form, Funktion d Sinn der parakaiö­ Sätze in denpaulinischen Briefen (Oslo: Universitets Forlaget, 1967). Bjerxelund has conclusively demonstrated that the Pauline parakaleö clauses are not part of a paraenesis form, but are an epistolary formula found in Greek papyri; Müller vainly tries to rebut the conclusions of Bjerkelund. Few would deny that Paul was a prophet or that prophetic speech is embedded in various sections of Paul’s letters. The basic problem is the formulation of objective criteria for identifying and isolating such prophetic speech forms. While Müller’s analysis of the seven letters in Rev 2-3 is unsatisfactory, his attempt to isolate prophetic speech forms in Paul is also largely unsuccessful. The Jewish-Christian homily, closely related to the Greco-Roman diatribe, while it has many subsidiary structures, does not appear to lend itself easily to the rigidities of the form critical method pursued by Müller. A morphological analysis of this Gattung must proceed on the assumption that, like the folk tale, optional rules are unpredictably operative. This book will be of little interest to most students of Bible or Old Testament, but of considerable interest to students of the history of the masoretic tradition—especially, of course, to those investigating the complex currents which affected the development of the text forms used in Europe. The kind of problem faced in this area is well illustrated by the lists of variants between bA and bN. The variants in the lists of ben Uzziel and Ml are arranged by the author in three groups: (1) in 160 cases the two lists share a common basis (although they agree fully in only 95 of these); (2) 300 cases are listed in Ml, but not in ben Uzziel; (3) 263 cases are listed in ben Uzziel, but not in Ml. The text of L (the closest MS to the bA of ben Uzziel which could be compared) agrees with the bA of ben Uzziel (groups 1 and 3) in over 90% of the cases, but with the bA of group 2 in only 65%. In contrast to this, C, the work of Moshe b. Asher, but nevertheless further from the bA of ben Uzziel than any MS studied (37% agreement), shows a much higher rate of agreement with the bA of group 2 (58%). (Incidentally Hil similarly shows only 50% agreement with the bA of ben Uzziel, but 68% with the bA of group 2.)

The figures given show it to be extremely unlikely that the M1 list was derived from that of ben Uzziel by a simple process of accretion and corruption, and suggests that considerable blocks of the material in these lists may have arisen from sources which, although different, still had some claim to represent the b A tradition. The understanding of this problem would bring us a lot closer to understanding the manifold developments which took place in the period between the production of the model codices of the masoretic masters and the fixing of the masoretic tradition in print.

Texts and Studies in the Hebrew Language and Related Subjects, 1. Tel-Aviv: Tel-Aviv University, 1975. Pp. 7Z>+235+xii. N.P.

A direct result of the growing interest in targum studies is a number of fine works on the Aramaic language. The most recent of these is Abraham Tai’s descriptive and comparative analysis of Targum Jonathan (TJ) to the Former Prophets. Written in Hebrew (with a brief, rather inadequate English summary), this revised Ph.D. thesis contains (1) an introduction about the text, its language and background and the methods used in writing the book; (2) chapters on pronouns, particles, adverbs, numerals, and the morphology of nouns and verbs; (3) a chapter on the various dialectal elements in the text: Old Aramaic, Western Aramaic, and Eastern Aramaic; (4) sections on unrecorded lexical materials and loan words: Hebrew, Greek, Latin and Persian; (5) a lexical analysis of five semantic fields: construction, workmanship, instruments, measures and weights, and money; (6) a brief section on Tosefta Targum; (7) an analysis of the midrashic material in TJ and TJ’s relationship to the targum of the Latter Prophets and the targum of Onkelos; (8) a conclusion.

Although during its transmission this text has suffered some of the influences of later forms of the language, Tai has devoted most of his effort to lexical matters in the hope of excluding these potentially less accurate elements from consideration. His conclusion, presented early in the work and tested and substantiated throughout is that the language of TJ “is closely connected with Old Aramaic” (viii). He notes that most of the elements which he has considered are found in ancient epigraphic texts and that there are frequent significant parallels between TJ and the corpora representing older forms of the language: Nabatean, Palmyrene, Qumran Aramaic and certain parts of Syriac. On the other hand, there are elements in TJ which are lacking or in the process of receding in Middle Aramaic while many of the common features are also documented in Old Aramaic. In fact, Tai concludes, this Aramaic of the post-biblical and pre-rabbinic corpora, an Aramaic which is deeply rooted in Old Aramaic and not yet subject to the diversity of forms of Middle Aramaic, includes the language of TJ. Tai dates the compilation of this text not later than the Bar Kochba revolt and sees in the Western Aramaic substratum (similarities with Christian Palestinian Aramaic and Samaritan but not the other dialects) evidence for its having been composed in Palestine. A lack of certain parallels with Galilean Aramaic leads him to point to Judea as its place of origin.

The book contains a wealth of important data and anyone seriously interested in the field will be able to make good use of it, but as fine as the contents may be, two of the book’s physical properties, both of which are important to the reader who has less than native fluency in Israeli Hebrew, leave something to be desired: the use of bold type as a guide to divisions in the lexical data is inconsistent and occasionally misleading; and the insufficient tables and paradigms are frequently not labeled. There is also the standard problem of poor bindings on Israeli publications. Nonetheless, the serious reader will be most appreciative of the many footnotes printed on the page, the thorough listing by chapter and verse of every source quoted, and even the inclusion of the unneeded superlinear Babylonian vocalization in some places, all of which tend to outweigh the disadvantages the volume may pose for the novice.

The author is fully at home in targumic literature and Aramaic dialectology and has made an important contribution to both fields, in the former in his clarification of the date and place of origin of an important text and in the latter in the lexical discussions. Any Aramaist or targumist will find this a welcome, reasonably priced addition to an ever-growing stock of tools and monographs.
