1. Introduction.

1.1 For the purposes of this study, collectives are defined as nouns which are morphologically singular, but which may have singular or plural coreferents. A coreferent of a collective is commonly described as showing “grammatical” concord when singular, “notional” concord (constructio ad sensum) when plural. If this is taken as indicating that concord is determined either by linguistic convention (a noun which is singular in form has singular coreferents) or by the speaker’s perception of reality (the plural is used to make reference to a collection of individ­ uals), a difficulty appears. A compound subject should have plural coreferents both according to grammatical convention, and as a reflection of reality, yet a compound subject of which the first component designates

1.2 There is regularity in the choice of number in the coreferents of collectives in one limited area. An attributive which presents a quality of the group designated by the collective (and not of the individuals which form it) is singular. As described, the basis of this is semantic, so the value of the term “grammatical” for such concord is questionable. Equally, one would expect that a speaker would be led to think of a group performing particular actions as a number of individuals rather than as a unit, and so to the “notional” use of a plural verb where the subject was a collective. It is true that an active verb with a collective subject is often plural, but there is no group of verbs which are charac­ teristically plural when the subject is collective. This might be held to show that the “notional” plural is a free variant of the “grammatical” singular, were it not that the use of the latter is, in one area at least, clearly conditioned.

3.2 The correlation between high transitivity and the treatment of a collective as plural can be investigated more precisely in terms of the parameters of transitivity set up in P. J. Hopper and S. A. Thompson. 12 Such an investigation is, however, confronted with several problems. The limits of our knowledge of Biblical Hebrew would reduce treatment of parameters like “aspect” and “punctuality” largely to guesswork. In addition, the boundaries of semantic categories are typically ill-defined. Some examples are easy to assign to opposing categories, but for a considerable proportion, assignment can only be arbitrary, as would be the case if all humans were to be categorized as “tall” or “short.” Moreover, a variety of factors typically condition the choice between contrasting items which carry a semantic load, just as a number of factors, such as sex and diet, would affect human stature. It is easy enough to select examples to show that a particular factor is significant in some cases, but the conclusion that it is significant in all cases must be derived by argument. It cannot be demonstrated, because no one factor is always dominant.

9. Conclusion.

9.1 This survey shows that, generally speaking, the choice of number in coreferents of the collectives in the corpus follows regular patterns. Where the collective is subject of a clause, the head of the predicate is generally singular, as are other coreferents within the clause, and in clauses subordinate to it. More distant coreferents, outside the syntactic unit formed by the clause, are generally plural. Where a collective does not act as subject, coreferents are also usually plural; the singular is common only within the same constituent as the collective. A nominal attributive to a collective is generally singular, but this also has no effect on other relationships within the clause. Where singular is expected, the use of the plural is mainly determined by the prominence of the collective or its clause in the narrative. Other factors, particularly high transitivity, and the fact that the collective is actor, are also conducive to the use of the plural. Certain passages, characterized above as “histor­ ical synopsis” or “prophetic register,” use an unusually high proportion of singular cjoreferents. Outside these passages, the proportion of exam­ ples which must be described as anomalies, in that they clearly conflict with the common pattern, is negligible.

5. ינא and יכנא in Human Speech, Conclusions

5.1

Where the use of ינא and יכנא is classified in terms of the status of the speaker relative to the addressee, a regular pattern appears. This is clearest where the speaker is status-marked. Such speakers typically use ינא. They use יכנא in little more than quarter of the possible cases. They can use it to show that the status difference between the mem- bers of the dyad is being ignored to influence the addressee, to show humility or politeness, or to show that the clause functions as ‘back- ground’. Non-status speakers usually use יכנא, but use ינא in over one third of the cases. It is used to put forward a claim of some sort, an obvious counterpart to the use of יכנא by a status-marked speaker showing humility. The use of ינא by a non-status speaker often reflects emotion or strong feeling, a similar usage since this also draws the speaker’s concerns forcefully to the attention of the addressee. The use of ינא by non-status speakers to mark a clause as significant is the counterpart to the use of יכנא by status-marked speakers to show that a clause is background. The most common situation in which ינא is used in this category, to heighten a contrast or other shift in topic, is characteristically marked by other ‘foregrounding’ devices in other situations: the use of a subject placed before the verb, of a free pronominal subject in a verbal clause, etc. Thus, although the recognition of any particular clause as ‘significant’ is, in isolation, arbitrary, comparison of usage can supply considerable support for such recognition. The use of ינא and יכנא by human speakers can, then, be seen as a self-consistent system which corresponds, on occa- sion, to features of other systems with similar functions.

5.2

In general, then, the use of ינא by humans shows either that the speaker is status-marked, or that the clause is ‘immediate’, is central

6.3

The use of יכנא usual in speech directed by God to a human can be interpreted as the sort of condescension shown by a status-marked human who uses יכנא as a form of politeness (4.2). The fact that such usage is rare with humans, usual with God, may reflect the fact that God’s status is never in doubt, whereas human status may be questioned, and so is constantly asserted. It seems quite likely that, in this feature, the speech of God may reflect that of human parents, who are unlikely (as usage in other languages, such as French and German, suggests) to use status language to small children. יכנא is also used in speech ascribed to God, as in that of status-marked humans, to mark clauses as ‘background’. יבא is used where the use of יכנא is not required for the purposes described. Thus the use of יבא and יכבא in speech by God can be explained as based on the same concepts as in speech by humans.26 However, speech ascribed to God does seem to contain more anomalies in the use of these forms than is the case with human speech.

7. The Wider Context

7.1 Biblical Hebrew

A set of distinguishing features allows the division of biblical Hebrew into an early’ form (EBH) and a ‘late’ form (LBH). In LBH, the only first person singular pronoun form used is יבא, as is the case in all later Hebrew (apart from quotation or imitation of the biblical language). The ‘P’ material of the Pentateuch (the characteristics of which are carefully studied in Hurwitz [1982]) represents LBH; the failure to use יכבא is characteristic of it. In the Book of Deuteron- omy, which represents EBH, יכבא is far more common than יבא, but the bulk of the examples occur in speech by God (transmitted through Moses), and so are consistent with the usage of the corpus (6.1), as are other uses of first person singular forms generally. The few anomalies are insufficient to support a claim that the usage in Deuteronomy has a different basis from that of the corpus. 7.2 Other Semitic Languages

In so far as we know their languages, the peoples surrounding the earlier biblical Israel used cognates of יכנא, not cognates of ינא (Garr [1985], 79-80).27 Cognates of both appear in Akkadian and Phoeni- cian (see Aartun [1971]) but their use is marginal. The biblical usage finds its only real parallel in Ugaritic, where a long form, ank, cog- nate with יכנא, is used in texts of all kinds, and a short form, an, cog- nate with ינא, is limited to poetic texts, in which both forms are used with no evident difference in function (Segert [1984], 58; for the examples see Whitaker [1972]). Ugaritic poetry has well-known affinities of style and vocabulary with biblical poetry, one being the use of pairs of synonyms, one common, the other often uncommon in the language as a whole, in the production of the characteristic parallelism.
