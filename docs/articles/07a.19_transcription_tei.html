UNIVERSITY OF TORONTO

1. INTRODUCTION

Pausal forms are generally (probably universally) held to be conditioned by phonological features similar to the intonation patterns which mark the ends of sentences in English. If this is so, they ought to occur regularly, according to predictable patterns. Their distribution is usually described in terms of the accent system. It is true that as a general rule pausal forms occur with silluq, atnaḥ, and ‘oleb we-yored. In fact a considerable proportion of pausal forms (over 25% in some books) occurs with other accents (even conjunctive accents), and there is a small but significant number of cases in which pausal forms are not used with major pausal accents. The accent system cannot, then, either be the conditioning factor, or reflect it.1

2.2. About 75% of the pausal forms occur at the ends of clauses. Those which occur within clauses can be grouped in two basic categories: those marking the ends of equivalent units used in parallel function, and those which simply divide a clause into two parts. Equivalent units in parallel function can range from a long series of nouns in a list, to two or three prepositional phrases. In such cases a pausal form is not used after every item unless the items are unusually long or significant. 2.6. As the above examples show, this system of division appears to follow the same principles as that represented by the accents, and the two systems very often coincide. 3. THE ORIGIN OF PAUSAL FORMS: EVIDENCE FROM PHONOLOGY

It is generally agreed that pausal forms reflect the vowel patterns of a stage of the Hebrew Language somewhat earlier than that represented by the received vocalisation. That is, they aroseat some period prior to that of the masoretes.1 It is also true, although this has not been noted, that the conditions which created the difference between pausal and contextual forms had ceased to operate by the time the received vocalisation was established. In various forms showing pataḥ or segol in the stress syllable qameṣ appears in pausal position where the orginal vowel was a but, where it was i, the pataḥ or segol is usually retained in pause.2 If the two vowels had been the same when the pausal forms were developed, they would have changed in the same way. The fact that pataḥ or segol deriving from original i is not usually replaced by qameṣ in pause, shows that the creation of new pausal forms had ceased before the time when the reflex of original i in these forms became identical to that of original a, that is, before the period represented by any of the three vocalisation systems.3 A similar conclusion can be drawn from the fact that the few contextual forms which occur with atnaḥ and silluq, and the few pausal forms which occur with conjunctive accents (no doubt also the larger number which occur with minor disjunctives) also remain unchanged.4 It is clear, then, that the pausal forms were already fixed in the reading tradition when its received form was established by the masoretes. Consequently, their position in the text, and so the system of text division which they represent, must date from some earlier period.

4. THE ORIGIN OF PAUSAL FORMS: EVIDENCE FROM BIBLICAL LITERATURE

4.1. The majority of pausal forms occur at the ends of clauses, where a stop might be expected in any linguistic tradition. The cases where pausal forms are used within clauses frequently do not coincide with the conventions of other languages, so that if another tradition does use stops in a similar way, the two are likely to be related. Two cases are particularly striking. (1) A pausal form is not usually used at the end of an introduction to speech, which conflicts with the Greek and Latin convention.1 (2) Lists are usually divided into groups of two or three items. This contrasts with the accentuation of the Mishna, where a pause is usually marked after every item in a list, agreeing (whether through coincidence or influence) with the Greek convention.2

4.2. In many cases the pausal forms within a clause appear to be used for emphasis. They either draw attention to what precedes, before some modifier is added, or they separate the parts of a syntactic unit (such as the verb and its object) and so cause suspense, and emphasise what follows (see 2.3). Interjections may perform a similar function. The phrase n’m yhwh is usually used at the end of a clause, but quite often occurs as an interjection within a clause. In these cases it often occurs in positions similar to those in which pausal forms are used within clauses, and may plausibly be interpreted as having a similar function.

5. THE ORIGIN OF PAUSAL FORMS: EVIDENCE FROM THE STELE OF MESHA

5.1. The use of the punctuation mark in the inscription of Mesha is also of interest here. This mark is usually placed at the end of a clause, marking off single clauses or (more commonly) groups of two or more clauses.² As with the pausal punctuation, a division is not marked after an introduction to speech (ll. 6, 14, 24–5), nor before a relative clause (ll. 28–9), nor usually before explanatory clauses introduced by ky (ll. 3–4, 5, 27), although as in the pausal system, exceptions may occur (l. 17). 6. THE FUNCTION AND ORIGIN OF PAUSAL FORMS: CONCLUSIONS

6.1. Pausal forms in the received text appear (with rare exceptions) in positions easily acceptable as the ends of sense units, that is, at a division in the text which is either logical, or serves some special purpose, such as emphasis. The units so terminated consist of a clause, or a group of clauses or (less commonly), part of a clause. The system of text division represented by these pausal forms, that is the system of grouping phrases and clauses into sense units, appears to follow conventions similar (in some cases) to those which can be seen in the language of the prophets (as it has come down to us), and possibly even in the punctuation of the Mesha incription. It is scarcely probable that this similarity in the logic behind the division of texts shown in these different sources reflects any academic theory of punctuation, as arose from the Greek schools of rhetoric and textual criticism. It seems virtually certain that in all these cases the basis of the text division was the natural patterns of the language, the division of speech into sense units delimited by intonation patterns. Such intonation patterns, an integral part of the sound system

[Page 177]

PAUSAL FORMS IN BIBLICAL HEBREW

of the language, would naturally have been used in the oratory of the prophets, or in the reading of texts. These intonation patterns evidently became a fixed part of the biblical reading tradition, and so affected its vowel patterns, producing the pausal forms.

6.2. After this fixation had occurred the pausal forms died out of living usage, so that new pausal forms were not created where suitable vowel patterns came into being, or where a different division of the text left a contextual form at the end of a unit. The intonation patterns which gave rise to the pausal forms were evidently transmuted into, or replaced by, the musical patterns of the chant, which thus took over the function of defining the boundaries of (and divisions within) semantic units. As a result the distinction between pausal and contextual vowelling lost its function, becoming simply one of a number of types of such functionless variations preserved in the reading tradition. The masoretic lists of cases where qameṣ occurs with zaqef, or pataḥ with atnaḥ or silluq, show that the relation between the pausal forms and the accents was recognised, but the incompleteness of these lists suggests that the recognition was late. Whether this is so or not, the masoretic tradition did preserve the pausal forms faithfully even in these apparently anomalous cases, and the fact that the patterns which gave rise to them can still be recognised constitutes an important testimony to the antiquity and reliability of the masoretic tradition.

7. IMPLICATIONS FOR THE STUDY OF BIBLICAL LITERATURE

7.1. The assumption that the pausal forms of the Bible (or some of them) reflect a method of reading customary at some time before that of the Masoretes has no doubt been made by many others,1 and certainly the phenomenon of pause in Arabic would support it. It is, however, of value to have such assumptions confirmed by a detailed investigation, and this investigation has the merit of revealing the high probability of direct connection between the method of reading reflected by the pausal forms and the patterns of speech or oratory of the biblical period. This does not, of course, imply that the method of reading

[Page 178]

reflected by the pausal forms persisted unchanged from ancient times,1 but it does mean that it reflects ancient patterns of logic and expression.

7.2. The significance of the conclusion that the pausal forms reflect an early stage of the reading tradition is not limited to its interest for the history of that tradition. These forms reflect the way in which the text was commonly understood at some period prior to that of the Masoretes, and can therefore provide information significant for the history of the development of literary units, from the forms identified by scholarly analysis as original, to those of the received text.2

7.3. The vast majority of the verses in the Book of Proverbs are composed of two stichs, each ending (if the word can form one) in a pausal form. In three anomalous cases (compared to 387 with pausal forms), the first stich ends in a contextual form (xxiv. 4, 30, xxx. 21). In longer verses, however, contextual forms occur much more commonly at the end of stichs (xxiii. 5, xxx. 9, 14, 15, 16, 17 compared to 34 with pausal forms). This suggests that these longer verses were also read as composed of two stichs, as suggested by the accents, and not as three or four, as suggested by the wording.3 In the Psalms, however, pausal forms not uncommonly reflect the reading of verses as three stichs (e.g. l. 7, lvi. 9, cxvi. 16, cxxxviii. 2).

7.4. Pausal forms in poetical passages in the prophets similarly sometimes reflect a reading of these passages in individual stichs, as suggested by the wording, as in Isa. lxv. 13. However, verses 8, 9, 12, 15 and 19 of this chapter show stichs which do not end in pausal forms. Presumably, then, the pausal forms reflect a reading tradition which recognised a variety of literary forms in this section, not a poem composed throughout in stichs of more or less equal length.

7.5. The allegory in Ezek. xvi. 1–34 is written as narrative prose, with verbs standing predominantly at the beginning of

their clauses. Pausal forms occur regularly with atnaḥ and silluq (23 cases), but it is common to find other clauses ending with contextual forms (14 cases in 34 verses, 10 with zaqef). In the more direct preaching in Ezek. xvii. 11-xviii. 20, verbs are commonly placed at the ends of clauses. Clauses regularly end with pausal forms, both with atnaḥ, and silluq (11 cases), and with other accents (16 cases in 34 verses, 13 with zaqef). Pausal forms occur twice within a clause. Only four clauses end with contextual forms (one with zaqef). Clearly the use of pausal forms suggests that different styles of reading were used in these two prose sections, corresponding to their differences in literary style and content.

7.6. As long as it is held, as it is now, that the distribution of pausal forms is conditioned by the accent system except for (a small proportion of) inexplicable anomalies, the way in which these forms are used in the passages described above can be of no interest. It is the author's hope that this article has demonstrated that the distribution of pausal forms is, in fact, due to the generally accurate preservation of an ancient tradition. If so, the questions raised by the ways in which these forms occur in literary units like those described above are of considerable interest and importance to the history of biblical literature.
