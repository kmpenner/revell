Sign and sound in the study of written texts

  • 1

    1. The increased interest in speech brought about by the development of modern Linguistics has resulted in a corresponding diminution of regard for written texts as a manifestation of language. In fact a majority of linguists, at least in North America, appear to regard only speech as “real language,” and writing as an incompetent attempt to record it, giving “an imperfect and incomplete picture of actual speech.”1 The purpose of this paper is to show that this attitude reflects a distorted view of the relation of speech and writing, and has had a pernicious effect on the grammatical study of written texts.

  • 2

    2. American linguists since Bloomfield appear to have defined language as spoken.2 The admissibility of such a definition, (philologicaly impeccable, if somewhat narrow in contrast to current usage) cannot be denied. Its use, however, requires some term other than “language” for the system of items and relations represented by the sounds of speech. The term grammar is used here for this purpose. It comprehends morphemes, their arrangements (morphology and syntax) and their meanings (semantics).3 “Speech” here refers to a spoken utterance, “writing” to a written text, each, in its way, representative of the grammar. There is obviously, then, no indissoluble bond between phonemes and morphemes. A morpheme does not consist of phonemes, it is represented by them, or, to be more accurate, by sounds which, for purposes of linguistic description, are classified as allophones of phonemes.

4. If morphemes are only represented by, not composed of, phonemes, the question of whether the sounds of speech always indicate all the morphs of the grammatical structure they represent may be raised. The interest of the Firthian school in the “context of situation” is an indication that they do not. The existence of “zero morphs” and “homophones” strongly suggests that they do not. Features coming under Chomsky's heading of “deeper structure” can also be interpreted in this way. In any case coming under these headings, the normal speaker of the language will “understand” some grammatical elements in addition to those actually represented in sound. It is presumably possible to explain most such cases of “understanding” by reference to complete patterns of the grammar: e.g., paradigmatic patterns in the case of “zero morphs” or collocations in the case of homophones. The fact remains, however, that these features are not represented in sound in a particular utterance, their existence can only be deduced from comparison of a number of utterances. Speech does not, then, always indicate all the morphs of the structure it represents.

5. The conclusions about the relation of speech to the grammar can, then, be stated as follows: The sound system of a spoken language is characteristic of that language. The elements of the grammar are not, however, formed from sounds, but represented by them. The representation may be complete or incomplete according to the situation. The concept of “phoneme” is a tool designed to permit the simple and clear description of the way in which this representation is achieved.

6.1. Speech, then, is the representation of the grammar of a language in sound. Writing, according to many linguists, is an imperfect representation of speech. The “imperfection” which they see in traditional writing systems consists in their falling short of the “ideal” of phonemic transcription.7 Granted their point of view, they are right. 7.1. If writing is not an attempt at phonemic transcription, what, then, is it? R. H. Robins says “It is broadly true that one prime purpose of any writing system is to represent in permanent form the distinctive sound units or phonemes of a language,” and takes some pains to support this claim. It is not, however, really true to say that “In spellings like rough and cough, ou and gh indicate /ʌ/ and /ɔ/, and /f/.” Because of the diversity of speech patterns within a language, a given spelling will “indicate” different sounds to different readers. The reason for this is that the sounds “indicated” are not attainable directly from the written form, but only by way of the morph. Rough “indicates” /rʌf/ to Robins only because he recognizes the morph represented by the letters, and knows the conventional representation of that morph in speech. The letters themselves do not preclude other pronunciations.

7.2. What writing does, then, is represent the morphemes of a language sufficiently well to permit their recognition by the reader trained to them. This is obvious for “ideographic” writing systems, where each sign represents a morph or group of morphs. Such a system has its advantages, among them complete independence of dialect, or even language, boundaries. Their obvious disadvantage is the number of signs required. The

Writing does not represent the morpheme system only by way of the sound system. As we have seen it would be much more true to reverse this statement. Consequently, any argument that writing fails to represent many features of speech is true but irrelevant. It is also true that writing sometimes fails to represent features of the grammar that are represented in speech.24 However, the reverse is also true.25 Writing cannot, therefore, be rejected as an object of linguistic study on the grounds that it gives an inadequate representation of spoken language.


Some maintain that written language is distorted, governed by rules which are valueless because not valid for the real language of the spoken form, and pernicious because there is a tendency to hold them up as a standard for it. Nobody ever made a grammatical error in a non-literate society26 is a statement typical of this viewpoint. This is, of course, simply a fragment of linguistic mythology. Children make innumerable mistakes in learning their mother tongue. Frequently these are caused by unconscious recognition of linguistic patterns: the more common form of a morpheme is used where an uncommon allomorph is customary. Sometimes such mistakes are not dropped from childhood speech, but maintained, eventually causing the replacement of the rarer by the more common allomorph throughout the language. Such analogic replacement is a common cause of linguistic change.


Whether or no during the course of such a change either form would be considered an error depends on whether the speakers recognize an ideal standard for their language, and how firmly they maintain it. This is a matter of social custom, not of linguistic medium. Such a standard may be based on a written form of the language,27 but this is not necessarily so. In England the speech of the upper classes is the standard.28 This could not possibly be derived from written English. Among the Arabs, classical Arabic was looked on as ideal, so much so, apart from the prestige of the Qur’ān, which later became the ideal, that it had to conform to it.29 Even where some such ideal standard is not maintained, it seems to be the normal practice for the members of a community to feel that their forms are ideal, and that any differences shown by others are errors. This was true in ancient Greece and in North America. Such community speech forms can act as a written ideal.31 It may be difficult to establish whether a standard of this sort from a genuine linguistic basis, but it would be surprising if it were impossible.


It is perfectly true that the written language may use structures foreign to its spoken counterpart, as an indication that writing is bound up with myriad grotesque and useless conventions, as is conservative usage. The history of the written forms may change slowly than the spoken, or that it has its own particular rules of correctness—can be of no linguistic concern.

10.1. There are no grounds, then, for rejecting writing as a manifestation of language. Nevertheless, there is a deplorable tendency on the part of linguists, when studying written texts, to convert them from their original writing system into a so-called phonemic transcription, and so to pretend to be studying records of speech.33 It is difficult to see how this practice could be justified, even where the scholar is familiar with the phonemic system used, for, as we have seen, writing and speech frequently follow different conventions. Where the phoneme system is not well known, as will normally be the case, the method is indefensible. An interpretation in terms of sound of a set of signs which are themselves at best inexactly and probably inconsistently imitating a set of sounds must be largely subjective. The etymology of ‘king’ in Soviet Turkic languages*

IN THE MODERN TURKIC languages there are three words for a non-Muslim, European monarch: kral, kıral, korol'. Obviously these words have the same common origin. This paper is an attempt at an explanation of the etymology of the last.

Kral is the term for “king” common to all the South Slavic languages. Although it is not attested in the canon of Old Church Slavonic,1 it must have been borrowed early enough from the Frankish root karl—that is, Charlemagne—to allow it to undergo the changes common to all roots of the type TorT.2 The language known as Turkish or Osmanli borrowed this root from the conquered Balkan Slavs to denote a non-Muslim monarch. However, because of the reluctance of Turkish to admit initial consonant clusters,3 two forms are seen: kral and kıral.
