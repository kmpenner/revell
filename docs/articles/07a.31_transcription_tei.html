STRESS POSITION IN HEBREW VERB FORMS WITH VOCALIC AFFIX

E. J. REVELL UNIVERSITY OF TORONTO

Introduction

1. Where a verb form ends in an afformative consisting of a vowel (as 3fs, 3pl perfect forms, 2fs, mpl, imperfect and imperative forms) stress is always penultimate in pausal situations, where the form can be regarded as terminal in the speech unit. Elsewhere, stress is regularly final in forms in which the penultimate syllable was originally short (open with a short vowel). Where the penultimate syllable was originally long (open with a long vowel, or closed), stress in verb forms with vocalic affix is generally penultimate, but exceptions are not uncommon. The principal purpose of this paper is to show that stress position in these forms is conditioned by intonation patterns, just as it is where the penultimate syllable was short. The importance of intonation patterns for the determination of stress position has already been shown in studies of the retraction of stress (cited below as NA), and the stressing of perfect forms with waw consecutive (cited below as WC).1

2. The intonation contours which conditioned the stress position in these forms were those of the form of the biblical reading tradition which gave rise to the stress and vowel patterns. This reading tradition was intended to express the meaning of the text; consequently it reflects syntactic and other semantic relationships. Where a verb form with vocalic affix and originally short penultimate syllable is not related to what follows (terminal position, a ‘pausal’ situation), stress is penultimate. Where the form is related to what follows (medial position, a ‘contextual’ situation), stress is final. Stress position in the forms to be discussed here is determined in exactly the same way, save that the development of final stress in these forms requires a particularly close relationship to what follows.

3. It is important to note that the received accentuation arose in a form of the reading tradition somewhat different from that reflected by vowelling and stressing of the text, so that the accentuation does not necessarily reflect the relationships which determined stress position. However the two forms of reading tradition were obviously closely related, so that the accentuation has value as corroborative evidence (see NA § 1.15). The relationships which determined stress position in the biblical text can only be described in terms of clausal syntax, or of features which show relationship between clauses, as particles, or cross-referent pronouns (see NA § 2.5). Such relationships could affect stress position within a speech unit, but not across its boundaries. Speech units were delimited by the intonation contours which give rise to standard pausal forms. Relatively few words appear in distinct pausal and contextual forms, however, so that the accentuation must be used as a guide to the probable boundaries of these units. Pausal forms almost invariably occur with the accents silluq and atnāḥ, and (in the Three Books) ‘oleh we-yored, so that these accents can be taken as marking the end of speech units (although about 20 per cent of pausal forms do not occur with them). The units terminated by these accents are called ‘major units’ (see NA § 1.19.ii).2

Stress position where the verb form ends the clause

5. Where a verb form of this sort stands at the end of the clause, stress is regularly penultimate. This is true even where the verb is first of a pair, a situation in which other words often appear in medial, not in terminal form (see WC § 11.1). Examples are sārû wayyēlēkû (‘They turned and left’, Jer. 5:23), and qûmî wādôšî bat ṣiyyôn (‘Rise and thresh, daughter of Sion’, Mic. 4:13). Exceptions showing final stress are wehattû (Isa. 20:5), welāʿû (Ob. 1:16), rāšû (Ps. 34:11), and rônnî weśimḥî bat ṣiyyôn (‘Sing and rejoice, daughter of Sion’, Zech. 2:14). The case in Isa. 20:5 is the first of a pair of more or less synonymous verbs joined by a conjunction. The pair forms a major unit (cf. § 8). The case in Zech. 2:14 stands in the same situation, save that the pair of verbs is followed by a vocative related to both (cf. § 28); so also the case in Ps. 34:11, save that the pair of verbs is preceded by a noun acting as the subject of both (but cf. § 22). These cases are unique in the similarity of meaning of the two verbs, and in their close relationship.9

6. Final stress also occurs in a verb which is not followed by subject or modifiers in a few cases where an imperative form is repeated. Stress is final in both cases in which a fs imperative form is repeated and followed by a third imperative with its object, and by a vocative related to all three verbs (Isa. 51:9, 52:1; the form in question is from ʿWR in both cases). Stress is also final in Jud. 5:12, where a fs imperative form from ʿWR is repeated and followed directly by a vocative, but not in Cant. 7:1, where a fs form from ŠWB stands in the same situation. Stress is penultimate where imperative forms are repeated under other conditions, as the fs forms not followed by a vocative in

Jud. 5:12; Cant. 7:1, and also in pairs of forms from different roots, as in Thr. 4:21. Since stress is final in Jud. 5:12, but penultimate in Cant. 7:1 under conditions which seem identical, it is possible that final stressing in this situation was facilitated by phonological or semantic factors characteristic of ‘WR but not of ŠWB. The case in Ob. 1:16 appears to have been affected by rhythmic considerations. Apart from this, these forms show final stress at the end of a clause only where the verb is particularly closely related to what follows. Consequently it is reasonable to argue that such close relationship was the primary cause of the shift of stress to final position. The immediate cause was the intonation contour, which reflected the relationship.

Stress position where the verb does not end the clause

7. The description of the conditioning of stress position in verb forms which do not stand at the end of a clause necessarily involves the description of clause structure. This follows the outline given in NA § 2.2, 2.4. Verbal clauses are made up of constituents of one or more words. Besides the verb constituent, these constituents may be nominal, functioning as subject, or as a modifier in objective or other relationship, or as a vocative; prepositional (a preposition plus a nominal element); infinitival (a preposition plus an infinitive and its modifiers); or adverbial (an adverb, including nouns with adverbial affix). Two or more words or phrases having the same relationship to the verb (as nouns forming a compound object) are considered as separate constituents, whether joined by a conjunction or not. The length of a constituent is measured in words (regardless of maqqef), save that prefixed particles written with two letters are considered as part of the following word. The relationship of the verb to what precedes it is an important factor in determining stress position. This is presumably particularly close where the clause modifies a preceding noun or verb, and so forms (part of) a constituent of another clause,10 or is preceded by some constituent other than

Particles, except for the conjunction waw, are grouped together. Perfect verb forms in the Twenty-One Books: short clauses

A. The clause ends a major unit

8. Where a perfect verb form is followed by a single one-word constituent which ends a major unit, stress is usually final in the Twenty-One books, as in ṣaḥẇ mēḥālāb (Thr. 4:7; also Num. 4:14; Deut. 2:25; Isa. 23:17, 24:23, 28:7; Jer. 11:16). These are all main clauses. The subject is either different from that of the preceding clause, and follows the verb, or is given as a noun or pronoun in the preceding clause. Other pronouns used in these clauses are also familiar from the preceding context. Stress is penultimate where the verb is particularly closely related to what precedes, as in ûp'āšû pārāšāw (Hab. 1:8) where the nom-inal constituent is subject both of this verb and of the preceding (by logic, but against the accentuation); see also Ex. 13:17; Cant. 2:17 = 4:6, in the last of two or more clauses with the same subject dependent on the same particle, and Jer: 50:2, where the clause is parallel to the preceding, with a different subject. This latter case differs from those in which stress is final, in that this clause and the preceding are related by a shared pronoun, the antecedent of which is separated from this pair of clauses by a preceding pair of parallel clauses. The significance of close relationship to what precedes is also seen in the fact that stress is penultimate where the verb is preceded by a constituent of the clause (Ex. 4:7; I Sam. 19:13). Stress is similarly penultimate where the clause modifies a preceding noun or verb (Ex. 6:4; Jos. 2:22; Jud. 20:36; I Sam. 14:22). A

B. The clause does not end a major unit

11. Where a verb followed by a single one-word constituent forms a clause which begins a major unit in the Twenty-One books, stress is final only where the clause is particularly closely related to what follows, as bāz 'ā lekā lā ‘agā lekā betūlat bat ṣiyyôn (II Kgs 19:21 = Isa. 37:22) in the first of a pair of clauses followed by the subject of both. Where the relationship to what follows is not unusually close, stress is penultimate (Num. 30:8; I Kgs 8:33; Isa. 19:22; Jer. 14:4, 20:11, 37:8; Mic. 3:7). All these forms are from hollow roots; forms from geminate roots do not occur in this situation. Where the clause stands within a major unit, stress is penultimate in forms from both hollow and geminate roots (Lev. 22:9; I Sam. 16:11; Jer. 44:12, 46:21; Ez. 24:11). Stress is final in the hollow root verb of a subordinate clause within a major unit in Num. 13:32, where it does not end the main clause, but penultimate in I Sam. 14:29, where the subordinate clause does end the main clause. Where the verb is preceded by a particle, stress is final in forms from geminate roots where the clause begins a major unit (Jer. 5:6, 14:7, 46:23; these clauses are not particularly closely related to what follows).12

Perfect forms in the Twenty-One Books: the conditioning of stress position

16. The most common requirement for final stress appears to be that the verb is adequately separated from the end of the clause, or from the nearest following division of similar significance. Final stress is most common where the verb is followed by two one-word constituents, but the clause does not end a major unit. Presumably in this situation, the clause was treated as a unit, with negligible division between the constituents. At the end of a major unit, divisions between the constituents would be more marked, due to the slower tempo (see § 20). This would prevent the development of final stress. Final stress also occurs where the verb is followed by a single word or a short phrase, and the clause is closely related to what follows (§ 11, 12). Presumably any division between the verb and the following constituent, or between the clause in question and the following one, was too slight to prevent the development of final stress, because of the close relationship between the two clauses. Where relationship to what followed was not so close, one or both of these divisions was so marked that final stress could not develop. This would also be true for the division between a verb and a single longer constituent. Th

cases in I Sam. 25:10 and Lev. 18:28 noted in § 9, where the verb is followed by a constituent composed of one word plus a longer modifier, no doubt represent the same sort of situation as a two-word clause closely joined to what follows. This is probably also true of the cases where final stress is shown in a verb followed by two one-word constituents at the end of a major unit. Final stress is shown where the second of the two constituents following the verb is its subject or a vocative (§ 10). In clauses of different structure, the relationship of the constituents would not require that all three be so closely joined. Consequently final stress does not develop. Close rela- tionship to what precedes (as where the clause modifies a preceding word, or the verb is preceded by a constituent, or even a particle) also generally prevents the development of final stress. Probably where the verb was closely joined to what preceded, there was a tendency to separate it corres- pondingly from what followed, so that final stress did not develop (cf. NA § 2.49.i, iii).

17. Final stress in the perfect forms with vocalic affix in longer clauses (§ 15) can be explained as conditioned by the position of the main division of the clause: final stress occurs where the clause was not divided after the first or second word. This seems obvious in wᵉrāʾā lᵉkāl zōʾt mikkāl hārāʿā... (II Sam. 19:8) where the first two words have conjunctive accents. In the other clauses in which final stress is shown, the verb or the fol- lowing word has a disjunctive accent, but (as in the first case) the main division of the clause as marked by the accents is separated from the verb by two or more words. Where the verb begins the clause, but stress is penultimate, it can be argued that the clause had to be divided after the second word, because the next semantic unit (a pair of parallel constituents or one long constituent) was too long to be included in the initial division. Division after the second word would prevent the development of final stress just as it does where the verb or the following word ends a clause within a major unit (§ 11). The second word has a disjunctive accent in each of these clauses, but this is also true in most of the cases in which final stress is shown.

The description in WC § 23, 25.2. stresses the significance of the presence and position of the object for the conditioning of stress position in waw consecutive perfect forms with open penultimate where the verb is followed by more than two constituents. This may well be overstated since this factor does not seem significant for nesiga nor for stress position in perfect forms with vocalic affix. As far as can be determined, then, final stress occurs in these waw consecutive perfect forms with open penultimate under much the same conditions as it does in the forms with vocalic affix described above.

19. Retraction of the word stress before a word stressed on its first syllable (nesiga) also occurs where two words are closely related, but the conditions required for nesiga differ from those in which final stress is developed in that nesiga occurs only when such a pair of words is separated from what follows, a condi- tion which generally prevents the development of final stress in verb forms with vocalic affix. Consequently nesiga is generally most common under conditions most likely to prevent the development of final stress in these forms. Thus nesiga is usual in a verb followed by a single one-word constituent unless the clause is closely related to what follows (NA §2 49.ii).15 Where the verb is followed by a single constituent of more than one word, the word following the verb is rarely separated from what follows sufficiently to permit the development of nesiga (NA § 2.3). Apart from this, nesiga is least common where the verb is followed by two one-word constituents (NA §2 49.ii).

In longer clauses, nesiga is conditioned by the length and relationship of the constituents of the clause in a manner similar to that described in § 15 (NA § 2:19–22). Close relationship to what precedes is generally conducive to the development of nesiga. Presumably this increases (under most circumstances) the likelihood that the clause will be divided after the word following the verb (NA § 49.i). The evidence from nesiga, then, also generally supports the view that the development of final stress in the forms described was conditioned by the intonation contours governing the units into which the text was divided.

20. Final stress is also quite common in the verb forms discussed here in a second situation — where the verb form is followed by a single word (or sometimes by a short phrase) at the end of a major unit (§ 8, 9). This appears to conflict with the situation described in § 16. A verb form followed by a single word regularly shows nesiga in this position. Nesiga gives rise to an unstressed syllable before the terminal main stress. Presumably this was required for the full presentation of the terminal contour. Some forms retain a full vowel in an open syllable before the main word stress when they stand in terminal position but show shewa in this syllable in medial position. Presumably the terminal contour results in a slower tempo, so that the vowel is not reduced.16 Evidently the degree of reduction of tempo at the end of a major unit, combined with the fact that the intonation contour reflected the relationship of the verb to the following word, permitted the development of final stress in the verb forms described. Where the verb of a clause of this sort was closely related to what preceded, the relationship to what followed was not close enough to induce either nesiga (NA § 2.7, 2.23) or the development of final stress (§ 8). Here again, of course, the effect of the conditioning factors differs in the different cases. The development of final stress is prevented by a lower degree of relationship to what precedes than is required to prevent nesiga.

21. This survey supports the claim that the basic factor conditioning stress position in the forms with vocalic affixes described here, in waw consecutive perfect forms, and perfect forms showing nesiga, is the same: the intonation contour of the clause. The effects of this conditioning are modified, in each of the three cases, by phenomena specific to that case, namely the length and structure of the word in question, and the direction of the shift of stress in it (WC § 26, NA § 1.12–13). In the case of the verb forms with vocalic affix described above, the only significant modifying factor of this sort appears to be root structure.17

Perfect forms in the Three Books

22. The first vowelling occurs where the terminal intonation contour has preserved the original penultimate stress, and has caused change in the vowel of the stressed syllable. The third vowelling occurs where the form is closely joined in speech to what follows, so that the intonation contour has induced final stress. The second vowelling is intermediate; the intonation contour has induced neither final stress nor vowel change. 34. The shift to final stress position in forms with an origi- nally short penultimate and a vocalic affix must have occurred somewhere about the turn of the era, being apparently quite complete in the transliterations of Origen (see above). If Nesiga was a later development, and the same factors that caused nesiga blocked the shift of stress to a final vowel from a long penultimate syllable (see above § 19n. 15). To be sure, (as was not in doubt) the shift of stress to a final vowel from a long penultimate was later than that from a short penul- timate. Both shifts, however, reflect the same tendency, and they are successive stages in the same process. This means that the later stage, the shift of stress from a long penultimate syllable to a final vowel, which is investigated here, is of considerable interest, since it was crystallized before it had developed far. Thus the evidence reveals the conditions under which final stress most easily developed, and those under which this development was most easily blocked. This information is potentially of interest for the study of other forms of Hebrew in which verb forms showing a ‘full’ vowel reflecting an originally short vowel in an open syllable before a vocalic affix occur in a wider range of positions than in the standard biblical text (e.g. so-called ‘pausal forms in non-pausal positions’ found in manu- scripts of the Mishna and Piyyuṭim). The information is also likely to shed some light on the variation of stress in forms such as ’attā, lāmmā, and ’ānā. Since stress position in the cases discussed here appears to be determined by intonational factors specific to the biblical reading tradition, reflecting the syntactic- semantic relations of the word in question, it cannot be assumed that the conditioning of stress position or vowel patterns in the other cases will be found to be the same. It is to be hoped, however, that the ideas presented in this study are of some use in the understanding of the other material.
