THE TWO FORMS OF FIRST PERSON SINGULAR PRONOUN IN BIBLICAL HEBREW: REDUNDANCY OR EXPRESSIVE CONTRAST?

E.J. Revell

UNIVERSITY OF TORONTO

  1. Introduction

1.1

This paper attempts to assign a value to the use of ינא and יכנא, the two forms of free (independent) first person pronoun used in bibli- cal Hebrew. The general opinion is that they are free variants (see Waltke-O’Connor [1990], 16.3). In the discussion of the origin of the two forms in Aartun (1971) it is suggested that the longer form was developed from the shorter by the addition of an enclitic mark- ing emphasis. However, if יכנא did have an emphatic function in contrast to ינא, it had lost it by the time the biblical text was fixed as we know it. H.B. Rosen (1984) suggested that the two forms differ in some features of usage, but the pattern which he found in the Pentateuch does not fit other books well.1 Consequently there is room for a new attempt to elucidate the question on a wider basis.2

1.2

The present study sets out to describe the use of ינא and יכנא in those prose materials in which both forms commonly appear. The Book of Deuteronomy and the parts of the Pentateuch assigned to P are not included, since here the two forms are used in proportions quite differ- ent from those found in the other parts of the Pentateuch.3 The corpus

ינא is the marked’ form for a non-status speaker, used to show that the pronoun carries some meaning additional to its basic content ‘speaker’. The choice of ינא draws attention to the use of the pro- noun; it marks the clause in which it is used as particularly impor- tant to the speaker, and so deserving particular attention from the addressee. Its use is categorized in the following paragraphs, which discuss all the examples in the basic corpus of the use of ינא by non- status speakers.

3.1 Self-Advancement

ינא is often used where the speaker is volunteering for (or requesting, or claiming) a position, as in ‘I will go down with you’ (1 Sam. 26:6), ‘Let me run also’ (2 Sam. 18:22), ‘I will be king’ (1 Kgs 1:5), ‘I will trick him’ (1 Kgs 22:21). ינא is similarly used where the pred- icate of the clause represents a condition or quality which gives the speaker some claim, as ‘I being a great people’ (and so deserving a larger share, Jos. 17:14); ‘I am your flesh and blood’ (so your sup- port of me will be mutually profitable, Judg. 9:2); ‘I am a widow woman’ (and so deserve your protection, 2 Sam. 14:5). ינא is also used in this way in assertions like ‘I will be your servant’ (2 Sam. 15:34 [three cases]; also 1 Kgs 18:36; 2 Kgs 16:7. Cf. the question in 2 Sam. 16:19). This is a declaration of fealty, but also a claim to the protection and support of the master (see Lindhagen, 1950, 56). Other examples of the use of ינא in asserting a claim occur in Judg. 16:17, 2 Sam. 19:44 (possibly with defective wording), and 1 Kgs 13:18, also the boasts in Gen. 14:23, 1 Sam. 17:10, and the claim to knowledge in 2 Kgs 2:3, 5. Probably Joab’s reply to the wise woman of Abel Meholah in 2 Sam. 20:17 (asserting a claim to the identity of Joab, David’s commander in chief) should be included.

3.1.1 Where the clause ascribes to the speaker some characteristic or quality which is distasteful or shameful, or is a disqualification for some position, יכנא is used, as ‘I was afraid, because I (יכנא) was naked’ (Gen. 3:10). 3.2 Emotion

3.2.1 A non-status speaker may use ינא or יכנא in a clause of self- identification, (a clause with a first person singular pronoun as sub- ject, and a phrase which designates the speaker by name or social position, or by relationship to other individuals, or groups, or even to events, as predicate). ינא is used in Gen. 27:24, where Jacob asserts for a second time (in response to his father’s evident suspicion), that he is Esau, and in Gen. 27:32, where Esau responds to his father’s question ‘Who are you?’ — a question which Esau’s preceding speech should have rendered unnecessary. ינא is also used by Hannah in 1 Sam. 1:26 where she identifies herself to Eli by recalling their former meeting. In all three cases, the speech is of unusual impor- tance to the speaker. Jacob must prevent discovery of his deception; Esau must prove his identity in the face of unexpected scepticism. Hannah’s strong interest in her speech is evident from her use of two vocatives and a formula of asseveration to introduce it. The use of ינא can, then, be seen as reflecting emotion. Where such strong feeling is not reflected, a non-status speaker uses יכנא in clauses of this sort, as does Jacob where he first introduces himself with ‘I am Esau your firstborn’ in Gen. 27:19.15

3.3 Emphasis

In other cases the use of ינא seems to have no connotations beyond the desire to draw attention to the pronoun. A status-marked speaker may use non-status language to suggest inti- macy with a non-status addressee21. In some cases, such usage forms part of the rhetoric of persuasion.   1. ינא and יכנא in Human Speech, Conclusions

5.1

Where the use of ינא and יכנא is classified in terms of the status of the speaker relative to the addressee, a regular pattern appears. This is clearest where the speaker is status-marked. Such speakers typically use ינא. They use יכנא in little more than quarter of the possible cases. They can use it to show that the status difference between the mem- bers of the dyad is being ignored to influence the addressee, to show humility or politeness, or to show that the clause functions as ‘back- ground’. Non-status speakers usually use יכנא, but use ינא in over one third of the cases. It is used to put forward a claim of some sort, an obvious counterpart to the use of יכנא by a status-marked speaker showing humility. The use of ינא by a non-status speaker often reflects emotion or strong feeling, a similar usage since this also draws the speaker’s concerns forcefully to the attention of the addressee. The use of ינא by non-status speakers to mark a clause as significant is the counterpart to the use of יכנא by status-marked speakers to show that a clause is background. The most common situation in which ינא is used in this category, to heighten a contrast or other shift in topic, is characteristically marked by other ‘foregrounding’ devices in other situations: the use of a subject placed before the verb, of a free pronominal subject in a verbal clause, etc. Thus, although the recognition of any particular clause as ‘significant’ is, in isolation, arbitrary, comparison of usage can supply considerable support for such recognition. The use of ינא and יכנא by human speakers can, then, be seen as a self-consistent system which corresponds, on occa- sion, to features of other systems with similar functions.

5.2

In general, then, the use of ינא by humans shows either that the speaker is status-marked, or that the clause is ‘immediate’, is central

The use of יכנא marks the speaker as non-status, or shows that the clause is ‘non-immediate’, peripheral to the speaker’s concerns, unimpor- tant. Relative status and degree of personal concern are not really distinct alternatives. The concerns of a status-marked speaker are perforce of interest to an addressee of lower status. The concerns of a non-status speaker can only be presented to a superior as ‘imme- diate’ where the speaker’s emotion, or feeling of urgency, overrides respect for the addressee’s status. Several other features are used in a similar way. The position of the vocative, and the opposition between personal pronouns and deferential forms, have a range of function similar to that suggested for these pronoun forms (see 2.1-2). The treatment of a collective as plural marks the clause of which it is subject as central to the concerns of the speaker or nar- rator; the treatment of it as singular marks it as non-central (see Revell [1993b]). There is, then, a considerable network of features used to mark the relative significance of individual clauses, a network which supplements the information provided by the grammatical marking of foreground and background (see n. 6). The two forms of first person singular pronoun are used as part of this network.

The use of יכנא usual in speech directed by God to a human can be interpreted as the sort of condescension shown by a status-marked human who uses יכנא as a form of politeness (4.2). It seems quite likely that, in this feature, the speech of God may reflect that of human parents, who are unlikely (as usage in other languages, such as French and German, suggests) to use status language to small children. יכנא is also used in speech ascribed to God, as in that of status-marked humans, to mark clauses as ‘background’. יבא is used where the use of יכנא is not required for the purposes described.   1. The Wider Context

7.1 Biblical Hebrew

A set of distinguishing features allows the division of biblical Hebrew into an early’ form (EBH) and a ‘late’ form (LBH). In LBH, the only first person singular pronoun form used is יבא, as is the case in all later Hebrew (apart from quotation or imitation of the biblical language). The ‘P’ material of the Pentateuch (the characteristics of which are carefully studied in Hurwitz [1982]) represents LBH; the failure to use יכבא is characteristic of it. In the Book of Deuteron- omy, which represents EBH, יכבא is far more common than יבא, but the bulk of the examples occur in speech by God (transmitted through Moses), and so are consistent with the usage of the corpus (6.1), as are other uses of first person singular forms generally. The few anomalies are insufficient to support a claim that the usage in Deuteronomy has a different basis from that of the corpus. 7.2 Other Semitic Languages

In so far as we know their languages, the peoples surrounding the earlier biblical Israel used cognates of יכנא, not cognates of ינא (Garr [1985], 79-80).27 Cognates of both appear in Akkadian and Phoeni- cian (see Aartun [1971]) but their use is marginal. Ugaritic poetry has well-known affinities of style and vocabulary with biblical poetry, one being the use of pairs of synonyms, one common, the other often uncommon in the language as a whole, in the production of the characteristic parallelism.
