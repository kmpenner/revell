1.1 For the purposes of this study, collectives are defined as nouns which are morphologically singular, but which may have singular or plural coreferents. A coreferent of a collective is commonly described as showing “grammatical” concord when singular, “notional” concord (constructio ad sensum) when plural. If this is taken as indicating that concord is determined either by linguistic convention (a noun which is singular in form has singular coreferents) or by the speaker’s perception of reality (the plural is used to make reference to a collection of individ­ uals), a difficulty appears. 1.2 There is regularity in the choice of number in the coreferents of collectives in one limited area. An attributive which presents a quality of the group designated by the collective (and not of the individuals which form it) is singular. As described, the basis of this is semantic, so the value of the term “grammatical” for such concord is questionable. Equally, one would expect that a speaker would be led to think of a group performing particular actions as a number of individuals rather than as a unit, and so to the “notional” use of a plural verb where the subject was a collective. It is true that an active verb with a collective subject is often plural, but there is no group of verbs which are charac­ teristically plural when the subject is collective. This might be held to show that the “notional” plural is a free variant of the “grammatical” singular, were it not that the use of the latter is, in one area at least, clearly conditioned.

1.3 This paper is an attempt to determine what the terms “grammatical” and “notional” represent when used in the way indicated. It is based on the books of Judges, Samuel, and Kings, but is limited to collectives which denote humans. This corpus provides some 400 cases in which a collective is subject of a verb, or is modified by an attributive, and so is marked for number, a sample large enough to provide a satisfactory basis for conclusions. 2.2 The names in group (1) represent not only population groups, but also their eponymous ancestors. The groups themselves are often desig- nated as the descendants (םינב) of these ancestors, as ‘the descendants of Israel’, etc. The existence of plural designations equivalent to these collectives is no doubt the reason why the latter are usually treated as singular.9 Equivalent collective and plural designations of this sort do not appear to be free variants. The collective forms act as subject of a clause in less than 10% of their occurrences, the equivalent plurals in 50% or more. For instance ןימינב ‘Benjamin’ as a collective, acts as subject in the corpus in 4 of 48 possible cases (8%), ינב ‘the descendants of Benjamin’ in 12 of 22 cases (55%). There is, then, a relationship between the representation of a group as “actor” (subject) and the choice of plural number.
