Hebrew Accents and Greek Ekphonetic Neumes At the end of the first millennium, Biblical (and other) texts in both Hebrew and Greek were frequently provided with ‘accent’ signs1 to regulate the chanting of the text. The demonstration of relation­ ship between Christian and Jewish liturgical music has led to various attempts to discover a relationship between these accent systems. The most recent of these, Gudrun Engberg’s article ‘Greek Ekphonetic Neumes and Masoretic Accents’ supports the idea of a close musical relationship between the systems, and sug­ gests that the thesis there put forward be further investigated.2 The present article arises from an attempt to follow this suggestion from the viewpoint of a hebraist.

Direct comparison of the accent systems is possible because, as far as we can tell, the use and function of the accents was the same both in Greek and in Hebrew texts. These texts were divided up into units — a word or a group of words — each of which was marked by an accent sign: at the end in Hebrew, at both beginning and end in Greek. Each accent had a ‘musical value’ in that it indicated the cadence to which the textual unit was to be chanted.3 Each ac­ cent also had an ‘interpunctional value’ in that it indicated the re­ lationship between that textual unit and the others in the context. The ‘major pausal accents’ silluq, atnah, and segolta, which mark the end of the main divisions of a verse, far from being the earliest accents known and the most commonly marked, are, in fact, the most frequently omitted, and the latest to be marked with specific signs (see below). There is, moreover, no evidence of development, in the Hebrew system, from one system using a few signs to mark punctuation towards another using many signs to mark music. There is only evidence of development in the detail and accuracy of the marking of the same phenomena. The system in which the signs are used does not change. This would seem to hold true for all Hebrew systems, whether used in Biblical or Rabbinic texts, and is probably true for the Greek system also. The Greek accent signs, it is true, may well have developed from punctuation signs, but I can see no evidence of a smooth process of evolution. I would assume that a system of signs intended to mark musical phenomena was intentionally created, using, in some cases, the same signs as were used as punctuation markers.

It is, then, assumed in this paper that the basic function of the accent signs was musical, and that their function as markers of re­ lationship was secondary.9 The signs are, moreover, regarded, not as individual entities with an intrinsic meaning, but as items in a system outside of which they are meaningless. The various types of accentuation are compared on the basis of the systems thus dis­ played, not on the basis of a particular text which they are used to punctuate. It is, after all, clearly true in all systems that the accen­ tuation, in showing the relationship of the units of text to each other, shows which are to be emphasized, and to what extent. THE HEBREW BIBLICAL ACCENTS

/. General The basic unit of the Hebrew Biblical accentuation10 is, of course, the verse. The length of a verse may vary from a few words to many, and the syntactic structure relating those words from a part of a clause to a group of clauses. Nevertheless, the accentuation of each verse follows the same basic pattern, and is autonomous — inde­ pendent of anything in the preceding or following verses.

Averse is normally divided into two halves. The end of the first

half is marked by atnah, and the end of the second (and of the whole verse) by silluq. Excepting this feature (and the use of segol· tn, see below) the two halves have the same possibilities of accentu­ ation. They are composed of a terminal clause which may be pre­ ceded by one or more contextual clauses. The terminal clause is ac­ cented tebir־־* tiflia ־+ atnah/silluq, with tebir omitted from shorter clauses. In longer clauses, the minor accents from the contextual clause (rebia etc.) are used to mark divisions preceding tebir. The end of the contextual clause is marked with zaqef. This may be the only accent in the contextual clause, but most commonly it is pre­ ceded by pash^a. Divisions preceding pashta are marked by rebia, wesh, pazer, legarmeh, or telisha, selected and arranged according to the requirements of the situation. More than one contextual clause may be used in one verse half. In the first verse half, the first of two or more contextual clauses may be made to stand out by the use of zarqa and segolta instead of pashta and zaqef to mark the last two phrases. A typical verse, then, is divided into two halves, each of which is composed of one contextual and one terminal clause (e. g. Lam. 1:1,2). More than one contextual clause may occur in the first half (Lam. 1:3,4), the second half (Lam. 1:10) or both halves (Lam. 1:7), and, if required, the first of two or more contextual clauses in the first verse half may be marked out by the use of zarqa-segolta (Lam. 1:12). Either verse half may be composed only of a terminal clause (Lam. 1:3,4, 5, second half, 1:6 first half). If the verse is unusually short, it is not divided into halves by atnah. It may then be composed of one contextual and one terminal clause (Lam. 3:1), or simply of one terminal clause (Lam. 3:2, 3).

CONCLUSION

This brief survey suggests that, although the practice of chanting Biblical (and other) texts was current throughout the Judaeo- Christian world, several different forms of chant were in use. One of these, the Hebrew Biblical chant, appears to have been unique. It could be argued that the halving of Syriac pasoqa units reflects a common basis, but the age of the Hebrew system, and the lack of any parallels in the Syriac system beyond the simple division into two parts, appear to deny this as a possibility. The Syriac system, it seems to me, should be classified with the Greek and Rabbinic systems, all of which are characterized by relatively short contextual clauses with relatively simple accentuation.
