Introduction

1. This paper explores the factors which determine the order of the constituents in the standard two-part verbless clause and its variations in the books of Judges, Samuel, and Kings.1 Little space is given to these clauses in grammars, and concen- tration on verbal clauses has tended to obscure the fact that a number of features of structure are common to clauses of both types. The same structures act as constituents of both types of clause, apart, of course, from the verbal constituent which characterizes verbal clauses. For present purposes these con- stituents can be classified simply as nominal or adverbial. Any noun or noun equivalent (including participles, adjectives, pronouns, or a phrase or clause functioning as the equivalent of a noun) can function as a nominal constituent. An adverb, or a preposition followed by a nominal, or by an infinitive and its modifiers, functions as an adverbial constituent.

2. Verbless clauses differ from verbal clauses (apart from the use of the verb) chiefly in the number of constituents used. Verbal clauses often have, beside the verb and its subject, several constituents which modify the verb, and are related to each other only through their relationship to the verb. Verbless

clauses are typically composed only of two constituents, which are in some way equated by the structure. There is an intermediary form of clause, in which this equating is marked by a form of the verb HYH. Such clauses are structurally verbal clauses, and may include more than one constituent besides the verb and its subject, as

  • 1 Sam. 3:1 wdbr yhwh hyh yqr bymym hhm
  • 1 Sam. 14:38 bmh hyth bhṭ’t hz’t hywm
  • 2 Sam. 19:23 thyh ly hywm lśṭn

Structures which can be analysed as two-part verbless clauses occasionally also show, in one of the parts, two or more phrases which do not appear to be directly related to each other, but are related only through the structure of the clause. In

  • 1 Sam. 27:11 Kh ‘śh dwd wkh mšpṭw kl hymym ’šr yšb bsdh plštym

the phrase kl hymym ... is related to the second (verbless) clause just as much as to the first (verbal) one. This verbless clause has, then, a predicate composed of two phrases which are not directly related to each other. Other examples of composite predicates of this sort are

  • 1 Sam. 9:12 zbḥ hywm l‘m bbmh
  • 2 Sam. 3:12 ydy ‘mk lhsb ’lyk ’t kl yśr’l
  • 1 Kgs 10:22 ’ny tršyš lmlk bym

There are various possibilities for the description of these clauses, but analysis as a verbless clause with a predicate composed of two or more phrases seems the best. It seems clear that the use of two or more constituents beside the subject does not constitute an absolute difference between verbal and verbless clauses.

3. The constituents of a verbal clause are commonly said to appear in a standard order, but this order is ‘standard’ only in the sense of being the most common. In principle, any constituent may stand in any position. Their order is conditioned by semantic considerations, not by syntax. The constituent placed first in the clause is that which the author wishes to be the primary focus of the reader’s attention. Thus in a narrative passage, there the story is treated as a sequence of events, the verb, which presents the event, typically stands first in each

clause. Where the participants change, however, as where a new subject is introduced, the author will often draw the change to the reader’s attention by placing the subject first in the clause. Similarly, clauses in which the verb does not stand first are often used at the beginning of a narrative, to present the main participant, or the time or location of the action, since, at the beginning of a narrative, this information is often of greater significance than that conveyed by the verb.2 For similar reasons, verb initial clauses are much less common in quoted speech than in narrative. Quoted speech constantly introduces new topics, and rarely includes long stretches of sequential action.3

4. The order of the constituents which follow the verb in a verbal clause is also conditioned by semantic considerations. The standard order represents the common gradation of the significance of the constituents in relation to the action: subject

The suffix refers (if first or second person) to the participants in the dialogue, or (if third person) to an antecedent in the preceding context, and so is of prime significance for the relation of the clause to its context. For the same reason anaphoric adverbs, such as šm also usually stand immediately after the verb. Other variations in the standard order are equally semantically conditioned, as was shown by Lars Lode.4

The simple two-part verbless clause

5. The basic form of verbless clause in Hebrew is composed of two parts: subject (the topic of the clause) and predicate (the information supplied on that topic). These are semantic classifications; neither subject nor predicate is consistently identified by any formal marker in any type of clause. Nevertheless, an adverbial, or a nominal headed by a participle or adjective, can confidently be identified as the predicate where the other constituent is an ordinary nominal. In such clauses, the standard (or most common) order is subject – predicate, which occurs in some sixty per cent of the cases where the predicate is adverbial, and in some eighty-five per cent of the cases where the predicate is headed by a participle or adjective. Consequently the description below concentrates on the cases in which the predicate precedes the subject. Where both constituents are nominal, but neither is headed by a participle or adjective (or if both are), the identification of the subject is more problematical, so a more general description is given. However it still appears that the subject precedes the predicate in a majority of cases. In verbal clauses, too, the subject typically precedes all other constituents but the verb in a verb-initial clause, and is by far the most common initial constituent where the verb does not stand first. This, of course, reflects the fact that the subject is generally the most significant constituent in a verbal clause (save for the verb in consecutive narrative)

Some of these are illustrated below, but much detailed study of clauses in their context is needed before a satisfactory categorization can be made.5

6. Where a clause is composed of a nominal constituent (the subject) and an adverbial constituent (the predicate), the factors conditioning the order of these constituents can be clearly seen where the clause is interrogative.

  • (1) Where the adverbial constituent is an interrogative particle, it begins the clause, as
    • Jud. 6:13 wʾyh kl nplʾtyw
    • 1 Sam. 19:22 ʾyph šmwʾl wdwd
    • 2 Sam. 19:35 kmh ymy šny hyy
    Interrogative particles typically stand at the beginning of a clause in Hebrew — possibly another manifestation of the general principle that the most significant item in a clause is placed first.
  • (2) Where the nominal constituent is interrogative (my or mh) this naturally takes first position. Where he interrogative introduces a question, it is prefixed to the constituent representing the item which is the concern of the question, and this is placed first, as the adverbial constituent in

    • 1 Sam. 9:11 hyš bzh hrʾh
    • 2 Sam. 9:3 hʾps ʿwd ʾyš lbyt šʾwl

10. Where one constituent of a clause is a pronoun, and the other is also nominal (but is not headed by a participle or adjective), it is again clear that the constituent which is the more significant in the context is placed first. This significance can be expressed as follows. A pronominal subject which precedes its predicate designates the particular individual or group (out of those possible) to which the predicate is appli- cable. Where the pronoun follows the predicate, the clause shows that that predicate (as opposed to other possible predi- cates) is applicable to the individual or group designated by the pronoun.

11. This is easily seen in clauses dealing with identity. (1) If the clause indicates which individual (out of a number of possibilities) has a particular identity, then the individual is named first. Thus in

1 Kgs 20:28 (wyd’tm ky) ’ny yhwh

the concern is not that people do not know the name of God. The message conveyed is ‘You will realize that I (the speaker and no-one else) am the Lord (with all the power and authority that that implies)’. Similarly in

1 Sam. 28:12 (lmh rmytny) w’th š’wl

the witch of Endor is not simply stating that the man’s name is Saul; she means that (although she had never suspected it) this individual is in fact Saul, (whose decree she has flouted). (2) Where there is a question of whether an individual has a particular identity, the question similarly places the pronominal subject first (cf. section 6.2), as

  • Jud. 13:11 h’th h’yš ’šr dbrt ’l h’šh
  • 2 Sam. 9:2 h’th ṣybh

The answer often presents only the significant initial part of the clause (the subject), and so in Jud.13:11 ’ny, in 2 Sam.9:2 ‘bdk. (3) A question asking what is the identity of a particular individual is typically put with an interrogative pronoun. The identity of the individual is placed first in the response, which may consist only of this significant initial part of the clause (the predicate), as

  • 1 Sam.17:58 bn my ’th hn‘r ... bn ‘bdk yšy
  • 2 Sam.1:8 my ’th ... ‘mlqy ’nky
  • 2 Kgs 10:13 my ’tm ... ’ḥy ’ḥzyhw ’nḥnw
  • cf. also 2 Kgs 1:7-8 mh mšpṭ h’yš ... ’lyhw htšby hw’

The particular form in which the identity is given varies, as these examples show. The form is no doubt partly responsive to the question, and partly a matter of social convention. The significant fact is that where the identity is given first, the clause answers the question ‘What is the identity of the individual represented by the pronoun?’ Where the pronoun is placed first, the clause answers the question ‘What individual has the identity named?’8

12. The same considerations govern the use of pronouns in other verbless clauses. Where the pronoun stands first, it is the more significant of the constituents, indicating the particular entity to which the predicate is applicable. Thus the demonstrative pronouns zh, z’t, etc., typically stand first in the clause, as

  • 2 Kgs 6:19 l’ zh mdrk wl’ zh h‘yr

Other pronouns are also placed first where they mark important relationship to the preceding context (see section 4), as

  • 1 Sam. 1:10 (wtqm ḥnh ...) why’ mrt npš
  • 2 Kgs 14:21 (wyqḥw ... ’t ‘zryh) whw’ bn šš ‘śrh šnh

A pronoun which follows the predicate is the less significant of the two constituents. The constrasting value of the two orders is neatly displayed in

13. Where both constituents are nominal, but neither is a pronoun, or is headed by a participle or adjective, the same principles can be seen to govern their arrangement. Typically, one constituent designates an individual, item, or group, and the other some attribute of that individual, item, or group. The latter, the predicate, usually stands in second position. The following are some characteristic examples of features presented in this way.

(1) Name. The standard order ‘and his name was PN’ is used constantly, as Jud. 13:2, 17:1, 2 Sam. 20:1, and in the formula naming a new king’s mother, as 1 Kgs 14:21, 31, etc. The reverse order is only used where the name is of particular significance in the context, as

2 Sam. 20:21 (ʾyš mhr ʾprym) šbʿ bn bkry šmw (nśʾ ydw) ...

The speaker is explaining that the capture of Sheba ben Bichri is the sole aim of his expedition. Other examples occur with Nabal (1 Sam. 25:25), where the name is the subject of a pun, and with outstanding individuals: Goliath in 1 Sam. 17:4, 23, and Josiah in 1 Kgs 13:2.

(2) Age. The standard order is exemplified in

1 Sam. 4:15 wʿly bn tšʿym wšmnh šnh

This standard order is reversed in the formulaic clauses giving the king’s age on his accession, where the age is of particular significance, as 2 Sam. 2:10, 5:4, etc.

Clauses with discontinuous constituents

14. The words forming a composite constituent of a verbal clause, as a noun with modifiers, or two or more nouns co-ordinated or in apposition, usually follow each other without interruption. They may, however, be interrupted by the insertion of other constituents. Technically a nominal subject (which stands in apposition to the pronominal morph in the verb form) forms a discontinuous constituent when it is separated from its verb. The purpose of such discontinuity is to allow the insertion, directly after the verb, of material more immediately significant for the understanding of the clause than is the description of the entity designated by the subject pronoun (see section 4). However a subject of this sort is only

the most common type of discontinuous constituent used in verbal clauses.

  • (1) Where an object, or other constituent, follows the verb, its head may be presented in its standard position, but separated from its modifiers by other constituents, as
    • Jud. 14:1 wyr’ ’šh b-tmnth m-bnwt plštym
  • (2) A constituent which is treated in this way is sometimes extraposed. The head of the constituent is given in the standard position, but the whole constituent is given at the end of the clause. The head may be repeated, as
    • Jud. 10:8 wyrṣṣw ’t bny yśr’l b-šnh hhy’ ... ’t kl bny yśr’l ...
    or it may be represented by a pronoun, as
    • Jud. 9:48 wy’l ’bymlk hr ṣlmwn hw’ w-kl h-’m ’šr ’tw
  • (3) A constituent which is placed before the verb to draw attention to its significance may be treated in the same way. Where such a constituent is composed of several co-ordinated nouns, or of a noun with modifiers, it may be given in discontinuous form, as
    • Jud. 18:24 ’t ’lhy ’šr śyty lqḥtm w-’t h-khn
    • 1 Sam. 30:5 w-šty nšy dwd nśbw ’hyn’m ... w-’bygyl
  • (4) A constituent preceding the verb may also be extraposed – given in full at the beginning of the clause, and represented in the standard position by a pronoun, as
    • Jud. 7:5 kl ’šr ylq b-lšwnw ... tṣyg ’wtw lbd

16. In these clauses, the purpose of the discontinuity is the same as in verbal clauses. A constituent is interrupted to allow the insertion of material in a position which gives due prominence to its significance. The discontinuity of the predicate in these cases results in the presentation of the semantic components of the clause in the order characteristic of verbal clauses with initial verb. This is strikingly demonstrated where the perfect of HYH is used with the participle, forming a composite verbal constituent, in

  • 1 Sam. 17:34 rʿh hyh ʿbdk lṣʾnw bṣʾn
The head of the predicate, whether it be participle, adjective, or verb, is followed by its subject, and then by its modifiers, because this is the usual order of relative significance of the components of a clause in which the head of the predicate is the most significant constituent in the context, and so stands first in the clause. In verbal clauses this ‘standard’ order is the most common of the possible variations in word order only in a narrative context, because only there is the verb usually the most significant constituent of the clause (see section 3 and note 4). In the ‘standard’ order for the components of a verbless clause with predicate headed by a participle or adjective, the predicate follows the subject and is not discontinuous. The order described above, where the head of the predicate precedes the subject, is the most common variation on this. Other variations, also invoving discontinuous predicates, are described in sections 17-19. Discontinuous predicates in other types of clause, which are less common, are described in section 20, and discontinuous subjects in section 21.

21. The subject of any type of verbless clause can be discontinuous, as can the subject of a verbal clause (see section 14). Commonly, the headword of the subject is placed first, and is followed by the predicate. Other parts of the subject, such as co-ordinate nouns, or attributive or other modifiers, follow the predicate. Examples are, where the parts of the subject are co-ordinate

Such order may also be used simply to present the structure of the clause more clearly than would be the case where a short predicate follows a long and complex subject (see section 22.5).

Negatives and Affirmatives: ’yn, yš, and l’

25. Negatives and affirmatives have been ignored in the above discussion. They have no effect on the order of the constituents in these clauses, but they do throw some light on the way it is conditioned.
(1) ’yn and yš are common only in clauses with adverbial predicate. Where the predicate is nominal, ’yn and yš occur only with an initial subject, usually a pronoun. Where the clause has an adverbial predicate, ’yn and yš usually stand before the first part of the clause, but may stand before the second.11 In either position they occur before the predicate somewhat more commonly than before the subject. The placing of ’yn and yš is determined by semantic considerations, as is the order of the parts of the clause. 26. The placing of ’yn and yš provides a further demonstration of the importance of semantic factors in determining word order in verbless clauses. The dominant factor determining the order of the constituent of these clauses – and even the components of constituents – is the significance in the context of the information which they present. The examples above have been chosen to show an easily appreciable difference in the significance of a constituent when it stands first from when it does not, so as to make this point as clearly as possible.

Variation in word order can, however, be used to convey quite delicate nuances, as is shown in the following contrasting pairs.

  • (1) 1 Sam. 20:5 ḥdš mḥr (w’nky yšb ’šb ‘m hmlk)
    ‘It is New Moon tomorrow – and (because it is New Moon) I must sit with the king.’ The significant fact here is the New Moon. Because of this, David’s presence is required at the meal.
    1 Sam. 20:18 mḥr ḥdš (wnpqdt ky ypqd mwšbk)
    ‘Tomorrow is New Moon – and (tomorrow) you will be missed (because you must sit with the King),’ The significant fact here is the time element. The crisis will occur tomorrow.
  • (2) 2 Kgs 9:36 dbr yhwh hw’
    This clause emphasizes the fact that the event was foretold. It stands in a quoted speech by Jehu, within which there is no antecedent for the pronoun (although the fate of Jezebel, to which it refers, has just been described by the narrator).
    2 Kgs 15:12 hw’ dbr yhwh
    This clause is a narrator’s comment. The pronoun is placed first to highlight the relationship to the preceding context (see section 12) in which the death of Zechariah, to which the pronoun refers, has just been described.

    The predicate-subject order lays stress on important attributes of David and his men which must be taken into account in the plans being discussed.

Where the negative is applied to the subject, stressing the lack of a king, the clause precedes the statement that everyone did what he wanted (Jud. 17:6, 21:25), and the statment that the tribe of Dan had to look for a new territory (Jud. 18:1), problems which resulted from the absence of a central authority. 27. It seems quite clear, then, that the main factor determining the order in which the constituents of a verbless clause are presented was the significance – for the narrator – of these constituents, or of their components, in the particular context. The exact interpretation of this significance is a matter of subjective speculation, but a detailed analysis and comparison of the clauses leaves no doubt that this significance was the main factor determining word order. This is the factor called by Geoffrey Leech ‘Thematization: the organizing of the elements of a message so that weight and emphasis fall in the appropriate place.’13 The same factor determines the order of the constituents of verbal clauses, resulting in the many features of structure common to both verbal and verbless clauses.
