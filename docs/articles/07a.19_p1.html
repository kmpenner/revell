I.INTRODUCTION

Pausal forms are generally (probably universally) held to be conditioned by phonological features similar to the intonation patterns which mark the ends of sentences in English. If this is so, they ought to occur regularly, according to predictable patterns. Their distribution is usually described in terms of the accent system. It is true that as a general rule pausal forms occur with silluq, atnab, and ‘oleh we-yored. In fact a considerable pro­ portion of pausal forms (over 25% in some books) occurs with other accents (even conjunctive accents), and there is a small but significant number of cases in which pausal forms are not used with major pausal accents. The accent system cannot, then, either be the conditioning factor, or reflect it.1

  1. THE ORIGIN OF PAUSAL FORMS:

EVIDENCE FROM PHONOLOGY

It is generally agreed that pausal forms reflect the vowel patterns of a stage of the Hebrew Language somewhat earlier than that represented by the received vocalisation. That is, they aroseat some period prior to that of the masoretes.1 It is also true, although this has not been noted, that the conditions which created the difference between pausal and contextual forms had ceased to operate by the time the received vocalisation was established. In various forms showing patab or segol in the stress syllable qames appears in pausal position where the orginal vowel was “a” but, where it was “i”, the patah or segol is usually retained in pause.2 If the two vowels had been the same when the pausal forms were developed, they would have changed in the same way. The fact that patab or segol deriving from original “i” is not usually replaced by qames in pause, shows that the creation of new pausal forms had ceased before the time when the reflex of original “i” in these forms became identical to that of original “a”, that is, before the period represented by any of the three vocalisation systems.3 A similar conclusion can be drawn from the fact that the few contextual forms which occur with atnah and silluq, and the few pausal forms which occur with conjunctive accents (no doubt also the larger number which occur with minor disjunctives) also remain unchanged.4 It is clear, then, that the pausal forms were already fixed in the reading tradition when its received form was established by the masoretes. Consequently, their position in the text, and so the system of text division which they represent, must date from some earlier period.

  1. THE ORIGIN OF PAUSAL FORMS:

EVIDENCE FROM BIBLICAL LITERATURE

4.1. The majority of pausal forms occur at the ends of clauses, where a stop might be expected in any linguistic tradition. The cases where pausal forms are used within clauses frequently do not coincide with the conventions of other languages, so that if another tradition does use stops in a similar way, the two are likely to be related. Two cases are particularly striking. (1) A pausal form is not usually used at the end of an introduction to speech, which conflicts with the Greek and Latin convention. 1 (2) Lists are usually divided into groups of two or three items. This contrasts with the accentuation of the Mishna, where a pause is usually marked after every item in a list, agreeing (whether through coincidence or influence) with the Greek 2 convention.

4.2. In many cases the pausal forms within a clause appear to be used for emphasis. They either draw attention to what precedes, before some modifier is added, or they separate the parts of a syntactic unit (such as the verb and its object) and so cause suspense, and emphasise what follows (see 2.3). Inter­ jections may perform a similar function. The phrase n'm yhwb is usually used at the end of a clause, but quite often occurs as an interjection within a clause. In these cases it often occurs in positions similar to those in which pausal forms are used within clauses, and may plausibly be interpreted as having a similar function.

  1. THE FUNCTION AND ORIGIN OF PAUSAL

FORMS: CONCLUSIONS

  1. i. Pausal forms in the received text appear (with rare excep­ tions) in positions easily acceptable as the ends of sense units, that is, at a division in the text which is either logical, or serves some special purpose, such as emphasis. The units so terminated consist of a clause, or a group of clauses or (less commonly), part of a clause. The system of text division represented by these pausal forms, that is the system of grouping phrases and clauses into sense units, appears to follow conventions similar (in some cases) to those which can be seen in the language of the prophets (as it has come down to us), and possibly even in the punctuation of the Mesha incription. It is scarcely probable that this similarity in the logic behind the division of texts shown in these different sources reflects any academic theory of punctuation, such as arose from the Greek schools of rhetoric and textual criticism. It seems virtually certain that in all these cases the basis of the text division was the natural patterns of the language, the divi­ sion of speech into sense units delimited by intonation patterns. 6 .2. After this fixation had occurred the pausal forms died out

The intonation patterns which gave rise to the pausal forms were evidently transmuted into, or replaced by, the musical patterns of the chant, which thus took over the function of defining the boundaries of (and divisions within) semantic units. As a result the distinction between pausal and contextual vowelling lost its function, becoming simply one of a number of types of such functionless variations preserved in the reading tradition. 7.1. The assumption that the pausal forms of the Bible (or some of them) reflect a method of reading customary at some time before that of the Masoretes has no doubt been made by many others,1 and certainly the phenomenon of pause in Arabic would support it. It is, however, of value to have such assump­ tions confirmed by a detailed investigation, and this investigation has the merit of revealing the high probability of direct connec­ tion between the method of reading reflected by the pausal forms and the patterns of speech or oratory of the biblical period. In three anomalous cases (compared to 387 with pausal forms), the first stich ends in a contextual form (xxiv. 4, 30, xxx. 21). Presumably, then, the pausal forms reflect a reading tradition which recognised a variety of literary forms in this section, not a poem composed throughout in stichs of more or less equal length.
