THE RELATION OF THE PALESTINIAN TO THE TIBERIAN MASSORA

E. J. Revell, Victoria College, Toronto

The term "Palestinian" here refers to texts with the niqqud ʾereṣ yiśrael. It may seem surprising to talk of "Massora" in connection with these texts, as they have been labelled "Pre- Massoretic". Indeed, few of these texts have any marginal no- tations similar to those of the marginal massora familiar in Tiberian texts. Such notations as there are nearly all refer to qere/ketiv situations. Those which do not are probably not original. These few notes which do not refer to qere/ketiv can also be found in Tiberian MSS, as can the only separate Massoretic compilation which can be identified as Palestinian. (The fragment of Okla we'Okla published by Weil, Annual of the Leeds University Oriental Society III, 1961-2, p.68-80). As for the qere/ketiv situations, they are nearly all the same as those given in BHK. The main difference between Tiberian and Palestinian MSS in this area is that Palestinian MSS have a strong tendency to use the qere form in the text. If the term "Massora" refers to these annotations, then, we must agree that there is, as far as is known, no independent "Palestinian" Massora. What we have appears to be a partial and inexact use of the same body of material used by the Tiberian scholars.

The term "Massora" can, however, be used with a wider meaning, to include all features of the writing of the Biblical Text: the layout of the page, the wording of the text, the use of petuhot and setumot, the form of the vowel and accent signs, and the system of using them. Palestinian usage in some of these features has been compared with Tiberian. Dr. Dietrich has concluded that the Palestinian fragments which he knew rep- resent "vulgar texts" (Neue palästinisch punktierte Bibelfragmente, Leiden, 1968, p.93). That is, they come from MSS in which the wording or spelling of the Biblical text was 87

[Page 88]

not preserved as carefully as it was in the best Tiberian examples. The Palestinian vocalization has been studied by a number of scholars, leading to the general opinion that the pronunciation represented reflects a stage of the language earlier than that represented by the Tiberian vocalization, but which is -- in most of the fragments if not in all - in a process of assimilation to the Tiberian tradition. My own view is that the Palestinian pointing represents a reading tradition which was originally similar to, or the same as, the Tiberian, but which has undergone further developments under the impact of normal linguistic change. In other words the reading tradition, like the textual tradition, was not well preserved. Whichever view of its history is correct, it is clear that the Palestinian vocalization represents a pronuncia- tion not greatly different from the Tiberian.

Several scholars have discussed the Palestinian accents, arriv- ing at the general opinion that the Palestinian accent system is more primitive than the Tiberian. In my opinion, these treatments have failed to grasp a basic principle of the Palestinian accentuation -- indeed of the Palestinian pointing as a whole. This might be called the principle of economy of sign form. A definite effort was made to keep the number of different sign forms used in these texts to a minimum. This is particularly well illustrated by the two fragments TS 12:197 and TS NS 246:22 (Published by P. Kahle, MdW II, and A. Díez Macho, Studia Papyrologica 6, 1967, p.15-25). These MSS use a sign wherever the Tiberian text uses a disjunctive or conjunc- tive accent. They even use three separate signs for the three shofar accents which the Tiberian system represents only by munaḥ. Despite the obvious sophistication of these systems, however, only 15 different accent signs are used altogether.

[Page 89]

Of these, only eight different signs represent the disjunctive accents, as against thirteen in the basic Tiberian system of disjunctives, and nine in the majority of Palestinian texts, including those thought to be the most ancient, and, because they have no signs for conjunctives, the most primitive. It is clear, then, that in the two MSS described, there was a definite effort to keep the number of different sign forms used to a minimum, and this is characteristic of the Palestinian tradition as a whole.

The following are the common patterns in which two or more accents are marked by one sign in the Palestinian texts.

  • 1. (a) .– the tifḥa sign represents tifḥa, atnaḥ, segolta (& silluq).
  • (b) ^ the atnaḥ sign represents atnaḥ and segolta.
  • 2. / the legarmeh sign represents legarmeh and zarqa.

The majority of texts show both pattern 1 -- either (a) or (b) - and pattern 2. Other patterns are found -- in one or two MSS each -- which need not be detailed here. In them, as in the patterns listed, a sign which represents one particular accent is used to mark one or two others as well. Such signs can be identified with a particular accent because in all MSS in which such a sign is used, it is used to represent that particular accent. It may represent others as well, but in some of these MSS, these other accents are represented by other signs. (Thus MSS using the inverted "v" for atnaḥ as in pattern 1(b) still use the subscript dot of pattern 1(a) to mark tifḥa.) This tendency to use one sign for several accents is not necessarily a mark of primitiveness. As we have seen, the smallest number of different signs for disjunctive accents is, in fact, used in the MSS differentiating the greatest number of accents.

[Page 90]

This use of a single sign to mark two accents, is, then, not an indication that the accents were considered to be identical, but is merely a graphic device, similar to the marking of śin and shin by the same letter of the alphabet, and, like that, probably due to failure to develop the writing system.

This proposition is further supported by a consideration of the accents which are not marked by their own sign, but by the sign for some other accent. There are about ten different systems of accent signs used in the twenty-five Palestinian Biblical MSS. The signs are listed in such a way as to make the next point clear. Most accents are represented in Palestinian texts both by a 'dot' form sign and by a 'stroke' form sign, although both lists are incomplete. These 'dot' and 'stroke' signs appear in the MSS mixed in different proportions. No MS uses all 'dot' or all 'stroke' forms, although some come very close.

The Tiberian accent signs actually show a similar mixture, and, when regarded from this viewpoint, they show an astonishing similarity to the Palestinian forms. This similarity appears to extend beyond the actual form, to some phenomenon underlying the sign for each accent whether in dot or stroke form, and whether from Palestinian or Tiberian MSS. Thus, the signs for zaqef and revia are always contrasted as high and low. In the Tiberian system, a second dot is placed under the 'high' dot form zaqef sign to emphasize this difference. Geresh is indicated by a dot at the right hand end of the word, or by a stroke leaning right, (and sometimes also prepositive) indicating interest in the beginning of the word. Pashṭa and Tevir are indicated by the same sign placed above or below the word - I think I can show that the dot is not original in the Tiberian sign for tevir. Enough has been said to indicate the type of relationship which occurs. It is more difficult to extend this to other dot and stroke forms, because of the uncertainty of the dot form signs, although some suggestions could be made. The relationship between the Tiberian and Palestinian stroke form signs can, however, be further emphasized. The munaḥ in Tiberian legarmeh is, as I think I can show, not original, so the sign was originally the same in both traditions. The Palestinian stroke forms for zarqa, pazer, both telishas, and also atnaḥ are variations on a 'v' shape. Probably this indicates a certain relationship between pazer and telisha - and the fact

that separate signs for zarqa and atnaḥ were a late development. The Tiberian signs for the same accents betray the same origin. The Tiberian sign for telisha qetanṇa contrasts with that for telisha gedola in a different way from the Palestinian. Com- pare, however the specialized conjunctive yeraḥ ben yomọ which is also called telisha qeṭanṇa (or telisha seghi̠ra), as opposed to 'telisha śemalit'. This evidence for relationship between the two traditions extends beyond the individual sign forms. The Tiberian signs for the accents of zaqef clause (except yetiv) are all written on or above the line, while those for silluq, atnaḥ, tifḥa and tevir are written below the line. The same is true for the Palestinian signs, whether in dot or stroke form. There can, I think, be no question that the Palestinian and Tiberian sign forms derive from the same origin.

It seems reasonable to suggest that the original from which the different forms of Palestinian and Tiberian signs for an accent derived was, in fact, the manual sign used to represent the accent in cheironomy. (These manual signs have, of course, often been suggested as the originals of the Tiberian signs.) It seems possible that a more or less uniform set of hand signs was used by those who taught the reading tradition. These would be passed on by those who taught Bible reading and chant. When these hand signs were converted into writing, however, a new set of people, the scribes, were involved in the develop- ment, and were not controlled by the bearers of the reading tradition. The result was that different representations of the same hand sign were produced in different areas. This idea would explain why it was possible to mix dot and stroke form signs. They both represented the same thing. The reason for the mixing was perhaps nothing more than the search for the most simple and easily recognizable set of signs. E.g., the

Other features generally show that the Palestinians tended to be careless about the preservation of their tradition. As pointed out, the consonantal text is full of minor variants, of which the rather common failure to write the ketiv form is an example. The marking of paragraphs shows a similar situa- tion. All MSS mark paragraphs, and divisions are usually made where the Standard Tiberian text marks one also. As regards the use of petuḥot and setumot; when Palestinian texts can be shown to use two types of paragraph division, which is not always the case, they rarely correspond closely to the Tiber- ian petuḥot and setumot, although in a few fragments they do correspond exactly.

It seems, in fact, that there was no definite Palestinian tra- dition in these matters. Both the Bodleian MS Heb. d29, f. 17-20 (published by Dietrich, op. cit., as MS Ob1) and the Jewish Theological Seminary MS ENA 1755, f. 3-7 (unpublished. I am most grateful to Dr. I. Yeivin for drawing this MS to my attention) cover Jos. 18:7-21:31. In 87 cases of textual variants from BHK in this material, the two MSS agree in 33 and differ in 54. In nine cases where they differ from BHK in the marking of paragraphs, they agree in only three. In the marking of qere/ketiv, where one or other differs from BHK, both agree in only two of six cases. In fact, in all these cases of differences from BHK, either MS agrees with BHK more commonly than with the other Palestinian MS. The only reasonable conclusion seems to me that these (and pre- sumably all other) Palestinian MSS do not carry a "Palestinian" tradition, but a variety of approximations to the standard Tiberian tradition.

[Page 96]

It seems to me correct to conclude from this information on the various facets of the Palestinian tradition that the basic difference between the Palestinian and Tiberian traditions is graphic. They are simply different methods of representing in writing what was basically the same reading tradition. The Babylonian pointing represents a different tradition, showing constant differences from the Tiberian in both vocalization and accentuation. There are no such constant differences between the Tiberian and Palestinian traditions. The differences which do exist can, it seems to me, be best explained in terms of two statements:–

  • 1). The Palestinians preserved the reading tradition which they inherited less well than did the Tiberians, so that various changes occurred, particularly in the vowel system.
  • 2). The development of the pointing system which the Palestinians invented to represent their reading tradition was inhibited by the principle of economy of sign form.

It is possible that this was not so much a consciously maintained principle as the result of an unimaginative conservatism, to which the idea of going beyond the restricted list of signs copied from the original hand-signs scarcely occurred. I personally favour this idea, as it suggests to me the attitude of provincials, isolated from the major scholarly centres. This situation -- a provincial area centred on no leading scholarly body -- would, it seems to me, explain several of the peculiarities of the Palestinian texts, such as their astonishing lack of uniformity, and their failure to preserve their reading and textual tradition unchanged. This, then, is how I would see the relationship of the Palestinian to the Tiberian Massora: They originated in the same stream of tradition, and

[Page 97]

came to differ mainly because the Palestinian tradition became isolated and lacked any leading scholarly body, so that its reading tradition changed faster than the Tiberian, while its pointing system developed more slowly.
