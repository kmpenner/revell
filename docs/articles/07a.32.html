First person imperfect forms with waw consecutive have recently received some attention in connection with the question of the authorship of the books of Ezra, Nehemiah, and Chronicles.1 A few details of these forms were overlooked in that discussion. It seems worth while to present these, in case similar interest in these forms is aroused in some other connection.2 Forms with pronominal suf­ fixes, which show no variations that might give clues to date or authorship, are not included in the following description.

(1) Where alef is the final root letter, the same 3 ms. form is regularly used with waw consecutive as without. This is also true for 1st person forms in 56 cases (including the qere in Josh, xxiv 8). The four exceptional forms, wj^esd^jh (Neh. ii 13), wjhqr^eh, (1 Sam. xxviii 15), and the ketib forms w^b^h (Josh, xxiv 8), and w^ws^h (Ezra viii 17), all appear to reflect the use of the affix -jh. This affixed form of the imperfect thus occurs in waw consecutive forms from roots with final alef much less frequently than in forms from any other roots except those with final he. This is to be expected, since this use of the affix is late (see below), and the differences between the two types of final weak root had been lost by the end of the biblical period.3

It seems more likely that the use of the affixed form with waw consecutive reflects the fact that this form was perceived as the equivalent, in the 1st person, of the short imperfect form in the 2nd and 3rd person.

The short imperfect form (unless preceded by the negative 7־) is characteristically used at the beginning of a clause, whether with waw consecutive, or without, as 2nd or 3rd person modal.9 The semantic contrast modal/indicative originally marked by the short and long imperfect forms was abandoned during the biblical period.10 The standard use of the contrasting forms was, however, continued, so that the short and long forms acted as allomorphs, the use of which was conditioned by the position of the verb in the clause. This is seen in Dan. xi, where imperfects appear in short form at the beginning of a clause whether jussive or indicative value is expected. Within a clause, long forms are used. The only excep­ tion is the qere form in Dan. xi 10. First person modals, the affixed imperfect forms, also characteristically begin their clauses. All examples in Daniel, Ezra, and Nehemiah, can be understood as having modal value, but it is clear that, in the late biblical period, the formal contrast between affixed and ordinary imperfect forms no longer distinguished modal from indicative value, and the same is true at Qumran (Qimron, p. 44). The use of affixed 1st person forms with waw consecutive represents, then, a syntactic or semantic development which was standardized in a post-exilic form of literary Hebrew. It is possible that some parts of the Bible were written in a Hebrew of this sort in which the standardization of this usage was either less complete than at Qumran, or has been obscured by later change. In the book of Nehemiah, however, the use of these forms distinguishes sections different from those of the usual literary division based on content and style. If the latter division is justified, the use of affixed 1st per­ son forms with waw consecutive shown in the MT must have arisen during the process of transmission. This conclusion must apply to the use of these forms throughout the Bible. They cannot be taken, in any book, as certainly representing the language of the author. First person forms in category (3) on which the affix -ah is not used show the following general pattern. Stress is penultimate where the penultimate syllable is open, but both antepenultimate and final syllables are closed.12 Stress is final where either antepenultimate or final syllable is open as well as the penultimate. It seems clear that the stress patterns of these forms reflect a natural phonological development in which stress position was con­ ditioned by syllable structure. The exception in Neh. iv 3 forms a negligible proportion even of 1st person forms. The vowel patterns suggest a correspondingly regular development. The vowel of a final closed syllable in these forms originally acted as short. In hollow root forms, and in forms from other roots, the vowel of this syllable changed in quality in all forms but those in which both penultimate and antepenultimate syllable were open (with the few exceptions noted). Where the vowel of this final closed syllable was stressed u or i, it was, in later times, often marked with a vowel letter, but there is no way of knowing to what extent the use of these letters in any part of the MT represents that of the work in its original form.

The use, in category (3), of long first person singular forms with waw consecutive is thus due to a phonological development quite distinct from the syntactic/semantic development which led to the use of affixed first person forms, both singular and plural, in categories (1), (3), and (4), and most probably also the use of long first person forms, also both singular and plural, in category (2). Both developments appear to have occurred towards the end of the biblical period, but there is no adequate basis for dating them either absolutely or relative to each other. The lack of relationship between the two developments is made clear (apart from the fact that both occur in category (3)) by the occurrence of two excep­ tional long 1st person forms with waw consecutive in Neh. iv 3 and iv 9, in a passage characterized by the failure to use affixed forms. Less striking confirmation is provided by the regular use of vowel letters in the final closed syllable of forms from category (3) in 1 Samuel (x 18, xii 1, xxviii 21) where an affixed form occurs in only one of twelve possible cases (or 4/15 if long III/z forms are included). This survey has, then, the negative merit of stressing the dangers of using 1st person forms with waw consecutive as criteria of authorship. The use of affixed forms cannot be attributed with any confidence to any biblical author. The use of long 1 s. forms from hollow roots (and others in category (3)) was characteristic of later biblical Hebrew generally.
