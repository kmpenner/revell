Ernest John Revell (1934–2017): Life and Work

Ernest John Revell, known as John, taught Hebrew at the University of Toronto. He worked on the Hebrew of the Dead Sea Scrolls, on the Palestinian vocalization found in manuscripts from the Cairo Genizah, and on the Masoretic accents and pausal forms. Later in his career he turned to the syntax and narrative style of Biblical Hebrew prose.1 He was also a botanical watercolour artist.2

Most of his articles are transcribed on this site, and the notes below link to them.

Family and early life

John Revell was born on 15 April 1934 in Bangalore (Bengaluru), India.1,2

His mother, Edith Mary Peckham Sheppard, was born in Toronto on 3 August 1900. She articled with Leighton McCarthy and was called to the Ontario bar with honours in January 1925, the 43rd woman lawyer in the province. She stayed on at McCarthy and McCarthy, and the Dictionary of Canadian Biography counts her among the first women to practise in a prominent corporate-law firm in a major Canadian city.

She resigned in the spring of 1929. On 14 June that year, in Bombay, she married Captain Alfred John Revell of the 1st Madras Pioneers, and the couple settled in Bangalore. They had two children, a daughter, Elizabeth, and a son, John, and both later had academic careers in Canada. Edith died in Bangalore on 16 November 1934, after an appendectomy, when John was seven months old.3

His father, Major Alfred John Revell (1893–1945), served with the 1st Battalion, 1st Madras Pioneers (King George's Own) from 1917 to 1934. From 1929 to 1932 he was Adjutant and Quartermaster at Kitchener College, an officer-training establishment at Nowgong. In 1934–35 he served with the 1st Kumaon Rifles.4 He died in 1945, when John was eleven, and is buried in England, in the cemetery close to John's childhood home.2

His mother's family, the Sheppards, were from Ontario. Her grandfather had built the family's fortune in the Georgian Bay Lumber Company, and she herself had studied at Havergal College and the University of Toronto.3

John's own obituary places his childhood home in England.2 His sister's obituary says more about her own path. Elizabeth was born in India on 31 July 1931, spent her childhood in England, and moved to Canada in her teens. In Toronto she attended Bishop Strachan School and then Trinity College at the University of Toronto, before going on to Oxford.5 John, less than three years younger, probably made the same move, but the sources used here do not say when he came to Canada or where he went to school.

Education and appointment at Toronto

John followed his mother to the University of Toronto, where he took a BA in Oriental Languages in 1956. He then read Oriental Languages at Cambridge, taking a second BA in 1958 and an MA in 1962. Back in Toronto, he was a teaching fellow at Trinity College in 1959–60 and a part-time instructor there in 1960–61, while working on his doctorate. In 1962 he joined the Department of Near Eastern Studies, received tenure in 1964, and became a full professor in 1974. He was a Canadian citizen.6

Doctorate and the Hebrew of Qumran

Revell took his doctorate at the University of Toronto in 1962. His thesis, "A Structural Analysis of the Grammar of 1 Q S", was directed by Professor J. W. Wevers.7 1QS, the Community Rule (also called the Manual of Discipline), is one of the scrolls found in the caves near Qumran from 1947 on.

His first articles came out of this work, and both appeared in Revue de Qumran. The first (1962) studies the order of the elements in the verbal clause of 1QS.7 The second (1964) examines clause structure in the prose documents of Qumran Cave I.8

The Canada Council supported his research in these years. Its first annual report (1958) lists a $400 travel grant for study at the Hebrew University in Jerusalem.9 Its 1963 report lists a $550 travel grant to attend the International Congress of Old Testament scholars in Bonn.10 In the preface to his 1970 book he thanks the Council for further travel grants and for a fellowship in 1967–68 that gave him a year in England. He also thanks the University of Toronto President's Fund for a trip to Cambridge in 1964.11

Palestinian vocalization

The Hebrew Bible was first written in consonants only. Between about the seventh and tenth centuries, scholars known as Masoretes added signs for vowels and accents. The system developed at Tiberias, associated with the ben Asher family, became the standard one; it writes most vowel signs below the letters. It was not the only system. Among the manuscripts recovered from the Genizah of the Ben Ezra Synagogue in Cairo at the end of the nineteenth century are texts in the Palestinian system, which writes its vowel signs above the letters.12

Revell's interest in these texts began with the books of Paul Kahle, whom he later met in Oxford. The work started as a joint study with Manfried Dietrich of the non-biblical texts in the Taylor-Schechter New Series at Cambridge; Kahle introduced the two of them. Dietrich later withdrew, and Revell went on to find about thirty new texts at Cambridge.11

The result was Hebrew Texts with Palestinian Vocalization (University of Toronto Press, 1970). It publishes thirteen texts, but Revell states that the main purpose of the book is to analyse and classify the Palestinian pointing, not to study and publish texts.

He faults earlier studies for classifying the Palestinian texts only by how closely they resemble the ben Asher pointing. The older view, that departures from the Tiberian norm were errors or ignorance, had rightly been rejected. But in his view the reaction against it had produced a new dichotomy between "true Palestinian" and "Tiberianized" texts. Revell instead describes some dozen non-biblical texts that show eleven types of vocalization. He explains these types as reflecting two major dialect groups of Palestinian Hebrew.11

A 1969 article in Textus applies the same approach to a single biblical text. TS NS 172:11 is a palimpsest fragment of 1 Chronicles from the Henriques Collection at Cambridge, found by N. Allony. Revell classifies its conjunctive accent signs by their Tiberian equivalents:13

Sign Tiberian equivalents Revell's description
(i) munah, mehuppak, merka, darga, maqqef The most common sign.
(ii) merka, darga Used before tifha or tebir.
(iii) — Probably signs (i) and (ii) on the same word, one an alternative to or a correction of the other.
(iv) azla A combination of a Palestinian and a Tiberian sign.
(v) telisha qetana

He concludes that the Palestinian tradition had two main streams.13 His 1977 monograph, Biblical Texts with Palestinian Pointing and Their Accents (Masoretic Studies 4), turned to the accents of the biblical texts with Palestinian pointing.14

Abbreviated ("shorthand") manuscripts

Some Genizah manuscripts, sometimes called serugin, write only part of each verse, with its vowel and accent signs. Christian David Ginsburg took them to be aids for students memorizing the text.15

In a 1974 paper Revell argued that "the basic purpose of this text is to represent the accent system". He rejected the view that such a manuscript was an aide-mémoire "designed to stimulate the recollection of one who had the text by heart", adding: "The system of abbreviation is much too complex for this." He concluded that the "shorthand" manuscript was "a quite widely used method of presenting the accent system."16

Kim Phillips, in a 2022 survey of these manuscripts, agrees with Revell that they were Masoretic tools. But he finds Revell's account of their function, as representing only the accents, "far too narrow".15

Translating Yeivin and editing for the IOMS

In 1980 Revell published his English translation of Israel Yeivin's survey of the Masorah, written in Hebrew, as Introduction to the Tiberian Masorah (Masoretic Studies 5); he also edited the volume. The book covers the Masoretic notes, the vowel signs and the accents of the Tiberian tradition. Current work on the Masorah still cites the English edition.17,18

For the International Organization for Masoretic Studies he edited Masoretic Studies 6 (1990) and 8 (1996), the second being the proceedings of the Twelfth Congress.19

The Leningrad Codex

The Leningrad Codex (Firkovich B19a), in the National Library of Russia, is the oldest complete Masoretic Bible. The scribe Samuel ben Jacob wrote and pointed it in Fustat, Egypt, early in the eleventh century. Biblia Hebraica (BHK), Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia (BHS) and Biblia Hebraica Quinta (BHQ) all use it as their base text.20

The 1998 facsimile edition, edited by Astrid B. Beck, David Noel Freedman and James A. Sanders, includes Revell's essay "The Leningrad Codex as a Representative of the Masoretic Text".21 (This site's page for that essay currently holds the wrong document; a corrected transcription is in progress.)

The 1996 volume of IOMS proceedings that Revell edited includes Harold P. Scanlin's study "Erased Ga'ayot in the Leningrad Codex", on secondary-stress marks that were erased from the manuscript.22

Pausal forms and stress

In Biblical Hebrew, a word at a major break in the verse, typically under the accents silluq or atnah, often takes a distinct pausal form, with a lengthened vowel or a different position of stress. Revell's 1981 article on the reflexes of original short a in Tiberian pointing relates one set of these vowel alternations to syntactic and semantic structure.23

In "Pausal Forms in Biblical Hebrew: Their Function, Origin and Significance" (1980), he argued that pausal forms in prose "reflect a system of text division which is independent of, and somewhat different from, that reflected by the accents". The Masoretic Text, on this view, contains two systems of text division. He thought the accent system had probably superseded the pausal system, which by then was already fixed in pronunciation. There was no conclusive evidence as to which system was earlier, but the pausal system had ancient roots.24

He extended the question to biblical poetry in a 1981 article in Vetus Testamentum,25 and in 1986 he examined pausal forms in texts with Palestinian pointing.26 A book-length treatment, The Pausal System: Divisions in the Hebrew Biblical Text as Marked by Voweling and Stress Position, edited by Raymond de Hoop and Paul Sanders, appeared in 2015.27

Related work on stress includes "Stress and the Waw 'Consecutive' in Biblical Hebrew" (1984) and the monograph Nesiga (Retraction of Word Stress) in Tiberian Hebrew (1987).28

Syntax and narrative

From the mid-1980s Revell's articles turned increasingly to the syntax and style of Biblical Hebrew prose.29 They treat:

  • narrative technique in the account of the battle with Benjamin in Judges 20 (1985);
  • the system of the verb in standard biblical prose (1989);
  • word order in verbless clauses (1989, 1999);
  • conditional particles (1991);
  • concord with compound subjects (1993);
  • the pronouns ʾny and ʾnky (1995);
  • the repetition of introductions to speech (1997);
  • the terms Midian and Ishmael in the Joseph story (2001).

His 1996 monograph, The Designation of the Individual: Expressive Usage in Biblical Narrative, studies the ways biblical narrative designates individual characters.30 He also wrote on the interpretative significance of the Masoretic punctuation for Hebrew Bible/Old Testament: The History of Its Interpretation, a project directed by Magne Sæbø.31

Botanical watercolours

Revell painted the seventy watercolours in "And Some Brought Flowers": Plants in a New World, by Mary Alice Downie and Mary Hamilton (University of Toronto Press, 1980). The book presents plants that early travellers found in North America, from ash to wintergreen, among them bittersweet, wappatoo, bedstraw, camass, fiddleheads and dandelion. Each painting is accompanied by quotations from explorers and settlers, and the plants are arranged alphabetically in the manner of the old herbals.32

His obituary describes him as a botanical watercolour artist and an avid gardener.2

Career and later life

Revell was a professor in the Department of Near Eastern Studies at the University of Toronto, later the Department of Near and Middle Eastern Civilizations. He served as its chair and became professor emeritus.2 He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada in 1986. He held fellowships at the Institute for Advanced Studies of the Hebrew University in Jerusalem in 1983–84, and at the Institute for Advanced Studies at Edinburgh University from October 1986 to March 1987.6 A history of the Canadian Society of Biblical Studies names him as a member of the Society, and as the only Canadian who had then contributed to Revue de Qumran.33

He was married for 52 years to Ann Margaret (née Morgan), who died before him. After retiring he moved to England, where he was a bell ringer for his local church; he also enjoyed long walks. He went on publishing as recently as 2016.2

He died in Antigonish, Nova Scotia, on 15 December 2017, aged 83. He was survived by his children, John and Bridget, his son-in-law Stephen Marmura, his grandchildren Hana and Allison Marmura, and his sister Elizabeth Revell. The funeral was held at St. Paul's Anglican Church in Antigonish on 20 December. His ashes were taken to England, to be buried near his father's grave.2

The Revell Digital Corpus

In 2026 a proposal in support of the objectives of the Gatto Chair of Christian Studies at St. Francis Xavier University set out to digitize Revell's articles: about 53 of them, some 850 pages. The proposal notes that standard OCR "reduces this linguistic data to unsearchable gibberish", because of the Hebrew vowel and accent signs.

The plan calls for 400 dpi scans, AI-assisted OCR, and manual proofreading of the Hebrew by a student research assistant. The texts are encoded in TEI XML and published on GitHub Pages.34 This site is the result.
